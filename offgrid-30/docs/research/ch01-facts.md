## 2. Facts by page

### P1 — Why water comes first

- Most people die within 3 to 7 days without water. → "Without consuming water, most people would die in three to seven days." → [`mlp-water`]
- (Supporting, less exact.) → "Without water, humans can survive only for days." → [`popkin2010`]
- Water is more than two-thirds of your body weight. → "Water makes up more than two-thirds of the weight of the human body." → [`mlp-water`]
- Babies have more body water than older people (about 75% down to 55%). → "Water comprises from 75% body weight in infants to 55% in elderly." → [`popkin2010`]
- Every cell and organ needs water. → "All the cells and organs need water to function." → [`mlp-water`]
- Water keeps your temperature normal. → "Keep a normal temperature." → [`cdc-drinks`]
- Water cushions joints. → "Lubricate and cushion joints." → [`cdc-drinks`]
- Water protects the spinal cord. → "Protect your spinal cord and other sensitive tissues." → [`cdc-drinks`]
- Water carries waste out of the body. → "Get rid of waste through urination, perspiration, and bowel movements." → [`cdc-drinks`]
- Water makes saliva and joint fluid; sweat cools you. → "It makes up saliva and the fluids surrounding the joints." / "Water regulates the body temperature through perspiration." → [`mlp-water`]
- Too little water harms thinking and mood. → "Drinking water can prevent dehydration, which may cause unclear thinking, mood change, overheating" (page also lists constipation and kidney stones). → [`cdc-drinks`]
- Severe dehydration can kill. → "Severe dehydration is a life-threatening emergency." → [`mlp-dehyd-ency`]
- Water is a top priority in an emergency. → "Having an ample supply of clean water is a top priority in an emergency." → [`rc-foodwater`]
- Water is scarce after disasters. → "Water quickly becomes a precious resource following many disasters." → [`fema-ayr`]
- You can cut food, but not water. → "Food, unlike water, may be rationed safely, except for children and pregnant women." → [`rc-foodwater`]
- WHO's emergency priority: enough water first, even before perfect safety. → "The first priority is to provide an adequate quantity of water, even if its safety cannot be guaranteed" → [`who-hum`]
- WHO's three emergency priorities. → "The three top priorities are the provision of sufficient quantities of safe water, basic sanitation arrangements and promotion of good hygiene behaviours." → [`who-hum`]
- **Most recent WHO death figure (all water, sanitation, hygiene):** 1.4 million deaths a year. → "1.4 million people die each year as a result of inadequate drinking-water, sanitation and hygiene." → [`who-san`, dated 5 Nov 2026]
- Of those, unsafe sanitation causes 564,000. → "Unsafe sanitation accounts for 564 000 of these deaths, largely from diarrhoeal disease" → [`who-san`]
- Dirty drinking water alone: about 505,000 diarrhoea deaths a year. → "is estimated to cause approximately 505 000 diarrhoeal deaths each year." → [`who-dw`, 2023]
- Diseases spread by dirty water. → "Microbiologically contaminated drinking water can transmit diseases such as diarrhoea, cholera, dysentery, typhoid and polio" → [`who-dw`]
- In 2022, at least 1.7 billion people drank from a source with feces in it. → "In 2022, globally, at least 1.7 billion people use a drinking water source contaminated with faeces." → [`who-dw`]
- Young children are most at risk. → "About 90 per cent of the deaths due to diarrhoea occur in children under 5." → [`who-hum`, 2005 text]

### P2 — How much water each person needs a day

**Emergency storage (US)**
- Store at least 1 gallon (3.8 L [conv]) per person per day. → "Store at least one gallon of water per person per day for several days, for drinking and sanitation." → [`ready-water`]
- Kit list wording. → "Water (one gallon per person per day for several days, for drinking and sanitation)" → [`ready-kit`]
- CDC: at least 3 days. → "Store at least 1 gallon of water per person, per day for 3 days." → [`cdc-store`]
- CDC: aim for 2 weeks. → "Try to store a 2-week supply if possible." → [`cdc-store`]
- Red Cross: 3 days to evacuate, 2 weeks at home. → "Water: one gallon per person, per day (3-day supply for evacuation, 2-week supply for home)" → [`rc-kit`]
- The gallon covers drinking **and** cooking/hygiene. → "You will also need water for food preparation and hygiene. Store at least one gallon per person, per day." → [`rc-foodwater`]

**Drinking only**
- Ready.gov (newest): about ¾ gallon (2.8 L [conv]) of fluid a day for a normally active person. → "A normally active person needs about three quarters of a gallon of fluid daily, from water and other beverages." → [`ready-water`, 2021]
- Older FEMA/Red Cross: at least ½ gallon (2 quarts; 1.9 L [conv]). → "A normally active person needs to drink at least two quarts (half gallon) of water each day." → [`rc-foodwater`, 2009]; same figure in [`fema-ayr`, 2004]: "A normally active person needs at least one-half gallon of water daily just for drinking." (See **Conflicts**.)
- Needs vary. → "However, individual needs vary depending on age, health, physical condition, activity, diet and climate." → [`ready-water`]

**WHO emergency minimums** (WHO Q&A, text dated 2005)
- 7.5 L (2 gal [conv]) per person per day meets most people's needs (drinking + food). Basis: breastfeeding women, moderate activity, warm weather. → "a minimum of 7.5 litres per capita per day will meet the requirements of most people under most conditions." → [`who-hum`]
- In an emergency, at least 15 L (4 gal [conv]). → "However, in an emergency situation, a minimum of 15 litres is required." → [`who-hum`]
- About 20 L (5.3 gal [conv]) for basic hygiene. → "A higher quantity of about 20 litres per capita per day should be assured to take care of basic hygiene needs and basic food hygiene." → [`who-hum`]
- Right after a disaster, treated water may be cut to 7.5 L. → "it may be necessary to limit treated water to a minimum of 7.5 litres per day per person." → [`who-hum`]

**National Academies (IOM, 2004–2005) Adequate Intake, total water (food + drinks)**
(I could not open the National Academies report itself: the site is blocked here. Figures below come from Tier 1/3 pages that cite it.)
- Men 3.7 L (125 oz; 0.98 gal [conv]); women 2.7 L (91 oz; 0.71 gal [conv]). → "3.7 liters (125 ounces) for men and 2.7 liters (91 ounces) for women." → [`cdc-nchs242`]

- Pregnancy 3.0 L/day (101 oz [conv]); breastfeeding given as 3.3 L/day — **secondary citation only; see Conflicts and Not verified.** → "The Institute of Medicine (IOM) established TWI at 3.0 L/day for pregnant women and 3.3 L/day for breastfeeding women" → [`bmcpreg2019`]
- Share from food: about 20%. → "add an additional roughly 20% of water intake, which is derived from some foods in addition to water and beverages." → [`popkin2010`]; also "In the United States it is estimated that about 22% of water comes from our food intake" → [`popkin2010`]
- Fruits and vegetables add water. → "Foods, especially those with high water content such as many fruits and vegetables, can also add to fluid intake." → [`cdc-drinks`]
- UK everyday guide: 6 to 8 cups or glasses a day. → "the government recommends that people should aim to drink 6 to 8 cups or glasses of fluid a day." → [`nhs-drinks`]

### P3 — Signs of dehydration at any age

**Adults and children (NHS list)** → [`nhs-dehyd`]
- "feeling thirsty"; "headache and feeling light-headed"; "dark yellow, strong-smelling pee"; "peeing less often than usual"; "feeling dizzy or lightheaded"; "feeling tired"; "a dry mouth, lips and tongue"; "sunken eyes".

**Mild to moderate vs severe (MedlinePlus)** → [`mlp-dehyd-ency`]
- Mild to moderate: "Thirst"; "Dry or sticky mouth"; "Not urinating much"; "Darker yellow urine"; "Dry, cool skin"; "Headache"; "Muscle cramps".
- Severe: "Not urinating, or very dark yellow or amber-colored urine"; "Dry, shriveled skin"; "Irritability or confusion"; "Dizziness or lightheadedness"; "Rapid heartbeat"; "Rapid breathing"; "Sunken eyes"; "Listlessness"; "Shock"; "Unconsciousness or delirium".

**Babies**
- Sunken soft spot. → "a sunken soft spot (fontanelle) on top of their head" → [`nhs-dehyd`]
- Few or no tears. → "few or no tears when they cry" → [`nhs-dehyd`]
- Fewer wet diapers. → "fewer wet nappies than usual" → [`nhs-dehyd`]
- No wet diaper for 3 hours or more (infants and young children). → "No wet diapers for 3 hours or more" → [`mlp-dehyd-topic`]
- Also: "Crying without tears"; "Being unusually sleepy or drowsy"; "Irritability"; "Eyes that look sunken"; "A high fever". → [`mlp-dehyd-topic`]
- Babies, children and older adults are at higher risk. → "Babies, children and older adults are more at risk of dehydration." → [`nhs-dehyd`]

**Older adults**
- Thirst fades with age. → "Older adults. Some people lose their sense of thirst as they age, so they don't drink enough fluids." → [`mlp-dehyd-topic`]
- → "As you get older, your thirst may change." → [`mlp-water`]
- → "older persons are less thirsty and drink less fluid compared to younger persons" → [`popkin2010`]

**Thirst is a late signal**
- → "Reliance on thirst alone is not sufficient to prevent mild dehydration" → [`cdc-yb-heat`]

**Urine colour**
- Aim for pale pee. → "Most people should aim to drink enough during the day so their pee is a clear pale yellow colour." → [`nhs-drinks`]
- → "You should drink enough during the day so that your pee is a pale colour." → [`nhs-dehyd`]
- Morning check is best. → "Using urine volume and color to monitor fluid needs is most accurate in the morning." → [`cdc-yb-heat`]

**Get help fast (red-flag box)**
- MedlinePlus: get help right away with "Confusion"; "Fainting"; "Lack of urination"; "Rapid heartbeat"; "Rapid breathing"; "Shock". → [`mlp-dehyd-topic`]
- Call 911 if "The person loses consciousness at any time." / "There is any other change in the person's alertness (for example, confusion or seizures)." / "The person has a fever over 102°F (38.8°C)." (as printed; exact conversion is 38.9°C [conv]) / "You notice symptoms of heatstroke (such as rapid pulse or rapid breathing)." → [`mlp-dehyd-ency`]
- NHS emergency (999) signs with dehydration: "blue, grey, pale or blotchy skin, lips or tongue"; "skin that feels cold"; "difficulty breathing, or is taking lots of quick breaths"; "are confused"; "are sleepier than normal or difficult to wake". → "These could be signs of shock, which is a serious complication of dehydration." → [`nhs-dehyd`]
- NHS urgent (same day) signs include: "your baby has a soft spot on their head that sinks inwards (sunken fontanelle)"; "you feel dizzy when you stand up and it does not go away". → [`nhs-dehyd`]
- Untreated severe dehydration can cause "Death", "Permanent brain damage", "Seizures". → [`mlp-dehyd-ency`]

### P4 — Who needs extra water

- Hot weather can double needs. → "In very hot temperatures, water needs can double." → [`ready-water`]; also "Very hot temperatures can double the amount of water needed." → [`fema-ayr`]
- Children, nursing mothers, sick people need more. → "Children, nursing mothers and sick people may need more water." → [`ready-water`]
- CDC storage add-ons. → "Consider storing more water than this for pregnant women, people who are sick, pets, or if you live in a hot climate." → [`cdc-store`]
- A medical emergency may need more. → "A medical emergency might require additional water." → [`fema-ayr`]
- CDC list: more water when "In hot climates." "More physically active." "Running a fever." "Having diarrhea or vomiting." → [`cdc-drinks`]
- NHS list: drink more if "pregnant or breastfeeding"; "in a hot environment"; "physically active for long periods"; "ill or recovering from illness". → [`nhs-drinks`]
- Fever, vomiting, diarrhea: drink early. → "Anyone with a fever, vomiting, or diarrhea should drink plenty of fluids. Do not wait for signs of dehydration." → [`mlp-dehyd-ency`]
- Watch kids and older adults when someone is sick. → "Pay close attention to children and older adults." → [`mlp-dehyd-ency`]
- Under 5s and older adults. → "Children under the age of 5 and older adults should get plenty of fluids as they have an increased risk of dehydration." → [`nhs-dehyd`]
- Heat risk groups: "older people, especially those aged 65 and over"; "babies and young children aged 5 years and under"; people who are pregnant; "people whose jobs involve manual labour". → [`nhs-heat`]
- Hard work in heat: 1 cup (237 mL [conv]) every 15–20 minutes, i.e. about 0.7–0.95 L (24–32 oz) per hour [conv]. → "During moderate activity in moderately hot conditions, workers should drink about 1 cup every 15 to 20 minutes." → [`cdc-niosh-heat`]
- Drink before you are thirsty when working in heat. → "drinking small amounts of water before becoming thirsty." → [`cdc-niosh-heat`]
- Sweat can reach 1 L (34 oz [conv]) an hour. → "sweat rates may reach 1 liter per hour, resulting in substantial fluid and sodium loss." → [`cdc-yb-heat`]
- Too much plain water can also harm (low blood salt). → "Excessive water ingestion is also a major contributor to exercise-associated hyponatremia" → [`cdc-yb-heat`]
- Breastfeeding/formula babies with dehydration: keep feeding. → "carry on breastfeeding your baby or using formula – try to give small amounts more often than usual" → [`nhs-dehyd`]
- Babies under 6 months: if giving extra water, boil and cool it first. → "(if your baby is under 6 months, then boil the water first before letting it cool)" → [`nhs-dehyd`]
- Babies 6–12 months may have 4–8 oz (118–237 mL [conv]) of water a day. → "Water (4 to 8 ounces a day)." → [`cdc-infant`]
- Caffeine and alcohol raise water needs. → "Caffeinated drinks and alcohol dehydrate the body which increases the need for drinking water." → [`ready-water`]
- Alcohol does not count as water. → "Alcohol does not add to the body's water because it is a diuretic." → [`mlp-water`]
- Heat stroke warning signs (for a "See also" chip; heat illness likely has its own chapter): "Extremely high body temperature (above 103 degrees F)" (39.4°C [conv]); "Red, hot and dry skin with no sweat"; "Rapid, strong pulse"; "Dizziness, confusion or unconsciousness". → [`ready-heat`]
- High altitude: CDC only says risk starts at about 2,450 m (8,000 ft) and lists dehydration as a look-alike of altitude sickness. → "Any unacclimatized traveler proceeding to a sleeping altitude of ≥2,450 m (≥8,000 ft)" → [`cdc-yb-alt`]. **No Tier 1–3 source found giving extra water amounts for altitude.**

### P5 — Water for pets and farm animals

**Official emergency guidance**
- Pets: keep a bowl and several days of water. → "Store a water bowl and several days’ supply of water." → [`ready-pets`]
- Kit item. → "Pet food and extra water for your pet" → [`ready-kit`]
- Pets, stay-at-home: 2 weeks. → "Stored water for two weeks" (Stay-at-Home Checklist for Pets and Service Animals) → [`fema-cert-animal`]
- Pets, evacuation: 3 days. → "Water for three days" → [`fema-cert-animal`]
- Livestock, stay-at-home: 1–2 weeks, more for heat. → "Water for 1-2 weeks (include considerations for extreme heat)" → [`fema-cert-animal`]
- Livestock: plan a way to run a well or melt snow. → "Camp stove and 2 or more gallons fuel for melting ice or snow for water or a generator and fuel to power a well" → [`fema-cert-animal`]
- Livestock evacuation sites need water. → "Ensure destinations have food, water, veterinary care and handling equipment." → [`ready-pets`]

**Daily amounts (peer-reviewed; no extension/USDA table could be reached — see Not verified)**
- Dogs: at least about 1 mL of water per calorie eaten (NRC 2006). → "conservatively estimated to be 1.0:1.0 mL water:kcal of ME" → [`zanghi2018`]
- Dogs on dry food drink about 63–73 mL/kg/day (about 1.0–1.1 fl oz per lb [conv]). → "consistent with previously reported mean of ~63–73 mL/kg/d for dogs drinking TW and fed dry food" → [`zanghi2018`]
- Cats: 50–60 mL/kg/day (about 0.8–0.9 fl oz per lb [conv]) per NRC. → "According to the NRC, cats should consume 50 to 60 mL/kg body weight (BW) of water" → [`kosmal2025`]
- Cats on wet food drink less from the bowl. → "In general, cats fed a wet diet have higher TWI but lower FWI compared to cats fed a dry diet" → [`kosmal2025`]
- Horses: about 30 L (7.9 gal [conv]) a day for a 500 kg (1,100 lb [conv]) adult at rest. → "approximately 30 L/day for an average adult nonbreeding, nonworking horse (500 kg body weight)" → [`freeman2021`]
- Horses: about 60 mL/kg/day (0.9 fl oz per lb [conv]). → "approximately 60 ml/kg/day (2 to 3 ml/kg/h)" → [`freeman2021`]
- Horses need more with work and heat. → "with higher volumes in response to activity and high ambient temperatures" → [`freeman2021`]
- Nursing mares: +37–74% (about 41–52 L / 11–14 gal a day [conv, from 30 L base]). → "Lactating mares can increase water consumption by 37 to 74% above maintenance needs to meet milk production" → [`freeman2021`]
- Beef cattle: about 21.65 L (5.7 gal [conv]) a day in mild weather. → "Under thermoneutral conditions (THI < 72), cattle drank 21.65 ± 0.02 L/d." → [`blake2026`]
- Beef cattle in severe heat drink about 50% more per body weight: 8.47 vs 5.53 L per 100 kg (about 1.0 vs 0.66 gal per 100 lb [conv]). → "animals in THI ≥ 90 consumed 8.47 ± 0.03 L/100 kg BW compared to 5.53 ± 0.01 in THI < 72." → [`blake2026`]
- Sheep: a 60 kg (132 lb [conv]) ewe drank about 2.18 L (0.58 gal [conv]) a day at 22°C (72°F [conv]). → "the average daily water intake of 40 empty Rasa Aragonesa ewes (60 kg of BW) was 2.18 l." → [`perez2023`]
- Sheep drink about twice as much in warm weather. → "ewes drank twice more water under warm (24.8 ºC) than cold (0.4 ºC) temperatures." (76.6°F vs 32.7°F [conv]) → [`perez2023`]
- Goats in heat: 7.4 L (2 gal [conv]); milking goats: 15.3 L (4 gal [conv]). → "Under heat stress, crossbreed goats drunk on average 7.4 l" / "this amount was higher, 15.3 l, in lactating Aardi goats" → [`perez2023`]
- Heat and milking raise needs. → "Therefore, the temperature and lactation status increase the water requirement." → [`perez2023`]
- Pigs (growing/finishing): about 7.1 L (1.9 gal [conv]) a day on average over fattening (Table 3, kg/pig/day); about 3.2 L per kg of feed (0.38 gal per lb [conv]). → "was 3.2 L/kg feed over the entire fattening period" → [`schale2023`]
- Pigs drink more when hot. → "When the air temperature increases, the water consumption of pigs also increases" → [`schale2023`]
- Chickens (adult meat birds): 150–200 mL (5–7 fl oz [conv]) a day, up to 300–350 mL (10–12 fl oz [conv]). → "An adult broiler chicken usually drinks about 150 to 200 ml per day, and it can reach up to 300–350 ml per day" → [`elsabry2023`]
- Rabbits: about 120 mL/kg/day (1.8 fl oz per lb [conv]). → "In general, daily water intake is approximately 120 ml per kilogram of body weight." → [`suckow-rabbit`]
- Rabbits on dry pellets drink more. → "rabbits will drink more water when consuming dry, pelleted feed" → [`suckow-rabbit`]

### P6 — Hidden water already in your home

**Safe sources**
- FEMA "Safe Sources" table: "Melted ice cubes"; "Water drained from the water heater (if the water heater has not been damaged)"; "Liquids from canned goods such as fruit or vegetable juices"; "Water drained from pipes". → [`fema-ayr`]
- Red Cross: "Safe water sources in your home include the water in your hot-water tank, pipes, and ice cubes." → [`rc-foodwater`]
- CDC list: "Water from your home's tap water heater tank." / "Melted ice cubes made with water that was not contaminated" / "Water from your home's toilet tank (not from the bowl), if it is clear and has not been chemically treated" (with toilet cleaners) / "Liquid from canned fruit and vegetables" → [`cdc-find`]
- Use the tap water heater, not a heating-system tank. → "(This is different than the tank used to supply hot water to radiators in older homes." → [`cdc-find`]

**Do NOT drink**
- FEMA "Unsafe Sources" table: "Radiators"; "Hot water boilers (home heating system)"; "Water beds (fungicides added to the water or chemicals in the vinyl may make water unsafe to use)"; "Water from the toilet bowl or flush tank"; "Swimming pools and spas (chemicals used to kill germs are too concentrated for safe drinking…)". → [`fema-ayr`]
- Red Cross: "You should not use water from toilet flush tanks or bowls, radiators, waterbeds, or swimming pools/spas." → [`rc-foodwater`]
- CDC: never use heating-system water at all. → "Never use water from radiators or boilers that are part of your home heating system for drinking, cooking, or hygiene." → [`cdc-safe`]
- Pools and spas: washing only. → "Water from swimming pools and spas can be used for personal hygiene, cleaning, and related uses, but not for drinking." → [`cdc-find`]
- Fuel or chemicals: no fix. → "Do not use water that has been contaminated by fuel or toxic chemicals." → [`cdc-find`]

**Treat or not?**
- CDC and FEMA call the home sources above "clean and safe" / "Safe Sources". CDC: "other sources of clean and safe water that can be found inside your home include" → [`cdc-find`]
- Outside water must be treated. → "Water from sources outside the home must be treated to kill harmful germs" → [`cdc-find`]
- Treat anything you're unsure of. → "Treat all water of uncertain quality before using it for drinking, food washing or preparation" → [`ready-water`]
- WHO: assume all water may be dirty in an emergency, even piped water. → "In an emergency it should be assumed that all water is at risk of contamination and this includes piped supplies." → [`who-hum`] (See **Conflicts**.)

**Getting water out of the pipes**
- → "To use the water in your pipes, let air into the plumbing by turning on the faucet in your home at the highest level. A small amount of water will trickle out. Then obtain water from the lowest faucet in the home." → [`fema-ayr`; same wording in `rc-foodwater`]

**Draining the water heater**
- Power or gas off first, then drain from the bottom. → "To use the water in your hot-water tank, be sure the electricity or gas is off, and open the drain at the bottom of the tank." → [`fema-ayr`; `rc-foodwater`]
- Close the cold intake, open a hot tap. → "Start the water flowing by turning off the water intake valve at the tank and turning on the hot-water faucet." → [`fema-ayr`]
- Refill before power/gas goes back on. → "Refill the tank before turning the gas or electricity back on." → [`fema-ayr`]
- Gas needs a pro to relight. → "If the gas is turned off, a professional will be needed to turn it back on." → [`fema-ayr`]; "If you turn off the gas for any reason, only a qualified professional can turn it back on." → [`ready-safety`]

### P7 — Shutting off your home's water safely

- Everyone at home should know how. → "It is important that everyone in your household learn how to shut off the main water valve to the house." → [`ready-safety`]
- Why: broken lines can let dirty water in. → "Cracked lines may pollute the water supply to your house." → [`ready-safety`]
- Keep it off until officials say OK. → "It's a good idea to shut off your water until authorities say it's safe to drink." → [`ready-safety`]
- CDC reason. → "You may need to shut off the main water valve to your home to prevent contaminants from entering pipes in your home." → [`cdc-find`]
- When: reports of broken water or sewer lines, or official advice. → "You will need to protect the water sources already in your home from contamination if you hear reports of broken water or sewage lines, or if local officials advise you of a problem." → [`fema-ayr`; `rc-foodwater`]
- Shutting the valve also keeps water in the heater and toilet tanks. → "The effects of gravity may drain the water in your hot water heater and toilet tanks unless you trap it in your house by shutting off the main house valve." → [`ready-safety`]
- Use the house valve, not the street valve. → "(not the street valve in the cement box at the curb—this valve is extremely difficult to turn and requires a special tool)." → [`fema-ayr`]
- Find it and tag it. → "Find the shut-off valve for the main water line that enters your house and tag it for easy identification." → [`ready-safety`]
- Test it now. → "Make sure this valve can be completely shut off. Your valve may be rusted open, or it may only partially close. Replace it if necessary." → [`fema-ayr`]
- How. → "To close the incoming water source, locate the incoming valve and turn it to the closed position." → [`fema-ayr`]
- Water heater: power/gas off before draining → see P6 [`fema-ayr`].
- **Frozen pipes (only the parts tied to valves/draining):**
  - Water expands when it freezes and can split pipes. → "No matter the strength of a container, expanding water can cause pipes to break." → [`rc-frozen`]
  - Outdoor taps: shut inside valve, drain, leave outside tap open. → "Close inside valves supplying outdoor hose bibs. Open the outside hose bibs to allow water to drain." → [`rc-frozen`]
  - → "Keep the outside valve open so that any water remaining in the pipe can expand without causing the pipe to break." → [`rc-frozen`]
  - A trickle helps stop freezing. → "Running water through the pipe - even at a trickle - helps prevent pipes from freezing." → [`rc-frozen`]
  - Away in cold weather: heat at 55°F (12.8°C [conv]) or more. → "leave the heat on in your home, set to a temperature no lower than 55° F." → [`rc-frozen`]
  - Thawing: no open flame. → "Do not use a blowtorch, kerosene or propane heater, charcoal stove, or other open flame device." → [`rc-frozen`]

### P8 — Your daily water budget planner

- 1 US gallon = 3.785 L; 1 quart = 0.946 L; 1 cup (8 fl oz) = 237 mL (standard US definitions; conversion facts, not fetched from a page).
- Planner base: 1 gallon (3.8 L [conv]) × people × days → [`ready-water`, `cdc-store`].
- Minimum days: 3. → [`cdc-store`]; "Three-day supply of water (one gallon of water per person, per day)" → [`rc-foodwater`]
- Goal: 2 weeks. → [`cdc-store`, `rc-kit`]; "Consider storing at least a two-week supply of water for each member of your family." → [`rc-foodwater`]
- If you can't store that much. → "If you are unable to store this quantity, store as much as you can." → [`rc-foodwater`]
- Add extra for heat, pregnancy, illness, pets → [`cdc-store`, `ready-water`].
- Worked example (my math, no source has one; CDC page checked: "example family calculation" ABSENT): 4 people × 14 days × 1 gal = 56 gal (212 L [conv]).
- Replace self-filled water every 6 months. → "If you are filling containers with water to store, replace the water every 6 months." → [`cdc-store`]; "Water that has not been commercially bottled should be replaced every six months." → [`ready-water`]
- Bottled water is safest; leave it sealed. → "Keep bottled water in its original container, and do not open it until you need to use it." → [`rc-foodwater`]
- Medicines: 7 days. (Context only.) → "Medications (7-day supply) and medical items" → [`rc-kit`]

### P9 — When water runs low: what to do

- Don't ration drinking water unless told to. → "Never ration drinking water unless ordered to do so by authorities." → [`ready-water`]
- Drink today's need; look for more tomorrow. → "Drink the amount you need today and try to find more for tomorrow." → [`ready-water`]
- Rest and stay cool. → "Minimize the amount of water your body needs by reducing activity and staying cool." → [`ready-water`]
- Never less than 1 quart (0.95 L [conv]; 4 cups) a day. → "Under no circumstances should a person drink less than one quart (four cups) of water each day." → [`fema-ayr`]
- Let people drink what they need. → "Allow people to drink according to their needs." → [`fema-ayr`]
- Drink known-safe water first. → "Drink water that you know is not contaminated first." → [`fema-ayr`]
- If you can't treat suspect water, delay it, but don't dehydrate. → "If water treatment is not possible, put off drinking suspicious water as long as possible, but do not become dehydrated." → [`fema-ayr`]
- Skip fizzy drinks as water. → "Carbonated beverages do not meet drinking-water requirements." → [`fema-ayr`]
- Skip caffeine and alcohol → [`ready-water`, quoted in P4].
- Skip salty food when water is short. → "If your water supply is limited, don't eat salty foods, since they will make you thirsty." → [`rc-foodwater`]
- Eat watery foods instead. → "Instead, eat salt-free crackers, whole grain cereals, and canned foods with high liquid content." → [`rc-foodwater`]
- Food (not water) can be cut — but not for kids or pregnant women → [`rc-foodwater`, quoted in P1].
- In heat: shade, no hard work in the hottest hours, no alcohol or caffeine. → "stay in the shade especially between 11am and 3pm" / "avoid exercise or activity that makes you hotter" / "Drink extra fluids but avoid alcohol, caffeine and hot drinks" → [`nhs-heat`]
- What to cut first (order of uses): **no Tier 1–3 source found** with a ranked list. WHO only notes untreated water can be used for laundry and bathing: "During emergencies, people may use an untreated water source for laundry, bathing, etc." → [`who-hum`]
