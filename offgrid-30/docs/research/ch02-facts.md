## 2. Facts by page

### P1 — Choosing safe containers

**Best choice: food-grade containers**
- Best is a food-grade water storage container. → "it is best to use FDA-approved food-grade water storage containers." → [`cdc-store`]
- Why: they don't add poisons to the water. → "FDA-approved food-grade storage containers will not transfer toxic substances into the water." → [`cdc-store`]
- Buy food-grade containers if you fill your own. → "If you must prepare your own containers of water, purchase food-grade water storage containers." → [`ready-water`]
- Where to buy (older FEMA text). → "purchase food-grade water storage containers from surplus or camping supplies stores" → [`fema-ayr`]
- "Food grade" in plain words. → "Containers should be "food grade," meaning they were meant to hold food or water." → [`usu-water`]
- Non-food plastic can leach chemicals. → "Non-food-grade plastic containers are not recommended for food storage because harmful chemicals can leach into the food." → [`usu-water`]
- Thin plastic can split; waterbed and pool-liner plastic is not food storage plastic. → "Very lightweight plastic might split or degrade under storage. Most plastics used in waterbeds or pool liners are not approved food storage plastics." → [`usu-water`]
- Army standard for non-standard containers: food grade or NSF 61. → "their water contact materials are nontoxic and preferably made of FDA food grade or NSF International Standard 61-listed material." → [`tbmed577`, para 16-6c]
- WHO: materials should be sturdy and drinking-water safe, ideally certified. → "All materials used for drinking-water collection and storage should be sturdy and safe for contact with drinking-water (e.g. PVC or ceramic), and should ideally be approved for drinking-water use via a certification scheme" → [`who-tfs-house`]

**What a good container looks like**
- Tight lid, tough material, not glass, narrow opening if possible (CDC list). → top that "can be closed tightly"; "durable, unbreakable materials (not glass)"; narrow neck or opening if possible → [`cdc-store`]
- Narrow neck stops hands and cups getting in. → "a narrow neck or opening so water can be poured out without hands or objects entering the container." → [`cdc-global-store`]
- One small opening, 5–8 cm (2–3 in [conv]). → "a single small (5–8 cm) opening" → [`cdc-global-store`]
- Standard size 20 L (5.3 gal [conv]), with a handle. → "hold a standard volume of water (20 liters)" and "a comfortable handle for easy carrying." → [`cdc-global-store`]
- Plastic, ceramic or metal. → "made of durable plastic, ceramic, or metal." → [`cdc-global-store`]
- Best containers have a lid and a spout or spigot. → "The best containers have secure lids and a spout or spigot that allows for dispensing water with minimal or no contamination." → [`usu-water`]
- Humanitarian standard: 10–20 L (2.6–5.3 gal [conv]) containers with lids; narrow neck or tap. → "Containers should have lids, be clean and covered. Storage containers should have a narrow neck or tap to ensure safe collection, storage and consumption of drinking water." → [`sphere`, Hygiene promotion std 1.2]
- Two containers per household: one to carry, one to store. → "two water containers per household (10–20 litres; one for collection, one for storage)" → [`sphere`]
- A tap lets you pour without dipping hands in. → "fit a tap to the storage container so that water can be poured directly into a cup or bowl" → [`who-tn05`]

**Plastic types (recycling codes)**
- Use food-grade buckets, carboys or drums marked PETE or HDPE. → "The best option is to use food-grade, heavy-duty plastic buckets, carboys or drums stamped with a PETE or HDPE recycle code." → [`osu-em9285`]
- PET/PETE is code #1, used for soda, juice and bottled water. → "listed as number 1 inside the recycling triangle diagram … commonly used for beverages such as soft drinks, juices and bottled water." → [`usu-plastics`]
- HDPE is code #2 (milk jugs, some juice and laundry bottles). → "High density polyethylene is the plastic commonly used for milk bottles … It is shown as number 2 on the triangle code." → [`usu-plastics`]
- Two-liter soda bottles are a good DIY choice. → "choose two-liter plastic soft drink bottles" → [`fema-ayr`]; "Two-liter pop containers are less likely to leak or hold residues." → [`usu-store`]
- Screw-top bottles made for food use. → "use screw-top containers manufactured for food use (such as plastic soda bottles, juice bottles or water jugs)." → [`osu-em9285`]

**Glass and metal**
- CDC: not glass. → "durable, unbreakable materials (not glass)" → [`cdc-store`]
- FEMA: no glass — it breaks and is heavy. → "do not use glass containers, because they can break and are heavy." → [`fema-ayr`]
- USU: glass seals out vapors but should not be your only storage. → "Glass should not be the sole source of water storage since it is easily broken and may be damaged during an emergency event." → [`usu-water`]
- Stainless steel works; other metals only if coated for food; avoid pewter and lead solder. → "Stainless steel can successfully be used for water storage. … Pewter or lead soldered metals should be avoided." → [`usu-water`]
- WHO: tankers should be stainless steel or other drinking-water-safe material. → "Water tanks should be made of stainless steel or other material suitable for the storage of drinking water." → [`who-tn12`]

**Never use**
- Containers that held poisons, such as bleach or pesticides. → "Do not use containers that were previously used to hold liquid or solid toxic chemicals, such as bleach or pesticides." → [`cdc-store`]; same warning in [`cdc-global-store`]
- Small traces stay behind. → "Never use a container that has held toxic substances, because small amounts may remain in the container." → [`csu-emerg`]
- Anything that held fuel, oil, pesticides, herbicides. → "Anything that has previously been used to store petroleum, oil and lubricant products, pesticides, herbicides, or other toxic materials, must not be used to carry potable water." → [`tbmed577`, para 6-2b]
- Milk and fruit-juice jugs and cardboard cartons: no. → "not plastic jugs or cardboard containers that have had milk or fruit juice in them. Milk protein and fruit sugars cannot be adequately removed from these containers and provide an environment for bacterial growth" → [`fema-ayr`]
- Cardboard leaks. → "Cardboard containers also leak easily and are not designed for long-term storage of liquids." → [`fema-ayr`]
- Milk jugs break down over time and are hard to clean. → "used milk jugs are not well suited as food storage containers. While the plastic is considered food grade, milk containers are difficult to sanitize properly, and the plastic will break down over time." → [`usu-plastics`]
- Oregon State gives the same advice. → "Do not use repurposed plastic milk jugs. … protein from the milk can cause the stored water to spoil." → [`osu-em9285`]

### P2 — Cleaning and sanitizing containers

**Household containers (CDC steps)**
1. Wash with soap, rinse well. → "Wash the storage container with soap and rinse completely with water." → [`cdc-store`]
2. Mix sanitizer: 1 tsp (5 mL [conv]) unscented bleach in 1 quart (0.95 L [conv]) water. → "Sanitize the container with a solution made by mixing 1 teaspoon of unscented liquid household chlorine bleach" (in one quart / four cups of water) → [`cdc-store`]
3. Bleach strength. → "Use bleach that contains 5%–9% sodium hypochlorite." → [`cdc-store`]
4. Close and shake so it touches every surface. → "Cover the container tightly and shake it well." → [`cdc-store`]
5. Wait at least 30 seconds, pour out. → "Wait at least 30 seconds and then pour the sanitizing solution out of the container." → [`cdc-store`]
6. Air-dry. → "Let the empty container air-dry before use." → [`cdc-store`]
- Metric version (CDC global page): 5 mL in 1 liter. → "5 mL (1 tsp) of unscented chlorine bleach in 1 liter of water" → [`cdc-global-store`]
- Ready.gov gives the same 1 tsp per quart. → "one teaspoon of non-scented liquid household chlorine bleach to a quart of water" → [`ready-water`]
- Use dish soap first. → "thoroughly clean the containers with dishwashing soap" → [`ready-water`]
- No soap left behind (FEMA). → "rinse completely so there is no residual soap." → [`fema-ayr`]
- Swish so it touches all surfaces (FEMA). → "Swish the sanitizing solution in the bottle so that it touches all surfaces." → [`fema-ayr`]
- Sanitize the cap too: turn the bottle upside down. → "Shake well, turning bottle upside down a time or two to sanitize the cap." → [`usu-water`]
- Bleach type: plain, unscented; no color-safe or added cleaners. → "Do not use scented bleaches, color safe bleaches or bleaches with added cleaners." → [`ready-water`]

**Large containers and tanks (Army method)**
- Soap solution: 1/3 cup (79 mL [conv]) liquid detergent in 10 gal (38 L [conv]) hot water; scrub with a long brush; flush spigots; rinse twice. → "Prepare a soap solution by adding 1/3 cup of liquid detergent to 10 gal of hot water." / "Rinse the container and spigots twice with potable water" → [`tbmed577`, para 7-1d(2)]
- Disinfect at 100 mg/L chlorine, keep all inside surfaces wet at least 60 minutes. → "Keep all interior surfaces wet with the solution for at least 60 min." → [`tbmed577`, para 7-1d(3)]
- Short on water? Swab walls with 5 gal (19 L [conv]) of 100 mg/L solution every 10 minutes for 1 hour. → "swab the interior walls of the tank every 10 min or as often as necessary to keep the walls wet with the solution for 1 hour." → [`tbmed577`, para 7-1d(4)]
- Gentler option: 10 mg/L for 24 hours. → "employ 10 mg/L chlorine residuals and a 24-hour contact time in lieu of the 100 mg/L and 1-hour contact time" → [`tbmed577`, para 7-1d(5)]
- Run solution through valves and spigots too. → "Run some of the solution through the valves and spigots." → [`tbmed577`]
- Metal tanks: never drop dry calcium hypochlorite granules in; dissolve them in a bucket first. → "Do not add calcium hypochlorite granules directly to the water in metal containers." → [`tbmed577`]
- Rust in stainless tanks: no grinders or sanders; use scouring powder and a nylon brush. → "DO NOT use a mechanical grinder or sanding device to remove rust." → [`tbmed577`]
- Mineral scale: cover with vinegar, wait 5 hours, flush. → "adding enough vinegar … to cover the mineral deposits, letting it sit for 5 hours" → [`tbmed577`]
- Don't dump the chlorine water into a lake, stream or storm drain. → "(not into a lake, stream, or storm drain)" → [`tbmed577`]
- Hydration bladders: scrub with soap and hot water; sanitize with 2 tsp (10 mL [conv]) bleach, 30 min; rinse; air-dry open. → "Sanitize the reservoir with water and 2 tsp of liquid bleach. Let it sit for 30 min." → [`tbmed577`, para 7-1b]

**WHO tank method (see P7 for cistern use)**
- Scrub inside with detergent and hot water (laundry soap powder works); clean corners and joints. → "Use a mixture of detergent and hot water (household laundry soap powder will do) to scrub and clean all internal surfaces of the tank." → [`who-tn03`]
- Quarter-fill, add 80 g (2.8 oz [conv]) granular high-strength calcium hypochlorite per 1,000 L (264 gal [conv]) of tank size, fill, close, wait 24 h. → "Sprinkle 80 grams of granular HSCH into the tank for every 1000 litres total capacity of the tank." → [`who-tn03`]
- In a hurry: double the chlorine, 8 hours. → "double the quantity of chlorine … This will reduce the time of disinfection from 24 to 8 hours." → [`who-tn03`]
- Then empty, fill with drinking water, stand 30 min, empty again. → "Fill the tank with drinking-water, allow to stand for about 30 minutes then empty the tank again." → [`who-tn03`]
- Clean hoses and pumps the same way. → "The hoses, pumps and pipes used for filling and emptying the tank must also be cleaned." → [`who-tn03`]

**How often**
- Clean regularly. → containers "should be cleaned on a regular basis." → [`cdc-global-store`]
- Tanker tanks: before use, after major repair, and at least every 3 months. → "Water tanks, and when applicable, pumps must be cleaned before they are used, after major maintenance and at least every three months." → [`who-tn12`]
- Every six months: empty, wash, refill (see P5).

### P3 — Filling and treating water for storage

**Tap water from a treated public supply**
- City (chlorinated) tap water needs nothing added. → "If the tap water has been commercially treated from a water utility with chlorine, you do not need to add anything else to the water to keep it clean." → [`fema-ayr`]
- Same advice from Oregon State. → "City water from municipal water sources is already treated to be safe, so no additional treatment is necessary." → [`osu-em9285`]
- Fill to the top (FEMA, soda bottles). → "Fill the bottle to the top with regular tap water." → [`fema-ayr`]
- Pour into the clean container, close with a tight lid. → "Pour clean water into the sanitized container and cover it with a tight lid." → [`cdc-store`]
- Don't touch the inside of the cap. → "Be careful not to contaminate the cap by touching the inside of it with your finger." → [`fema-ayr`]

**Well or spring water (not chlorinated)**
- It must be treated first: boil or add bleach. → "Water from a well or spring, known to be pathogen-free but not chemically treated, should be purified by boiling or adding unscented, household, liquid chlorine bleach." → [`osu-em9285`]
- FEMA dose (very low; see Conflicts C1). → "If the water you are using comes from a well or water source that is not treated with chlorine, add two drops of non-scented liquid household chlorine bleach to the water." (context: 2-liter soda bottles) → [`fema-ayr`]
- Oregon State SP 50-835 and Colorado State: 2 drops per gallon. → "Add two drops chlorine bleach per gallon. (Use 5.25% sodium hypochlorite …)" → [`osu-sp50835`]; "well water that has been treated by adding two drops of chlorine bleach per gallon." → [`csu-emerg`]
- Utah State: 1/8 tsp = 8 drops per gallon (3.8 L [conv]) for long-term storage. → "One gallon can be treated by the addition of 1/8 teaspoon of liquid chlorine bleach containing 4 to 6 percent sodium hypochlorite. … This is equivalent to 8 drops" → [`usu-water`]
- CDC standard dose for unsafe water (5–9% bleach): 8 drops = ½ mL per gallon; wait 30 min; store in clean, sanitized, covered containers. → "Add 8 drops of bleach" / "Add ½ mL of bleach" / "Let the water stand for at least 30 minutes before you drink it." / "Store the disinfected water in clean, sanitized containers with tight covers." → [`cdc-safe`]
- EPA dose per gallon: 8 drops of 6% or 6 drops of 8.25% bleach; double if cloudy or very cold. → "8 drops of 6% bleach, or 6 drops of 8.25% bleach, to each gallon of water. Double the amount of bleach if the water is cloudy, colored, or very cold." → [`epa-disinfect`]
- Larger containers (Oregon State table, 5–6% bleach implied): 5 gal (19 L) → 3/4 tsp (3.7 mL); 7 gal (26 L) → 1 tsp (4.9 mL); 15 gal (57 L) → 2 tsp (9.9 mL); 55 gal (208 L) → 2 tbsp (30 mL). [all metric conv] → "5 gallons water: 3/4 teaspoon bleach … 55 gallons water: 2 tablespoons bleach" → [`osu-em9285`]
- Use fresh bleach: it loses strength. → "Use fresh chlorine bleach since it can lose up to half its strength after 6 months." → [`usu-water`]; EPA: bleach "stored at room temperatures for less than one year." → [`epa-disinfect`]
- Bleach strength on labels today: 6% or 8.25% (EPA); "between 5% and 9%" (CDC). → [`epa-disinfect`], [`cdc-safe`]

**Labeling**
- Write "drinking water" and the date. → "Label container as "drinking water" and include storage date." → [`cdc-store`]
- FEMA: date on the outside. → "Place a date on the outside of the container so that you know when you filled it." → [`fema-ayr`]
- Army: mark non-drinking water clearly so no one mixes them up. → "All containers used to store or transport nonpotable water must be clearly and legibly labeled so they are not mistaken with containers holding potable water." → [`tbmed577`, para 16-5c(5)]

### P4 — Where to keep stored water

- Cool place: 50–70 °F (10–21 °C [conv]). → "Keep containers in a place with a cool temperature (50°F –70°F)." → [`cdc-store`]
- Out of direct sun. → "Keep containers away from direct sunlight." → [`cdc-store`]
- Away from gasoline and pesticides. → "Keep containers away from toxic substances, such as gasoline or pesticides." → [`cdc-store`]
- Why: plastic lets some vapors through. → "Since plastic is permeable to certain vapors, water stored in plastic should not be near gasoline, kerosene, pesticides or similar substances." → [`usu-water`]
- Odors from cleaning products and fertilizer can pass into the water. → "Store water away from cleaning supplies, fertilizer and any product with strong odors. These odors can be absorbed by the storage container and transferred to the water." → [`osu-em9285`]
- Cool, dark place (Ready.gov for bottled water; FEMA for self-filled). → "store it in the sealed original container in cool, dark place." → [`ready-water`]; "Store in a cool, dark place." → [`fema-ayr`]
- Keep it at a steady temperature. → "store the water in an environment that stays at a constant temperature and is out of direct sunlight." → [`osu-em9285`]
- Off the ground. → "Store water in a clean, dry place off the ground and away from sunlight." → [`usu-water`]
- In basements, use shelves (floodwater risk). → "When storing the water in a basement, keep the water supply on shelves and not on the ground. In the event of a flood, stormwater entering the basement could contaminate the stored water." → [`csu-emerg`]
- Light and warmth help germs grow; store bottled water in shade with airflow. → "Bottled and packaged field water should not be stored in direct sunlight because the light and warmth support bacterial growth in the water." → [`tbmed577`, para 4-14c(1)]
- Shade water to keep it cool in hot places. → "water trailers and other containers … in hot regions should be shaded to keep water as cool as possible." → [`tbmed577`, para 8-2d]
- WHO: clean, covered, cool, dark. → "Water should be stored in clean, covered containers and kept in a cool dark place." → [`who-tn05`]
- WHO: raise bulk containers off the ground, sealable lid, outlet tap. → "Ideally, the bulk storage container will be raised off the ground (e.g. either on a concrete pad or elevated stand), have a sealable lid or cover, and have an outlet tap" → [`who-tfs-house`]
- Once opened, keep it cold if you can (at or below 40 °F / 4.4 °C) and use within 1–2 days. → "store opened containers in a refrigerator at or below 40 degrees Fahrenheit or 4.4 degrees Celsius. … Use water in opened containers within one or two days." → [`csu-emerg`]
- Don't open more containers than you need. → "do not open more containers than are needed at the time." → [`csu-emerg`]

### P5 — Rotating stored water

- Self-filled water: replace every 6 months. → "Replace water every six months." → [`cdc-store`]
- Ready.gov: same, for water you bottled yourself. → "Water that has not been commercially bottled should be replaced every six months." → [`ready-water`]
- FEMA: same. → "Replace the water every six months if not using commercially bottled water." → [`fema-ayr`]
- Store-bought water: follow the expiration date. → "Observe the expiration date for store-bought water." → [`cdc-store`]; "Observe the expiration or "use by" date." → [`fema-ayr`]
- Keep bottled water sealed in its original container until needed. → "Keep bottled water in its original container and do not open it until you need to use it." → [`fema-ayr`]
- Typical bottled water label: shelf life 1 year or more. → "Commercially bottled water usually has a labeled shelf life of 1 year or more." → [`tbmed577`, para 4-14c(2)]
- No date on the label: Colorado State says at least one year with an IBWA or NSF seal. → "If none is given, bottled water with the IBWA or NSF seal should have a shelf life of at least one year." → [`csu-emerg`]
- First in, first out. → "Stored bottled/packaged field water should be used on a first in, first out basis" → [`tbmed577`]
- Why rotate if treated water keeps? Quality. → "When drinkable water is properly disinfected and stored, it should have an indefinite shelf life, but to maintain the optimum drinking quality, water should be rotated every six months." → [`usu-store`]
- What to check: lids, cracks, cloudiness. → "Check for secure lids, broken or cracked containers and for cloudiness. Replace the water and treat as before." → [`usu-water`]
- Flat taste: pour back and forth between two clean containers. → "If stored water has a flat taste, pour it back and forth between two clean containers several times to reoxygenate it." → [`osu-em9285`]
- Use old water for the garden or cleaning (not wasted). → "Repurpose your "expired" water by using it in the garden or for cleaning." → [`osu-em9285`]
- Once open, use it; don't re-store it. → "Once the container is opened, use the water rather than re-storing it." → [`osu-em9285`]
- Who regulates: FDA (bottled water); EPA (tap water). Bottlers must follow FDA good manufacturing practice rules. → "“current good manufacturing practice” regulations that require bottled water to be safe and produced under sanitary conditions" → [`fda-bw`] (page does not mention shelf life or expiration).

### P6 — Big storage: barrels, totes and tanks

**Weight of water**
- 1 US gallon weighs 8.34 lb (3.78 kg [conv]). → "One gallon of water weighs 8.34 pounds. A cubic foot of water weighs 62.4 pounds." → [`psu-rumen`]; "8.34 pounds per gallon" → [`usfs-math`]
- 1 liter weighs about 1 kg (2.2 lb [conv]). → "the weight of 1 cubic centimeter of water is 1 gram" → [`usfs-math`]
- A full 55-gallon (208 L [conv]) barrel weighs about 450 lb (204 kg [conv]) and can tip on an uneven base. → "A full 55-gallon barrel weighs 450 pounds and can tip over on an unstable surface." → [`epa-gi-rain`]
- Put tanks on a stable base; a full 3,000-gal (11,356 L [conv]) tank weighs over 24,000 lb (10,900 kg [conv]). → "be placed on a stable foundation, as water is heavy at 8 pounds per gallon (a full 3,000-gallon tank will weigh over 24,000 pounds)" → [`osu-em9101`]
- Oregon State's quick figure (1 gal = 8.3 lb) and a carrying question. → "One gallon of water weighs 8.3 pounds. Will the water jugs be too heavy to transport if you are evacuated?" → [`osu-em9285`]
- Army: storage tanks should sit level. → "Water storage tanks should be level." → [`tbmed577`, para 16-5b(1)]

**Barrels and drums**
- Large food-grade plastic containers or drums made for water exist. → "You can also purchase large food-grade plastic containers or drums that are specifically made to hold water." → [`csu-emerg`]
- 50- or 75-gallon (190–280 L [conv]) food-grade barrels are the easiest big tanks for homes. → "The most easily available and affordable storage tank solutions for homeowners are 50- or 75-gallon, food-grade barrels." → [`osu-em9101`]
- Barrel bungs have ¾-inch pipe threads (for connecting pipes or a spigot). → "Bung caps used for securing contents of the barrels have ¾-inch pipe threads to easily connect PVC pipes" → [`osu-em9101`, Fig. 10c caption]
- Drums or tankers that held fuel or non-drinking products must not hold drinking water. → "Tankers, trailers, drums, cans, pumps, or other containers, hose lines or other equipment intended or previously used for storage or distribution of petroleum products or other materials not intended for drinking should NOT be used to store or distribute potable water under any circumstances." → [`tbmed577`, para 16-5c(4)]
- Bleach dose for a 55-gal barrel: 2 tbsp — see P3. [`osu-em9285`]

**Re-used bulk tanks (WHO rule; applies to totes)**
- Only tanks that held food-grade liquids; never fuel or sewage. → "Selected tanks should only have been used for holding food-grade liquids, for example, milk, cooking oils, fruit juices, wines and spirits or vinegar. Tanks previously used for holding non food-grade liquids such as fuel and sewage should not be used." → [`who-tn03`]
- Even tiny leftovers spoil taste. → "Even minute amounts of some liquids can give the water a bad taste and people will refuse to drink it." → [`who-tn03`]
- Tanks must be easy to clean, no sharp corners. → "Tanks must be easy to clean. This means they must be accessible for cleaning and have no sharp corners that may hold dirt" → [`who-tn03`]
- Tanks must be covered, with a lockable access lid. → "Tanks must therefore be covered and fitted with an access point with a lockable lid." → [`who-tn03`]
- Unknown history: Army samples water left 24 hours to several days and has it lab-tested. → "Samples of water stored in the proposed-use containers for 24 hours to several days may be collected and analyzed for contaminants" → [`tbmed577`, para 6-2b]

**Spigots, hoses, pumps**
- A tap is better than dipping. → "From a water quality perspective, a tap should be used where possible." → [`who-tfs-house`]
- Dippers, if used, need a long handle and clean, dry storage off the ground. → "ideally they will have a long handle to minimize the risk of contamination from the user's hands (e.g. a ladle)" → [`who-tfs-house`]
- Clean and flush hoses and pumps; disinfect by recirculating chlorinated water about an hour. → "Run the pump for about an hour." → [`who-tn03`]
- Keep hose ends off the ground; Army standpipe hoses at least 4 ft (1.2 m [conv]) up. → "Hose nozzles should be clean and kept off the ground." → [`tbmed577`, para 16-5c]
- Store hoses sealed. → "Hoses and related couplings should be stored in a sealed container to protect them from contamination." → [`who-tn12`]
- Army 5-gal can: run soap solution and rinse water through the spigot. → "Drain some of the solution through the spigot to clean it." → [`tbmed577`, para 7-1c]
- Tank volume formulas (useful for bleach math): rectangle = L × W × D (m) × 1,000 = litres; cylinder = π D² / 4 × L × 1,000. → [`who-tn03`, Box 3.1]

### P7 — Building a cistern

> Check your local law: rainwater collection is not allowed everywhere. → "Collecting rainwater is not allowed in all places." / "Some states consider rainwater the property of the state and regulate its collection." → [`cdc-rain`]. EPA does not regulate household cisterns. → "the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) does not regulate household cisterns" → [`epa-cistern`]

**Siting**
- Most home cisterns are underground and feed a pressure system. → "Most cisterns are located underground and supply water to households through a pressurized plumbing system." → [`psu-supply`]
- In cold places, bury tanks below the frost line or keep them indoors where it is heated. → "the tank(s) must be protected from freezing by either burying them below the frost line or placing them in a heated garage or basement." → [`psu-lowyield`]
- Distance from toilets/latrines: Sphere default is at least 30 m (98 ft [conv]) between excreta pits and water sources when soil tests can't be done; pit bottoms at least 1.5 m (4.9 ft [conv]) above groundwater; more for fissured rock or limestone. → "the distance between containment facilities and water sources should be at least 30 metres, and the bottom of pits should be at least 1.5 metres above the groundwater table." → [`sphere`, Excreta management std 3.2] (written for water sources generally, not cisterns specifically)
- Fence animals out: ideally at least 15 m (49 ft [conv]) from the tank. → "Ideally, the fence or barrier should be constructed at least 15 meters from the storage tank." → [`who-tfs-rain`]
- Do laundry, washing and bathing well away, and downhill. → "Activities other than water collection (e.g. laundry, washing, bathing etc.) should be carried out at a safe distance from the water collection area (preferably downhill)." → [`who-tfs-rain`]
- Overflow away from the house foundation and septic drainfield. → "have an overflow outlet directed away from the house foundation or septic drainfields" → [`osu-em9101`]
- Soakaway (rubble-filled hole) under the overflow and tap so water doesn't pool. → "A soakaway prevents water from pooling and stagnating in the vicinity of the water collection area." → [`who-tfs-rain`]

**Materials**
- Common tanks: PVC and concrete (WHO); wood, plastic, metal or concrete (EPA). → "PVC and concrete storage tanks are commonly used for rainwater storage." → [`who-tfs-rain`]; "Cisterns may be made of wood, plastic, metal, or concrete" → [`epa-rainharv`]
- Some materials put lead, copper or paint chemicals in the water; use drinking-water-certified materials. → "Certain construction materials can impact the quality of the drinking-water from rainwater harvesting systems (e.g. lead, copper, certain metallic paints)." → [`who-tfs-rain`]
- CDC: storage materials can add asbestos, lead or copper. → "chemicals like asbestos, lead, or copper can get into the water" (sources include "Storage materials") → [`cdc-rain`]
- Coatings inside non-military tanks must be approved for drinking water (NSF or equal). → "Non-DOD container interiors and coatings must be approved for potable water contact by NSF International or an equivalent organization." → [`tbmed577`, para 16-7d(2)]
- Above-ground tanks should be opaque (see P8).

**Design details**
- Sealed and covered, ideally a lockable lid. → "The tank should be sealed and covered to protect stored water against contamination, ideally with a lockable lid." → [`who-tfs-rain`]
- Access hatch big enough for a person, dust-proof lockable cover, screened air vent. → "The tank should have an access port preferably large enough for a person to enter for cleaning purposes. The access must be covered with a dust-proof lockable cover. There should also be an air-vent with an outlet that is screened to prevent dust, insects, birds and other vermin entering the tank." → [`who-tn12`]
- Hatch must be watertight, gasketed, locked; cover lip overlaps the frame by at least 2 in (5 cm [conv]). → "A shoebox-style hatch must be fitted with a solid watertight cover which extends down around the frame at least 2 inches." → [`epa-hatch`] (public-system rule; good model for home tanks)
- Hatch height: buried tank — at least 24 in (61 cm [conv]) above ground; ground-level tank — at least 4 in (10 cm [conv]) above the tank roof. → "Buried Tank: 24-inch Minimum Height" → [`epa-hatch`]
- Tank needs a vent (it "breathes" as water goes in and out); screen it. → "Vents are a tank's lungs, breathing air in and out as water levels and air pressures change." → [`epa-vent`]
- Floor sloped to a drain sump with a plug, for cleaning. → "Ideally, the floor of the tank will be sloped towards a drain sump, where a sump plug can be removed to drain the tank for cleaning and maintenance." → [`who-tfs-rain`]
- Tap not too low (dirt, settled sediment). → "a low tap may withdraw settled sediment from the tank during operation if the tank is not regularly cleaned." → [`who-tfs-rain`]
- Cracks and loose tops let floodwater in. → "Cisterns can be contaminated by floodwater if not properly sealed at the top or if floodwater enters through openings, vents, or cracks." → [`psu-flood`]
- Rainwater must never flow into pipes carrying safe tap water. → "make sure rainwater cannot enter the pipes carrying safe drinking water." → [`cdc-rain`]

**Disinfect after building**
- A new tank must be disinfected (e.g. chlorine) before use. → "After a new rainwater collection system is constructed, disinfection (e.g. with a chlorine solution) of the storage tank is required before the water is used." → [`who-tfs-rain`]
- Same for any new container or tank. → "Before a new container or tank is used to store drinking-water, disinfection is required (e.g. with a chlorine solution)." → [`who-tfs-house`]
- Method: WHO 80 g HSCH per 1,000 L, 24 h (P2) [`who-tn03`]; or CDC bleach method below.
- Periodic disinfection may be needed, e.g. after long dry spells. → "Periodic disinfection of the tank may also be required (e.g. after long periods without rain)." → [`who-tfs-rain`]

**Cleaning and disinfecting a cistern (CDC, 5–6% bleach in the fact sheet; 5–9% on the web page)**
- Scrub: 1 cup (about 0.25 L) bleach in 10 gal (about 38 L) water, stiff brush; rinse; drain. → "Scrub the inside with a stiff brush and a solution of 1 cup (about 0.25 liter) of unscented liquid household bleach (5%-6%) mixed with 10 gallons (about 38 liters) of water." → [`cdc-cistern-fs`]
- Disinfect: 3 cups (about 0.75 L) bleach per 100 gal (about 380 L) of water; run each tap until you smell chlorine; leave at least 12 hours; drain; refill; run taps until no smell. → "Add 3 cups (about 0.75 liters) of 5%-6% unscented liquid household bleach for every 100 gallons (about 380 liters) of water" / "at least 12 hours" → [`cdc-cistern-fs`]; same on [`cdc-cistern`]
- Do not drink, bathe or cook with the water during disinfection. → "Do NOT drink, bathe, or cook with this water." → [`cdc-cistern-fs`]
- Bleach table (cups, full tank): 200 gal (757 L) → 6; 450 gal (1,703 L) → 13.5 (fact sheet) / 14 (web page); 600 gal (2,271 L) → 18; 1,000 gal (3,785 L) → 30. [litres conv] → [`cdc-cistern-fs`], [`cdc-cistern`]
- After refilling: 1 tbsp (15 mL [conv]) bleach per 100 gal to stop germ growth. → "Add 1 tablespoon of 5%-6% unscented liquid household bleach for every 100 gallons of water in your cistern to prevent microbial growth." → [`cdc-cistern-fs`]
- Flooded, untreated-rainwater cistern: don't use the water; bleaching floodwater doesn't work well. → "Disinfecting the water in cisterns filled with storm debris or floodwaters does not work well." → [`cdc-cistern`]
- Fuel or chemicals in the cistern: drain it fully; disinfection won't fix it. → "Water contaminated with fuel or toxic chemicals will not be made safe by disinfection." → [CDC, quoted in `epa-cistern`]
- **Never climb into a cistern** (dangerous gases). → "Do not enter the cistern. Gases and vapors can build up, creating a hazardous environment." → [CDC, quoted in `epa-cistern`]
- If someone must enter a large tank: blow in fresh air first, protective gear, a second person always outside at the hatch. → "Make sure someone remains outside the tank, next to the access hatch all the time in case the cleaner has an accident." → [`who-tn03`, Box 3.2]
- Brush on a long pole lets you clean without going in. → "Attaching the brush to a long pole may make it possible to clean the tank without entering it" → [`who-tn03`]
- Dispose of chlorine wash water away from homes, not into rivers or ponds. → "Liquid waste should not be disposed of in rivers and ponds as the organic materials and high chlorine levels may kill fish and plant life." → [`who-tn03`]
- Test rainwater you drink, cook or bathe with regularly. → "If you drink, cook, or bathe with rainwater, test it regularly for germs and chemicals" → [`cdc-rain`]

**Maintenance schedule (EPA table, rainwater systems)**
- Gutters and downspouts: twice a year. Inlet screens and filters: four times a year. Tank lids, vents, mosquito screens: once a year. Sediment in tank: every third year. → "Inspect and clean storage tank lids, paying special attention to vents and screens on inflow and outflow spigots. Check mosquito screens and patch holes or gaps immediately — Once a year" → [`epa-rainharv`, Table 1]

### P8 — Keeping out mosquitoes and algae

**Mosquitoes**
- Cover water storage tightly so mosquitoes can't lay eggs. → "Tightly cover water storage containers (buckets, cisterns, rain barrels) so mosquitoes cannot get inside to lay eggs." → [`cdc-mosq-home`]
- No lid? Use wire mesh with holes smaller than a mosquito. → "For containers without lids, use wire mesh with holes smaller than an adult mosquito." → [`cdc-mosq-home`]
- Exact mesh size used for drinking-water tanks: #24 mesh, stainless preferred; openings 0.0277 in (0.70 mm [conv]). → "Plain weave 24x24 mesh screen wire diameter is 0.014 inches which produces 0.0277-inch openings." → [`epa-24mesh`]
- #24 mesh keeps all insects out. → "#24 mesh (0.014-inch wire) will keep all insects out of storage tanks" → [`epa-24mesh`]
- Insects squeeze through gaps smaller than themselves. → "Insects can fit through spaces smaller than they are." → [`epa-24mesh`]
- Vents must be screened #24 mesh. → "Vents are a contamination pathway, so they must be screened with non-corrodible #24 mesh to keep out insects, rodents, and birds" → [`epa-vent`]
- Screen where water enters a rain system. → "Use a screen to keep out bugs and debris." → [`cdc-rain`]
- No screen? Empty rain barrels at least every 10 days. → "Empty the rain barrel at least every 10 days" → [`cdc-rain`]
- Egg to adult takes 7–10 days. → "A mosquito egg takes 7–10 days to develop into an adult mosquito." → [`cdc-aedes`]
- Aedes eggs stick to container walls above the water and survive drying up to 8 months — so scrub, don't just empty. → "Adult female mosquitoes lay eggs on the inner walls of containers with water, above the waterline." / "Eggs can survive drying out for up to 8 months." → [`cdc-aedes`]
- Weekly: empty and scrub, turn over, cover or throw out anything holding water. → "Once a week, empty and scrub, turn over, cover, or throw out any items that hold water" → [`cdc-mosq-home`]
- WHO: cover, empty and clean household water containers weekly. → "cover, empty and clean domestic water storage containers on a weekly basis" → [`who-dengue`]
- Aedes (dengue, yellow fever, chikungunya, Zika) breed in containers. → "Aedes (dengue, yellow fever, chikungunya and Zika virus), which breed in water containers such as bottles, buckets and tyres." → [`sphere`, Vector control]
- Larvicides only for water NOT used for drinking. → "Use larvicides to treat large bodies of water that will not be used for drinking and cannot be covered or dumped out." → [`cdc-mosq-home`]
- Keep gutters clean: puddles in gutters breed mosquitoes. → "Gutters should be kept clean to prevent ponding of stagnant water during short dry spells that may encourage mosquito breeding." → [`who-tfs-rain`]
- Septic tanks and vent pipes: fix cracks, cover pipes. → "Repair cracks or gaps in your septic tank if you have one." / "Cover open vent or plumbing pipes." → [`cdc-mosq-home`]

**Algae and light**
- Sunlight drives algae growth; limit it. → "Direct and indirect sunlight will act as a catalyst for algae growth in the cistern, so exposure to sunlight should be limited where possible." → [`epa-rainharv`]
- Use opaque tanks; avoid see-through, light-colored plastic. → "Cisterns made of translucent materials such as light colored plastics should be avoided." → [`epa-rainharv`]
- Oregon State: tanks should be opaque to inhibit algae; covered; vents screened against insects and frogs. → "be opaque to sunlight to inhibit algae growth" / "be covered and have vents screened to discourage infestation by insects and frogs" → [`osu-em9101`]
- Vent covers also cut sunlight and dust. → "The watertight cover must extend to the vent screen bottom to keep out rain and snow and minimize dust and sunlight entry." → [`epa-vent`]
- Light and warmth support bacteria in stored water too. → [`tbmed577`] (see P4)
- Algae cleanup on rain barrels: non-toxic cleaner. → "Clean the barrel using a non-toxic substance to remove residue or algae." → [`epa-gi-rain`]
- Don't kill a big algae bloom in drinking water with algaecide (toxins released). → "Use of an algaecide to eliminate the algal mass in order to obtain drinking water may be inadvisable, because killing algae …" (releases contaminants) → [`tbmed577`, para 8-2j] — writer: paraphrase; read the full paragraph before use.

### P9 — Protecting water from freezing

- Water expands about 9% when it freezes; leave 90–100 mL headspace in a 1-liter bottle (about 10% of the bottle). → "Water will expand by 9% when frozen, so you must leave about 90 – 100 ml of headspace in a one-liter bottle to avoid breakage." → [`csu-emerg`]
- Leave 2–3 in (5–8 cm [conv]) of air space at the top. → "Leave 2 to 3 inches of air space at the top of the container to keep it from breaking as the water expands during freezing." → [`csu-emerg`]; same 2–3 in → [`usu-water`]
- Some glass breaks no matter what. → "Some glass containers may break regardless of the air space provided." → [`csu-emerg`]
- Regular glass jars break easily in the freezer; narrow-mouth jars crack at the neck. → "Regular glass jars break easily at freezer temperatures." / "Expansion of the liquid could cause the jars to break at the neck." → [`nchfp-freeze`]
- Freezing water can crack a closed container (EPA lab method, supporting). → "the water expands significantly as it freezes and may crack the sample container." → [`epa-5035a`]
- Freezer trick: fill containers, freeze, then cap. → "Let the water freeze, then cap the container." → [`osu-em9285`]
- Frozen jugs keep a freezer cold in a power cut and become drinking water as they melt. → "It helps keep the freezer cold as long as possible in the event of the loss of power. As it melts, it provides safe drinking water." → [`osu-em9285`]
- Home tanks: bury below the frost line or keep in a heated space. → [`psu-lowyield`] (see P7)
- Farm tanks need freeze protection unless water use is steady. → "Storage tanks for farm water should also be protected from freezing unless water use tends to be continuous enough to prevent freezing." → [`psu-lowyield`]
- Rain barrels: disconnect in winter, empty, wash, store upside down; reconnect after frost. → "Disconnect the rain barrel during the winter to avoid damage. Disconnect it from the downspouts, empty the barrel, wash it out, and store it upside down" → [`epa-gi-rain`]
- Larger rain systems may need to be taken offline in fall. → "In colder climates the system may need to be taken offline in the fall of the year as colder temperatures could freeze stored water and cause damage to components." → [`epa-gi-rain`]
- Insulating jackets help keep 5-gal (19 L [conv]) water cans at an OK temperature in heat or cold. → "Five-gal water cans can be covered with an insulating jacket … to help maintain an acceptable temperature in either hot or cold regions." → [`tbmed577`, para 8-2d]
- Tank vents can freeze; heating coil where freezing is a concern. → "Install a heating coil if freezing is a concern" → [`epa-vent`]
- Check after a freeze: lids secure, containers not cracked. → [`usu-water`] (see P5)
