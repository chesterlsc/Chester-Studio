#💨 The killer you cannot see or smell First 72 h

Carbon monoxide (CO) is a gas with no color and no smell, and it can kill you.2 You will not notice it building up.

Every flame and every engine makes it. CO is in the fumes whenever fuel burns in cars and trucks, small engines, stoves, lanterns, grills, fireplaces, gas ranges and furnaces.2

Indoors, it collects. CO builds up in enclosed or partly enclosed spaces and poisons the people and animals who breathe it.2

With no grid power, generators, grills, camp stoves, gas ovens and car engines become the tools for power, cooking, charging and heat. Official outage guidance from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and Ready.gov (FEMA) warns against each of them used the wrong way.16

☠️ Danger

Outside is not automatically safe

After Hurricanes Katrina and Rita in 2005, portable generators poisoned at least 78 people in Alabama and Texas and killed 10. Most of those generators were outside, but near the home.3

#📅 What recent blackouts show

Generators have killed people after hurricanes, and police investigated a generator CO case after the 2025 blackout in Spain. Below are documented events and the scale of recent outages. Every entry is a recorded fact, not a forecast.

  1. 2005

    Alabama and Texas, after Hurricanes Katrina and Rita: portable generators poisoned at least 78 people with CO and killed 10. Most of the generators were outside but near homes.3

  2. 2017

    Puerto Rico, after Hurricane Maria: surveyed households went an average of 84 days without power.11 Long outages mean the rules in this chapter apply for months, not days.

  3. Feb 2021

    Texas winter storm: more than 4.5 million Texans lost power, some for up to 4 days.9 The state counted 246 storm-related deaths, about two-thirds of them from cold.10

  4. Sep 2024

    Hurricane Helene left 5.9 million customers without power across 10 states.12

  5. Apr 2025

    Spain and Portugal: a widespread blackout on April 28 stopped subways, traffic lights and ATMs.8 In Galicia, police investigated CO from a generator after an older couple and their adult son died at home.7

The lesson from 2005: outside was not enough. Most of those generators were outdoors but near the home.3 The CDC rule is outdoors and at least 6 m (20 ft) from doors, windows and vents.1

In a cold outage, choose only safe heat. Staying Warm Without Heat shows how to keep warm without burning anything.

#🤒 Symptoms, and why sleeping people die

The warning signs are headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain and confusion.1 The CDC notes that people often describe them as "flu-like".2

Breathing in a lot of CO can make you pass out or kill you.2

People who are asleep or drunk can die from CO before they have any symptoms.2 No headache wakes them up. A CO alarm can.

Who is at higher risk

Everyone is at risk. Infants, older people, and people with chronic heart disease, anemia or breathing problems are more likely to get sick from CO.2

⚠️ Caution

Do not try to tell CO from flu

CO can poison every person and animal who breathes it in the same space.2 If several people, or the dog, feel unwell at once while an engine, grill, stove or heater is running, treat it as CO. Get everyone outside first.1

👶 Babies & children

Babies and children

Infants are more likely to get sick from CO.2 A baby cannot tell you about a headache, so put a CO alarm near where the baby sleeps, as you would for every sleeping area.2

Let older children hear the CO alarm's test sound. Teach them one rule: when it sounds, go straight outside to the family meeting spot.

🧓 Elderly & disabled

Older and disabled people

Older people and anyone with heart disease, anemia or breathing problems are more likely to get sick from CO.2 During an outage, check on older neighbors.6

If someone cannot get out of bed alone, decide now who helps them outside when the alarm sounds.

#⛽ Generators: outdoors and far away

Run a portable generator outdoors only, at least 6 m (20 ft) from doors, windows and vents.1

Never run one in a home, garage or carport. Open doors and windows do not make it safe.14 Point the exhaust away from your house and any other building.4

Distance matters as much as "outside". Most generator poisonings after Katrina and Rita involved generators placed outdoors but close to homes.3

Set up a generator, step by step

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Choose the spot now

    Outdoors, at least 6 m (20 ft) from every door, window and vent.1 Mark it with a stake or paint so nobody guesses in the dark.

  2. Aim the exhaust away

    Point it away from your home and from any other building.4

  3. Run a long cord

    Use an extension cord longer than 6 m (20 ft), so the generator can stay far away.5

  4. Switch on a battery CO alarm inside

    Keep a battery-powered CO alarm running in the home whenever the generator runs.5

  5. Never move it closer

    Not into the garage or carport, not even in rain or to stop theft.14

A generator is fine in the garage if the big door is open.

The US Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) says never use a generator inside a home or garage, even if doors and windows are open.4

If the generator is outside the house, everyone inside is safe.

Most generator CO poisonings after Katrina and Rita involved generators outside but near homes.3 Keep it at least 6 m (20 ft) from openings.1

💡 Tip

Agree on generators with your neighbors

Your neighbors' doors and windows count too. Agree as a street that nobody runs a generator within 6 m (20 ft) of any home's doors, windows or vents.1 See Organizing Neighbors: Mutual Aid & Watch.

🐕 Dogs & pets

Dogs and other pets

CO poisons animals as well as people.2 Keep dogs away from generator exhaust, and never keep a pet in a garage or carport with an engine running.

If your dog seems weak or sick at the same time as people, take everyone, dog included, into fresh air. Then see Dog First Aid.

For fuel storage, refueling, cords and transfer switches, see Generators & Fuel Storage.

#🔥 Grills, camp stoves, ovens and cars

Use charcoal grills and camp stoves outdoors only, at least 6 m (20 ft) from windows.6

Never heat your home with a gas oven or stove.61 A gas range burns fuel, and burning fuel makes CO.2

Never run a car or truck in a garage or carport.1 If you charge phones from a car, park it in the open first.

☠️ Danger

Tents, campers and cars are enclosed spaces too

CO builds up in enclosed and partly enclosed spaces.2 A grill or camp stove inside a tent, camper, boat cabin or parked car is the same danger as one inside your kitchen.

Cook outdoors with the methods in Cooking Without Power. For stove installation, clearances and chimney care, see Heating With Wood. For safe light at night, see Light Without the Grid.

#🚨 CO alarms: your night watch

Use a battery-powered or battery-backup CO alarm.26 An alarm that runs only on mains power goes silent in a blackout.

Put one near every sleeping area.2 That is where people are when they cannot notice symptoms.2 Ready.gov also advises alarms in central locations on every level of the home.6

Replace every alarm after 5 years.2 Keep one switched on whenever a generator is running.5

Set up your alarms

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Check the label

    It must say carbon monoxide. A smoke alarm is a different device unless its label says it detects both.

  2. Choose battery power

    Battery-powered, or plug-in with battery backup. Nothing that dies with the grid.2

  3. Mount one near each sleeping area

    Include the places where babies and older relatives sleep.2

  4. Write two dates on it

    The install date, and a replace-by date 5 years later.2

  5. Test it and teach the sound

    Press the test button as the manual says. Let every family member, including children, hear what it sounds like.

🎯 Practice now

Run a night-time get-out drill

The National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) advises practicing a home escape plan twice a year, during the day and at night, with two ways out of every room.13 Use the same drill for the CO alarm: wake up, get out, meet outside, count heads.

🛒 Get it before you need it

Get these before you need them

  • Battery-powered or battery-backup CO alarms, one per sleeping area2
  • Spare batteries in the size each alarm takes
  • One extra battery CO alarm to run whenever the generator runs5
  • An extension cord longer than 6 m (20 ft)5

#🩺 If someone may be poisoned: fresh air first

Fresh air is the first treatment. If the alarm sounds, or anyone feels dizzy, sick or confused, get everyone outside into fresh air. Then get medical help.1

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Get everyone out now

    People and pets, straight outside into fresh air.1 Do not stop to hunt for the source.

  2. Keep yourself safe

    Do not go back in for belongings. The Red Cross notes that keeping yourself safe helps you take care of others.15

  3. Get medical help

    Call emergency services, or get the person to medical care as fast as you can.1

  4. Check anyone who does not respond

    Check quickly. If they do not respond and are not breathing normally, or are only gasping, start CPR and use an automated external defibrillator (AED) as soon as one is available (AHA, 2025).14

  5. Stay out while the source runs

    Do not go back in while the engine, grill or stove is still burning. Where responders exist, let them say when it is safe.

Learn CPR before you need it: see CPR & Choking: Adults, Children, Infants.

Do

  • Take the pets too2

  • Search the house for the source
  • Go back in for belongings15
  • Put a sick person to bed in the same house
  • Shrug it off as flu while engines or burners are running
⏳ The 30-year view

When no ambulance is coming

Fresh air is the one first step every household can always take.1 In a long grid-down life, prevention is the whole plan: correct placement, working alarms and a family that gets out fast.

#🛏️ The CO-safe bedtime check

Night is when CO kills quietly. Sleeping people can die before they notice symptoms.2 Do this check every night that anything has burned fuel.

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Walk the house

    No generator, grill, camp stove or engine running inside, in the garage or in the carport.16

  2. Check the generator spot

    Outdoors, at least 6 m (20 ft) from doors, windows and vents, with the exhaust pointed away.14

  3. Put out lanterns and fuel lamps

    Lanterns make CO too.2 Switch to battery lights for the night.

  4. Check every alarm

    One near each sleeping area, battery in, working.2

  5. Agree who does what

    Name the adult who wakes the children, who carries the baby, who helps older relatives and who takes the dog.

💡 Tip

Make it a habit, not a decision

Tie the check to something you already do every night, such as locking the door. A routine you do without thinking still happens when you are exhausted.

#⏳ The 30-year view Long haul

CO risk lasts as long as you burn anything. Fireplaces, stoves, lanterns and furnaces all make CO, not just generators.2

If your heat, cooking and light come from wood, charcoal or oil, the same rules apply every single day. Burn outdoors, or in a stove with a properly installed, clean chimney. See Heating With Wood.

Alarms wear out on a schedule. Replace them every 5 years,2 so a 30-year plan needs several generations of alarms.

⏳ The 30-year view

30 years of alarms: the math

At one replacement every 5 years,2 each sleeping area needs 6 alarms over 30 years. A home with 3 sleeping areas needs 18.

Store spares with the batteries they take. Check the date printed on each spare before you fit it.

The rules never expire. Teach them to every child in the household, so the next generation runs the home the same way.

#🧠 Remember this

Where does a generator go?

Outdoors, at least 6 m (20 ft) from doors, windows and vents, exhaust pointed away from buildings.

Is the garage OK with the big door open?

No. Never in a home, garage or carport, even with doors and windows open.

Why do sleeping people die of CO?

They can die before they have any symptoms. A CO alarm is their warning.

Where do CO alarms go?

Near every sleeping area. Battery or battery backup. Replace every 5 years.

Alarm sounds or everyone has a headache. First move?

Everyone, pets too, outside into fresh air. Then get medical help.

The CO symptoms?

Headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain, confusion.

Can a gas oven heat the house?

No. Never heat your home with a gas oven or stove.
