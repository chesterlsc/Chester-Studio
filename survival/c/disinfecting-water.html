⚡ The 30-second version

  • Boil clear water at a rolling boil for 1 minute. Above 2,000 m (6,500 ft), boil for 3 minutes. Boiling kills bacteria, viruses and parasites.1
  • Cannot boil? Use regular, unscented bleach. 8 drops of 6% or 6 drops of 8.25% per 3.8 liters (1 gallon). Double it for cloudy or very cold water, then wait 30 minutes.2
  • Bleach and iodine do not kill Cryptosporidium. Boiling does. Chlorine dioxide does too, but it needs hours.34
  • Boiling and disinfection do not make water with fuel or toxic chemicals safe. If you suspect them, find another source.1
  • No iodine for pregnant women or people with thyroid problems. CDC does not recommend it for them.3
  • Bleach weakens with age. EPA's method uses bleach stored less than a year. For the long haul, learn the pool shock method.2

#🧭 Pick your method in 10 seconds

Safe bottled water comes first. If you have none, EPA ranks boiling ahead of bleach. Bleach is the backup when you cannot boil.2

Boiling is the best way to kill germs, including viruses, bacteria and parasites.1 CDC notes that, except for boiling, few water treatment methods are 100% effective against all germs.3

Use the four steps below every time you treat water. Step 1 matters most, because no disinfectant fixes chemical pollution.

Quick picks

  • You have fuel: boil. It is the most reliable method.1
  • No fuel, clear water: unscented bleach, then wait 30 minutes.2
  • No fuel, unknown source or a sick person at home: chlorine dioxide tablets for the full label time, because they also kill Cryptosporidium.4
  • Cloudy water: clear it first, and double the bleach dose.2
  • Possible fuel or chemicals: do not treat it. Find another source.1

#🔥 Boiling: the most reliable method First 72 h

A rolling boil for 1 minute makes clear water safe from germs. Above 6,500 ft (about 2,000 m), boil it for 3 minutes.1

The full minute is already a safety margin. CDC asks for a whole minute to allow for people misjudging when water is really boiling.5

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Clear cloudy water first

    Let it settle and draw off the clear water, or pour it through a clean cloth, paper towel or coffee filter.1

  2. Bring it to a rolling boil

    A rolling boil means big bubbles that keep breaking the surface even when you stir.

  3. Hold the boil for 1 minute

    Hold it for 3 minutes above 2,000 m (6,500 ft). Use the timer below.1

  4. Cool it with the lid on

    Do not add ice unless the ice was made from safe water.

  5. Store it clean and covered

    Pour it into clean, sanitized containers with tight covers.1 See Storing Water.

Timer: Rolling boil: 1 minute (3 minutes above 2,000 m / 6,500 ft). Interactive in the digital edition.

You must boil water for 10 or 20 minutes to make it safe.

CDC says 1 minute at a rolling boil, or 3 minutes above 6,500 ft.1 Boiling kills germs quickly, and the full minute already adds a safety margin.5 Extra minutes only burn fuel you will need later.

Flat taste? Pour the cooled water back and forth between two clean containers a few times.1

☠️ Danger

Boiling does not remove chemicals

Water polluted with fuel or toxic chemicals cannot be made safe by boiling or disinfection.1

Use a different source. See Chemical Contamination.

💡 Tip

Save fuel for water

Boiling depends on fuel. Keep a pot with a lid and a fuel reserve that is only for water. See Cooking Without Power for stoves that run on wood and charcoal.

#🧴 Bleach: the right bottle, the right drops

Use regular, unscented chlorine bleach whose label says it is for disinfection and sanitizing.2

Do not use scented, color-safe or "with cleaner" bleach. EPA rules those out for drinking water.2

Read the strength on the label. EPA's table is for bleach with 6% or 8.25% sodium hypochlorite.2

Use fresh bleach. EPA's method calls for bleach that has been stored at room temperature for less than one year.2 That limit assumes room temperature, so keep bottles in a cool, dark place and date each one.

EPA gives these doses for clear water and says to double them for cloudy, colored or very cold water.2 For bigger containers, multiply the 1-gallon dose, or use the calculator.

📊 Key fact

Bleach of another strength

EPA's table covers 6% and 8.25% bleach only.2 If your label shows another percentage, do the arithmetic from EPA's 6% dose: drops per gallon = 48 ÷ your %, rounded up.

For example, 5.25% bleach needs 10 drops per gallon and 4% bleach needs 12. The same formula gives EPA's 6 drops for 8.25%. Double it for cloudy, colored or very cold water.2

Bleach calculator. Interactive in the digital edition.

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Clear the water

    If it is cloudy, murky or colored, let it settle or pour it through a clean cloth, paper towel or coffee filter.2

  2. Count the drops

    Use a clean dropper and the table above. Keep that dropper only for bleach.

  3. Stir and wait 30 minutes

    Do not drink it, cook with it or give it to pets during the wait.2

  4. Smell it

    It should smell slightly of chlorine. If not, repeat the dose and wait another 15 minutes.2

  5. Fix a strong taste

    Let it stand open to the air for a few hours, or pour it from one clean container to another several times.2

  1. Minute 0

    Add the drops and stir.

  2. Minute 30

    Smell test. A faint chlorine smell means it is ready.2

  3. Minute 45

    Only if there was no smell: you repeated the dose at minute 30, so it is ready now.2

  4. A few hours

    Optional: let strong-tasting water stand open to the air.2

Timer: Bleach contact time: 30 minutes. Interactive in the digital edition.

Do

  • Use regular, unscented bleach with the % on the label
  • Write the purchase date on every bottle
  • Clear cloudy water before dosing
  • Wait the full 30 minutes
  • Double the dose for cloudy or very cold water

Don't

  • Use scented, color-safe or "with cleaner" bleach
  • Use bleach stored over a year if fresh bleach exists
  • Guess the dose by pouring
  • Drink before the wait is over
  • Rely on bleach for Cryptosporidium
⚠️ Caution

Five common bleach mistakes

  • Wrong bottle: scented, color-safe or "with cleaner" bleach is not for drinking water.2
  • Old bottle: EPA's method uses bleach stored less than a year.2
  • Cloudy or icy-cold water at the normal dose: double it.2
  • Drinking early: the 30-minute wait is part of the treatment.2
  • Dirty containers: clean water poured into a dirty jug is no longer clean. Store it in clean, sanitized containers with tight covers.1

Just pour in a splash of bleach. More is always safer.

Measure it. EPA gives exact drops for clear water and says to double them only for cloudy, colored or very cold water.2 If the water then tastes too strong, let it stand open to the air for a few hours.2

#🦠 Cryptosporidium: the germ bleach cannot kill

Bleach and iodine kill most harmful bacteria and viruses. They are less effective against the tougher parasites Cryptosporidium and Giardia.1

Against Cryptosporidium ("Crypto"), iodine and chlorine are not effective. That is CDC's rating in its water treatment guide.3

Boiling kills it. So does chlorine dioxide, if you follow the maker's instructions.1 Chlorine dioxide is more potent than the same dose of chlorine and kills most waterborne germs, including Cryptosporidium.4

Layer your methods

A filter with pores of 1 micron or smaller, plus chlorine dioxide, has a "very high" rating against Cryptosporidium in CDC's guide.3

Filter first, then disinfect. CDC says to add a disinfectant after filtering to kill viruses and any remaining bacteria.13 Filter types and pore sizes are in Water Filters & SODIS.

📊 Key fact

Only boiling comes close to 100%

CDC states that, except for boiling, few water treatment methods are 100% effective at removing all germs.3 When someone in your home is very young, very old or sick, boil when you can.

Symptoms of Crypto, Giardia and other water germs are in Waterborne Diseases.

#💊 Iodine and chlorine dioxide tablets

Iodine: short-term only

Iodine does not reliably kill Cryptosporidium, and it works less well in cloudy water.4

Follow the manufacturer's instructions for the dose and the waiting time.3

⚠️ Caution

Who should not use iodine

CDC does not recommend iodine-treated water for:

  • Pregnant women
  • People with thyroid problems
  • People with a known iodine hypersensitivity
  • Anyone using it continuously for more than a few weeks at a time3
🌸 Women & girls

Pregnant? Skip iodine

CDC does not recommend iodine-treated water during pregnancy.3 Boil water instead, or use bleach or chlorine dioxide tablets.

Write "NOT FOR PREGNANCY" on the iodine bottle so no one forgets.

🧓 Elderly & disabled

Thyroid disease or iodine allergy

CDC does not recommend iodine-treated water for people with thyroid problems or a known iodine hypersensitivity.3 Ask about thyroid conditions before you hand out iodine water.

Chlorine dioxide: slow but thorough

Chlorine dioxide kills most waterborne germs, including Cryptosporidium.4 The catch is time.

Tablets and drops need several hours of contact time. They also leave no lasting disinfectant in the water.4

📊 Key fact

Why the label wait matters

In a 2014 lab study of chlorine dioxide tablets, killing Cryptosporidium took hours, not minutes, and weaker doses took longer.6

Lab results are not a field promise. Always use the wait time on your product label.

Protect the tablets. Chlorine dioxide is volatile and breaks down in sunlight. Do not expose tablets to air, and use any mixed solution quickly.4

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Read the label

    Find the water volume per tablet and the wait time. Some labels give a longer time for Cryptosporidium.

  2. Clear the water first

    Settle it, then pour it through a clean cloth.1

  3. Add the tablet

    Use the exact volume on the label. Open one blister at a time.

  4. Wait the full time

    If you do not know the source, use the longest time on the label. Crypto needs hours.4

  5. Keep it covered

    Treated water has no lasting protection, so store it clean and closed.4

#🏊 Pool shock: EPA's long-term chlorine source Long haul

Granular calcium hypochlorite is dry chlorine. EPA's emergency disinfection guidance, as archived in 2013, uses it in two steps: make a strong stock solution, then dilute it into the water you drink.7

The method is written for high-test granular calcium hypochlorite with about 70% available chlorine.7 If the label lists a different active ingredient, this method does not apply.

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Work outside, away from kids and dogs

    Use a measuring spoon and bucket that you keep only for chlorine.

  2. Make the stock

    Dissolve 1 heaping teaspoon (about ¼ oz, 7 g) in 2 gallons (7.6 L) of water. This gives about 500 mg/L of chlorine.7

  3. Label the stock

    Write "CHLORINE STOCK: DO NOT DRINK" and the date on the container.

  4. Dilute 1 part to 100

    Add 1 part stock to 100 parts clear water. That is roughly 1 pint (16 oz) of stock per 12.5 gallons (47 L).7

  5. Air out a strong smell

    If the chlorine smell is too strong, pour the water back and forth between clean containers.7

☠️ Danger

The stock is not drinking water

The stock is about 500 mg/L of chlorine, 100 times stronger than the water you drink after dilution.7 Keep it labeled, capped and locked away.

Never mix pool chemicals with other cleaners, and keep the granules dry and sealed. If someone swallows the stock, see Poisoning.

⚠️ Caution

Pool shock still misses Crypto

Calcium hypochlorite is chlorine. CDC rates chlorine as not effective against Cryptosporidium.3 Boil, or use chlorine dioxide, when Crypto is a concern.

#👶 Babies, children, dogs and elders

👶 Babies & children

Babies and children

  • Lock up bleach, tablets and pool shock where children cannot reach them.
  • Keep children away from pots of boiling water. Let water cool on a back burner with the lid on.
  • For mixing formula and feeding bottles, follow Feeding Babies When Formula Runs Out.
  • Teach older children the 30-minute rule: no drinking from a bucket that is still "waiting".
🐕 Dogs & pets

Dogs and pets

Give your dog the same treated water your family drinks, in a bowl you wash daily.

Never leave stock solution or a dosing bucket where a dog can drink from it. Dog water diseases are covered in Dog Health.

🧓 Elderly & disabled

Older and disabled people

  • A full pot of boiling water is heavy. Let it cool, then ladle it out instead of lifting it.
  • Counting drops is hard with poor eyesight. Pre-mark a measuring spoon, or have one person do all dosing.
  • Check thyroid conditions before using iodine.3

#⏳ Disinfecting water for 30 years

⏳ The 30-year view

Plan for each method running out

  • Bleach: EPA's method uses bleach under a year old.2 Stored bleach is a first-year tool, not a 30-year one.
  • Iodine: CDC does not recommend continuous use for more than a few weeks at a time.3
  • Chlorine dioxide: Tablets must stay sealed from air and light.4
  • Boiling: Works for decades if you have fuel.1

The arithmetic for one household

Say your household drinks and cooks with 15 liters (4 gallons) a day. That is about 5,500 liters (1,460 gallons) a year. Your real daily need depends on climate, work and family size.

Bleach: at 12 drops (⅛ teaspoon) of 8.25% bleach per 2 gallons, 1,460 gallons takes about 91 teaspoons.2 That is about 450 mL (15 fl oz) of fresh bleach a year, for clear water.

Pool shock: 1 heaping teaspoon makes 2 gallons of stock, which treats 200 gallons at 1 to 100.7 So 1,460 gallons needs about 7 heaping teaspoons (about 50 g, 1.8 oz) a year.

This is dosing arithmetic only. It is not a promise of shelf life. Store granules dry, sealed and dated, and check the label.

💡 Tip

Use more than one barrier

Clear water first, then kill germs. Over the years, pair a biosand filter or a certified filter with boiling or chlorine.3

Keep a simple water log

Write down every batch: the date, the container, the method and the dose. When someone gets sick, a log shows which water they drank.

Add a line when you open a new bleach bottle or tablet pack. That tells you when the bleach passes one year.2

🎯 Practice now

Practice this now

  • Dose 1 gallon of tap water with bleach. Count the drops, wait 30 minutes and learn the faint chlorine smell.2
  • Time a 1-minute rolling boil on your emergency stove and note how much fuel it uses.
  • Look up your home's altitude and write "1 min" or "3 min" on your pot lid.1
🛒 Get it before you need it

Get these before you need them

  • Regular, unscented bleach with the strength on the label (date every bottle)
  • A glass or plastic dropper and a measuring spoon set used only for chemicals
  • Chlorine dioxide tablets, kept sealed
  • A large pot with a lid, and fuel set aside for water
  • Granular calcium hypochlorite labeled about 70%, stored dry and locked
  • Coffee filters or clean cotton cloth for clearing water

#🧠 Remember this

How long do you boil water?

1 minute at a rolling boil. 3 minutes above 2,000 m (6,500 ft).

How much 6% bleach per gallon (3.8 L)?

8 drops for clear water (6 drops of 8.25%). Double for cloudy or very cold water.

How long after adding bleach?

30 minutes. No chlorine smell? Repeat the dose and wait 15 more minutes.

Which germ do bleach and iodine not kill?

Cryptosporidium. Boil, or use chlorine dioxide with a long wait.

Who should avoid iodine water?

Pregnant women, people with thyroid problems or iodine allergy, and anyone using it for more than a few weeks.

Pool shock ratio?

1 heaping tsp in 2 gallons makes stock. Then 1 part stock to 100 parts water.

Does boiling remove fuel or chemicals?

No. Find another source.

How old can stored bleach be?

EPA's method uses bleach stored at room temperature for under a year.
