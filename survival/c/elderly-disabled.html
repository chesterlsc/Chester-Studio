#📉 Who dies first when the power fails

The disaster record points one way. Older and chronically ill people carry the most risk when power, water and medical care stop.18

This chapter covers what that means for the people in your care. It gives the documented facts first, then the actions.

Hurricane Maria, Puerto Rico, 2017

Surveyed households went an average of 84 days without power, 68 days without water and 41 days without cell phone service.2 In remote areas, 83% of homes had no electricity for the whole period studied, from landfall to December 31, 2017.2

The first official death toll was 64.2 A George Washington University study, commissioned by the governor, estimated 2,975 excess deaths from September 2017 to February 2018.1

Many of those people were not killed by wind or water. The household survey linked about one third of excess deaths to delayed or interrupted medical care.2 Poor and elderly people faced the highest risk.1

The official death toll shows who a disaster killed.

After Maria the first official count was 64.2 The George Washington University study estimated 2,975 excess deaths, with poor and elderly people at highest risk.1 Deaths from missed care kept coming for months: the study counted through February 2018.

Texas winter storm, February 2021

Texas health officials counted 246 storm-related deaths. About two-thirds were from cold exposure, mostly hypothermia (dangerously low body temperature).7

The final count rose from 210 to 246.7 Cold exposure killed more people than every other cause combined.

The Hurricane Rita evacuation, 2005

The evacuation killed more people than the storm did. Over 100 of the at least 119 deaths attributed to Rita were linked to the evacuation itself.8

At least 10% of evacuation deaths were linked to overheating in vehicles. Medical examiners suspected overheating or a worsened chronic illness in about another 50%.8

A bus carrying nursing-home residents caught fire in the evacuation traffic, and about two dozen people died.8 More on this in Stay or Go?

⏳ The 30-year view

The 30-year view: everyone becomes the elder

Over 30 years, a 45-year-old parent becomes 75 and a 60-year-old becomes 90. Plan as if every adult in your group will need this chapter one day.

Write each person's needs down now, while they can tell you, and update the list at every family drill. See Building Your Group.

#🔌 Oxygen, CPAP and other powered equipment

Many people stay alive or mobile on machines that plug into the wall. In the US, over 3 million Medicare patients have claims for electricity-dependent medical equipment.3

Over 4.6 million rely on that equipment or on services such as dialysis, oxygen or home health care.3 Oxygen concentrators, powered wheelchairs and home dialysis all depend on electricity.3

Other common examples are continuous positive airway pressure (CPAP) machines for sleep apnea, electric hospital beds, and feeding or suction pumps. Each one stops when its battery is empty.

Make the plan before the outage. Ready.gov says to talk to your medical provider about a power outage plan for medical devices that run on electricity.4

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. List every device and every user

    Include your family and your street. Write the person's name, the device, and what the provider says happens if it stops.

  2. Find the real battery time

    Read the manual and write the hours on a label on the device. Then test a full charge while the device runs, and write that time too.

  3. Ask the provider for an outage plan

    Cover each device and each refrigerated medicine.4 Ask what to do if the device cannot run at all.

  4. Choose one safe power source

    A generator runs outdoors only, more than 6 m (20 ft) from windows, doors and vents.10

  5. Decide where to go

    If the home gets too hot or too cold, and it is safe to travel, go to a place with heat or cooling.4 Choose it now, with a route.

☠️ Danger

Generators kill families who run them indoors

Never use a generator inside a home or garage, even if the doors and windows are open.11 In the April 2025 Spain blackout, police in Galicia investigated carbon monoxide from a generator after an older couple and their adult son died at home.12

Put a battery-powered carbon monoxide (CO) alarm near every sleeping area, and replace it every 5 years.10 See Carbon Monoxide: The Blackout Killer.

⚠️ Caution

Oxygen and open flames

Light the house with flashlights, not candles. Ready.gov lists candles as a fire risk during outages.4

Keep every flame, stove and smoker away from oxygen equipment. Ask the oxygen supplier now for its written fire-safety rules and keep them with the equipment.

💡 Tip

Label every oxygen cylinder

Ask the supplier how long each cylinder lasts at the person's prescribed flow rate. Write that time and the fill date on a tag tied to the cylinder, so any helper can read it in the dark.

⏳ The 30-year view

Powered devices over 30 years

Every machine that needs electricity depends on your power system for as long as the grid is down. Ask the provider now what non-powered options exist for each device.

For power you can make yourself, see Small Solar & Batteries and Low-Tech Power for the Long Haul.

#💊 Medicines: the clock starts when the power stops

Start with a written list. The Red Cross lists a medication list and medical information among the documents to copy for your kit.6 Write each medicine's name, dose, times and what it is for.

Keep at least 7 days on hand. The current Red Cross survival kit list says a 7-day supply of medications and medical items.6 An older Red Cross checklist says a 1-month supply.15

Ask the doctor or pharmacist how to build a larger emergency supply legally. Ready.gov also lists non-prescription medicines: pain relievers, anti-diarrhea medicine, antacids and laxatives.16

Medicines that need a fridge

The one-day rule: if the power is out for more than a day, throw away medicine that should be refrigerated, unless the label says otherwise.4

If a life depends on that medicine, talk to a doctor or pharmacist and use it only until a new supply is available.4

Insulin has its own rule. Vials and cartridges, opened or unopened, may be left unrefrigerated at 15–30°C (59–86°F) for up to 28 days. Pens and other products differ, so read the label.13

Replace insulin that has been exposed to extreme heat or cold.13 For any other medicine kept at the wrong temperature, check its storage instructions or ask a pharmacist, provider or the maker.14

🧓 Elderly & disabled

Long-term medicine plans

Medicines for heart disease, diabetes, lung disease and other long-term conditions are covered in Chronic Conditions Without Pharmacies. How long stored medicines stay good is in Medicines: Shelf Life & Expiration Facts.

#🌡️ Heat and cold hit older bodies hardest

Older adults and young children are especially vulnerable to extreme temperatures, says Ready.gov.4 The record agrees.

In the 2021 Texas storm, about two-thirds of 246 deaths came from cold exposure.7 In the Rita evacuation, at least 10% of evacuation deaths were linked to overheating in vehicles.8

Move early, not late. If the home becomes too hot or too cold and it is safe to travel, go to a place with heat or cooling.4 Decide while roads and daylight still allow it.

Never heat a home with a gas oven or stove. Ready.gov warns against it because of carbon monoxide.4

Do

  • Use flashlights, not candles4
  • Move frail people early to a place with heat or cooling4
  • Check on older neighbors in person4
  • Keep a battery CO alarm by each bed area10

Don't

  • Heat the house with a gas oven or stove4
  • Run a generator inside, even with doors open11
  • Leave a frail person in a hot, stuck car8
  • Wait until the roads are full to leave
👶 Babies & children

Babies and elders in one room

Ready.gov names both older adults and young children as especially vulnerable to extreme temperatures.4 Keep them together in the warmest room in winter, or the coolest in summer, so one adult can watch both.

See Staying Warm Without Heat and Staying Cool Without Air Conditioning.

🧓 Elderly & disabled

Visit, do not just call

Ready.gov puts checking on neighbors on its list of things to do in an outage.4 Knock on the doors of older people who live alone or use medical devices. A dead phone tells you nothing.

Signs and treatment of hypothermia and heat stroke are in Hypothermia, Frostbite & Heat Stroke.

#🦯 Mobility, hearing, vision and communication aids

Small aids decide whether someone can walk, hear a warning or read a label. The Red Cross kit list includes hearing aids with extra batteries, glasses, contact lenses, syringes and a cane.6

Ready.gov adds prescription eyeglasses and contact lens solution.16 It also says to replace expired items and to re-think your needs every year.16

Communication

Make the plan usable by the person it is for. The CDC says accessible planning and communication may help close the preparedness gaps for people with disabilities.9

Write a card for each person. Put their name, conditions, medicines, allergies and devices on it, and how they communicate best: speech, writing, sign language or pictures. Keep one copy on them and one in the kit.

Know what is normal for them. Normal vital signs change with age and overall health.17 Ask family what this person's usual alertness, speech and walking look like, and write it on the card.

Before you decide someone is newly confused, check that their hearing aids and glasses are in and working. How to check a sick person step by step is in Assessing a Sick or Injured Person.

Light, water and a toilet within reach

Give each person their own flashlight with extra batteries, and keep it within reach of the bed. Ready.gov says to use flashlights, not candles.4

Water is heavy. One liter weighs 1 kg and one US gallon weighs 8.33 lb.18 So a full 19 L (5-gallon) jug weighs about 19 kg (42 lb). Keep small containers within reach of anyone who cannot lift that.

Put a toilet within reach of anyone who cannot walk far, with its own handwashing water. See Toilets Without Water.

🎯 Practice now

Learn the hands-on skills now

Ask the person's nurse, doctor or therapist to show you, while help still exists:

  • How to move them from bed to chair
  • How to prevent pressure sores
  • How to make their home safer against falls
  • How to handle dementia day to day

Write down what they teach and keep it with this book. Bed care and pressure sores are also in Nursing the Sick at Home.

#🚪 Moving people who cannot move fast

People with disabilities face disasters more often. In a June 2024 CDC survey, 80% of adults with disabilities had lived through at least one natural disaster, against 70% of adults without disabilities.9

They also have fewer ways out. 26% said they had nowhere to go, against 15%. 20% named the cost of evacuating, against 14%.9

Plans were rare on both sides: only 48% of adults with disabilities and 46% without had an emergency plan.9

Step by step · 6 steps

  1. Name a main helper and a backup

    NFPA says to plan for anyone with a disability and to assign a backup in case the main helper is not home.5

  2. Check every meeting place

    FEMA says to make meeting places accessible for household members with disabilities.19 Check steps, slopes and distance.

  3. Keep the car ready

    Keep at least half a tank of gas at all times, and a full tank if an evacuation seems likely.20

  4. Leave early

    Go early enough that severe weather cannot trap you.20 Long waits in traffic were where Rita evacuees overheated.8

  5. Pack for a long, hot wait

    Carry water and a way to stay cool for every person in the car, plus the aids and medicines listed above.86

  6. Practice with them

    Run the drill twice a year, by day and by night, as NFPA advises for fire escape plans.5

🧓 Elderly & disabled

Two ways out of every room

NFPA's escape plan asks for two ways out.5 For someone who uses a wheelchair or walker, check that both ways are actually usable: no steps, no narrow gaps, nothing stacked in the way.

Full family plans are in The Family Emergency Plan, and grab-and-go lists in Go-Bags for Every Family Member.

🐕 Dogs & pets

Assistance dogs and pets

Pack the dog's own kit. The Red Cross lists a collar, leash, ID, food, carrier and bowl.6

Temporary public shelters may not allow animals, so find pet-friendly places to stay before you need them.21 See Dogs in a Long Emergency.

#🍲 Food, water, choking and infection

Older adults get sicker from food poisoning. Adults 65 and older are in the CDC's higher-risk group.22

Get care early for severe signs: bloody diarrhea, a fever over 38.9°C (102°F), frequent vomiting, signs of dehydration, or diarrhea that lasts more than 3 days.22 Safe food without a fridge is in Food Safety Without a Refrigerator.

Give them your best-treated water. In people with weak immune systems, the parasite Cryptosporidium can cause diarrhea for weeks to months, with wasting.23 See Disinfecting Water.

Protein does not shrink with age

The adult recommended dietary allowance (RDA) for protein stays at 0.8 g per kg of body weight from age 19 to over 70.24

For a 60 kg (132 lb) person, that is 48 g of protein a day. For calories and special diets, see Calories & Nutrition and Special Diets.

Choking in a chair or wheelchair

If you cannot get your arms around someone's waist, use chest thrusts instead of abdominal thrusts.25 Full adult choking steps are in CPR & Choking.

#🧠 Remember this

Who had the highest risk of dying after Hurricane Maria?

Poor and elderly people. Older men had a 35% higher death risk than expected.

What share of Maria's excess deaths was linked to interrupted care?

About one third.

Refrigerated medicine after more than a day without power?

Throw it away unless the label says otherwise. If a life depends on it, ask a doctor or pharmacist and use it until new supply arrives.

How long can insulin vials stay out of the fridge?

Up to 28 days at 15–30°C (59–86°F). Pens differ: read the label.

Where does a generator go?

Outdoors, more than 6 m (20 ft) from windows, doors and vents. Never in a garage, even with the door open.

How many helpers for each person who cannot move fast?

A main helper and a backup.

Which small aids do people forget?

Hearing aids with extra batteries, glasses, contact lenses and solution, a cane, and the medicine list.
