#🗺️ Why a written family plan matters First 72 h

A family plan answers five questions before you need them. Where do we meet? Who do we tell? Who gets the kids? Who helps the people who need help? What happens to the dog?

In a crisis there is no time to discuss these. A written plan means every person already knows their job.

Most adults do not have one. In a June 2024 CDC survey, only 46% of adults without a disability and 48% of adults with a disability had an emergency plan.1

This chapter builds your plan in order: the out-of-area contact, meeting places, school and work, the people and pets who need help, drills, and a fill-in template at the end.

Two decisions live in other chapters. Whether to shelter or leave is in Stay or Go?. What each person carries is in Go-Bags for Every Family Member.

⏳ The 30-year view

The 30-year view: build two layers

Official family plans lean on phones and texting. Keep that layer, because it is fast while networks work.

Then add a no-phone layer: fixed meeting places, set check-in times and paper notes. That layer still works when there is no power to charge anything. It is covered below in "When phones are down".

#📞 The out-of-area contact and the paper contact list

Choose one person who lives outside your area to be your family's message center. The American Red Cross advises this because local phone lines tend to be overloaded after an emergency.4

FEMA and Ready.gov give the same rule: pick someone outside town, because they may be easier to reach in a disaster.56

Everyone reports to the same person. Then one message to that person tells you where everyone is, and you do not have to reach five phones one by one.

Set up your out-of-area contact

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Ask a calm person far away

    Choose a friend or relative who lives outside your area. The Red Cross calls this your out-of-area contact.4 Ask them, and get a clear yes.

  2. Pick a backup in a different region

    If your main contact lives in the same disaster zone as you on the day, you still have a second person to reach.

  3. Agree on a short message format

    Who, where, condition, next move. Example: "Ana OK. At spot 3. Kids with me."

  4. Write both numbers on paper

    Put them on a wallet card for every person, including in each child's backpack.8

  5. Test it

    Have every person text the contact once. Fix any wrong number the same day.

Text first, keep calls short

Do

  • Text for anything that is not a life-threatening emergency57
  • Keep calls brief and give only vital information7
  • Limit non-emergency calls to save your phone battery7
  • Call 911 first for a life-threatening emergency7

Don't

  • Redial at once if a call fails; wait 10 seconds first7
  • Make non-emergency calls that tie up the network7
  • Count on texting 911; it only works in some places7

If something happens, we will just call each other.

Calls can fail even when the equipment works, because network congestion blocks them. The FCC advises texting instead, because texts may get through when calls do not.7 FEMA says the same.5

The paper contact list

Write every important number on paper. A contact list inside a dead phone cannot help you.

List the out-of-area contact and backup, close neighbors, each school or daycare, each workplace, your doctor or clinic, your vet, and the local animal shelter.

💡 Tip

Use the official wallet card

FEMA's family communication plan comes as a fillable, wallet-sized card.8 Fill one in for every person. Put copies in each go-bag, each car and each child's school bag.

🎯 Practice now

Practice this now while phones still work

This week, have every family member text the out-of-area contact: "Test. I'm OK. At home." Fix any wrong number on every card the same day.

#📍 Four meeting places, near to far

Pick safe, familiar places where your family can go for protection or to reunite. FEMA's plan uses four levels: indoors, in your neighborhood, outside your neighborhood and out of town.5

The Red Cross gives the reason for the two closest ones. One spot right outside the home is for a sudden emergency like a fire. A second spot is for when you cannot return home.4

What each meeting place is for
SpotUse it whenGood examples
1. Inside homeHigh winds or a storm: you must shelter indoors5A small, windowless inner room on the lowest floor5
2. Just outsideA sudden emergency like a fire4A big tree, the mailbox or a neighbor's house5
3. Out of neighborhoodYou cannot get home4Library, community center, house of worship, friend's home5
4. Out of townYou cannot get home or to spot 3, or your area is told to evacuate5A relative's or friend's home in another town5

Every spot must work for every person. FEMA says to make sure meeting places are accessible for household members with disabilities.5 Check steps, slopes, distance and whether there is shelter from rain and sun.

Every spot must work for your animals too. FEMA says to think about animal-friendly locations.5 Temporary public shelters may not allow animals, so line up pet-friendly options ahead of time.13

Measure the route to spots 3 and 4 on a paper map and write the distance in both kilometers and miles. See Map & Compass Navigation.

⚠️ Caution

Give each spot a backup

A library can flood. A friend's house can burn. For spots 3 and 4, write a second choice next to the first, and agree that whoever arrives first leaves a dated note if they have to move on.

#🏫 School, daycare and work plans

Disasters do not wait until everyone is home. Your plan must cover the hours when children are at school, adults are at work and someone is on the road.

The Red Cross says to know the evacuation sites at work and at school or day care.4 It also tells commuters to make a plan for how the family will stay in contact if separated.10

📊 Key fact

Separation is a documented risk

After Hurricanes Katrina and Rita in 2005, 5,192 children were reported missing. Officials called it the largest child-recovery effort in US history, and it took more than six months.2 The last child was reunited in March 2006.3

Make the school and daycare plan

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Ask for the school's emergency plan

    Ask where children go if the building is evacuated, and how the school contacts parents.

  2. Update the pickup list

    Ask who the school will release your child to. Add at least two trusted adults besides the parents, and keep it current.

  3. Teach your child the pickup names

    Name the adults on the school's list out loud with your child. Teach them to go only with those people.

  4. Put a card in every backpack

    Parents' numbers, the out-of-area contact and the meeting places, on a wallet card.8

  5. Teach the plan at the child's level

    Ready.gov has a family plan page written for kids.6 Practice it together.

Work and commuting

Know your workplace evacuation site.4 Then decide now who leaves work to collect the children and who goes home to the animals and older relatives.

Write down a walking route home from work in case roads or transit stop. Keep walking shoes, water and a paper map at work, as part of your work go-bag.

👶 Babies & children

Babies and young children

Assign one adult to each baby, plus a backup in case that adult is away. NFPA gives this advice for infants in home escape plans.9 Do the same for every child too young to get out alone.

On the plan, write each child's feeding needs, formula type, medicines and allergies. Keep a current photo of each child with the wallet card.

#🔀 When phones are down: the no-phone plan

If the network is down, your plan must work on foot and on paper. That means fixed places, fixed times and written notes.

  • Fixed check-in times. Example: "If we cannot reach each other, go to spot 3. Be there at 9 a.m. and 5 p.m. every day."
  • Dated notes. Anyone who leaves home or a meeting spot leaves a note: date, time, names, where they went.
  • One direction. Always move outward through the spots in order: 1, 2, 3, 4. Then searchers know where to look next.

For ways to pass messages without phones, see Radios: Ham, GMRS, CB & Listening.

🌸 Women & girls

Women and girls

Plan routes so that no woman or girl has to walk alone to a far meeting place, especially at night. Pair people up in the plan.

If someone is pregnant, add her due date, her clinic and her prenatal records to the plan. See Personal Safety for Women & Girls.

⏳ The 30-year view

Keeping the plan alive for decades

Children grow up, people move and pets change. Review the whole plan at each twice-yearly drill, the same schedule NFPA gives for escape plan practice.9

Write in pencil so it is easy to update. Keep copies in sealed plastic bags, and give one to your out-of-area contact.

#🧑‍🦽 Babies, older adults, disabilities and pets

More adults with disabilities have lived through a disaster. In the CDC's June 2024 survey, 80% of adults with disabilities had lived through at least one natural disaster, compared with 70% of adults without disabilities.1

They also face more barriers to leaving. 26% said they had nowhere to go, compared with 15% of adults without disabilities. 20% named the cost of evacuating, compared with 14%.1

The CDC says accessible planning and communication may help close these gaps.1 That starts with your family plan.

🧓 Elderly & disabled

Older adults and people with disabilities

Name a helper and a backup for each person who needs help to move. NFPA says to plan for anyone with a disability and assign a backup in case the main helper is not home.9

List their medicines, devices (wheelchair, walker, hearing aids, glasses) and exactly how to move them. Check that every meeting place is accessible to them.5 See Caring for Elderly & Disabled People.

🐕 Dogs & pets

Dogs and other pets

Pets belong in the family plan. Ready.gov says to include them and to have your vet microchip each pet.11 If a lost pet is taken to a vet clinic or shelter, staff can scan it for the microchip number.13

Find pet-friendly places to stay before an emergency, because public shelters may not take animals.1213 Check the microchip registry has your current phone number. See Dogs in a Long Emergency.

#🔁 Drills: how and how often

Practice twice a year. NFPA says to practice your home fire escape plan twice a year.9 The Red Cross plan also says to practice evacuating your home twice a year.4

Drill in daylight and at night. For young children, NFPA suggests telling them about a drill before bed, because the goal is practice, not fear.9

  1. Week 1

    Write the plan, fill in wallet cards and choose all four meeting places.

  2. Month 1

    Daytime drill: use both exits from each room, then text the out-of-area contact.9 Walk the routes to spots 3 and 4 with the children and write down the time and distance.

  3. Month 6

    Night drill, then review and update the whole plan.9

  4. Month 12

    Daytime drill again. Keep going: one day drill and one night drill every year.9

  5. Each new school year or job

    Update school, daycare and work details and the pickup list.

Run a home drill

Step by step · 7 steps

  1. Tell young children first

    Explain what will happen. For a night drill, tell them before bed.9

  2. Start the drill

    Press the smoke alarm test button so everyone learns the sound.

  3. Use the second way out

    Every room needs two ways out. Check that the doors and windows that lead outside open.9

  4. Get low under smoke

    Practice crawling low to the exit.9 Close doors behind you to slow the fire.9

  5. Meet at spot 2 and count heads

    Helpers arrive with their assigned baby, older adult and dog.

  6. Text the out-of-area contact

    "Drill. All OK at spot 2." This tests the contact's number as well as your exits.

  7. Fix what went wrong

    Talk about what was slow or confusing. Update the plan that day.

☠️ Danger

Never go back inside

Once you are out, stay out. Do not go back into a burning home for any reason.9 A fire can leave you as little as 1 or 2 minutes to escape once the smoke alarm sounds.9 Tell the fire department who is missing, including pets. Firefighters have the equipment to do rescues.9 Teach this rule to every child.

In an emergency you can always text 911.

Text-to-911 is only available in some locations. Always try to call 911 first.7

For a bigger household or group, give each adult a written role. See Building Your Group: Size, Roles & Skills.

#🧠 Remember this

Why an out-of-area contact?

Local phone lines tend to overload after an emergency. Everyone reports to one distant person.

What are the four meeting places?

Inside home, just outside, out of the neighborhood, out of town.

Call or text?

Text. Texts may get through when calls do not. Keep calls short.

How often do you drill?

Twice a year, once in daylight and once at night.

How long might you have to escape a home fire?

As little as 1 or 2 minutes. Never go back inside.

Who helps the baby, Grandma and the dog?

A named helper for each, plus a named backup.

Phones are down. Now what?

Go outward through the spots in order, show up at check-in times and leave a dated note.
