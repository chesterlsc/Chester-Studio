#🧭 Where to get water, in the right order

When the taps stop, work from the safest source outward. Stored water first, then hidden water inside the building, then outdoor water that you treat.

FEMA's advice is plain: use bottled water first when you have it, because it is the safest and most reliable option.2 Building that store is covered in Storing Water for Months & Years, and how much you need is in How Much Water You Really Need.

No official body publishes one safety ranking of rain, springs, wells, streams and ponds. CDC's rule is simpler: every outdoor source needs treatment before anyone drinks it.1

The table below puts every common source in one place. It runs from your first choice to the water you must never drink.

#🏠 Hidden water inside your home or building

Before you walk anywhere, check the building you are in. FEMA names three indoor sources you can use when the taps stop.2

  • Water heater tank. Use it only if it is part of your drinking water system, not your home heating system.2
  • Melted ice. Safe if the ice was made with water that was not contaminated.2
  • Toilet tank (the box behind the seat). Only if the water is clear and no toilet cleaner has been added, and disinfect it first.2

An older FEMA and Red Cross booklet ruled out toilet tanks completely.3 FEMA's current guidance allows clear, cleaner-free tank water once it is disinfected.2 When in doubt, treat it.

How to drain a water heater safely is covered step by step in Storing Water. Do it once now, while you can still call a plumber if something goes wrong.

Getting water out of a toilet tank

Step by step · 4 steps

  1. Lift the tank lid and look

    Check for colored water, a cleaner tablet or a clip-on cleaner. If any toilet cleaner is in there, do not drink it.2

  2. Use it only if it is clear

    FEMA allows tank water only when it is clear.2

  3. Scoop it, do not flush it

    Ladle it into a clean container. Flushing sends it to the bowl, and bowl water is never drinking water.3

  4. Disinfect it

    Boil or treat it before anyone drinks it.2 See Disinfecting Water.

Do use (indoors)

  • Water heater tank on the drinking water line
  • Ice made from clean water
  • Clear toilet tank water, after disinfecting
  • Pool or spa water for washing and cleaning

Don't drink

  • Toilet bowl water
  • Radiator or hot water boiler water
  • Waterbed water
  • Pool or spa water
☠️ Danger

Heating systems and waterbeds are not drinking water

FEMA's emergency food and water guide says not to use water from radiators, waterbeds, toilet bowls, or swimming pools and spas for drinking.3 FEMA's older shelter guide gives the reason for waterbeds: fungicides added to the water, or chemicals in the vinyl, can make it unsafe.4

💡 Tip

Remove toilet cleaner tablets now

An in-tank cleaner tablet turns a usable tank into one you cannot drink from.2 Take tablets out today and flush a few times, so every tank in the house stays a backup supply.

#🌧️ Outdoor sources: rain, springs, wells, streams and ponds

CDC lists four kinds of outdoor water you can make safe: rainwater; streams, rivers and other moving water; ponds and lakes; and natural springs.1

All four must be treated to kill germs before anyone drinks them.1 No outdoor source gets a free pass, however clean it looks.

Rain

Rain is on CDC's list of outdoor sources, and it still needs treatment.1 Roof catchment, gutters, first-flush diverters and tank math are in Rainwater Harvesting.

Springs

Natural springs are on the same list, with the same rule: treat the water before drinking.1

Wells

Well water can be contaminated during floods.1 EPA says private wells need to be tested and disinfected after floodwaters recede.7

Well types, hand pumps and shock chlorination are in Wells & Hand Pumps Without Electricity.

Streams, rivers, ponds and lakes

CDC warns that rivers, streams and lakes may be contaminated with livestock waste, human sewage, chemicals and other contaminants.1

Treatment kills germs, but boiling does not remove chemical pollutants.1 That makes choosing the source your first filter. See Chemical Contamination for what filters can and cannot remove.

Step by step · 6 steps

  1. Look upstream first

    Note farms and animal pens, sewage pipes and septic systems, and factories, fuel stores or dumps. These match the livestock waste, sewage and chemicals CDC warns about.1

  2. Reject chemical water

    If the water may hold fuel or toxic chemicals, walk away. Boiling will not remove them.1

  3. Collect in a clean container

    Use a container that has only held food or water. Keep the cap and the rim clean.

  4. Clear cloudy water

    Settle and strain cloudy water before you treat it. Methods are in Water Filters & SODIS.

  5. Treat every liter

    Boil or disinfect it to kill germs.1 Doses and times are in Disinfecting Water.

  6. Store and label it

    Pour it into a clean, covered container. Write "TREATED" and the date on it so nobody mixes it up with raw water.

Clear, fast-running stream water is clean enough to drink as it is.

CDC lists streams and rivers among sources that must be treated, and warns they may carry livestock waste, human sewage and chemicals.1 How fast the water moves does not change that rule.

🌸 Women & girls

Women and girls: fetch water together

Go to water in pairs or groups, in daylight, with an agreed route and return time. Never send a girl alone to a distant source. Safe paths, lighting and latrine design are in Personal Safety for Women & Girls.

🧓 Elderly & disabled

Older and disabled people: lighten the load

A full 19 L (5 gal) container weighs about 19 kg (42 lb), because each liter of water weighs about 1 kg (2.2 lb). Use 4 L (1 gal) jugs, a wagon or a garden cart. Give people who cannot carry a seated job, such as treating, labeling and logging water.

#🗺️ Map every water source near you, now

The best time to find water is before you need it. A paper map of nearby sources lets anyone in the family find water when you are sick, away or asleep.

Draw it on paper, so it works without a charged phone. See Maps & Compass for reading and marking maps.

Step by step · 6 steps

  1. Get a paper map

    A local street or topographic map, or a hand-drawn sketch. Mark your home in the center.

  2. Walk a circle around home

    Mark every stream, river, pond, lake and spring. Add wells, rain barrels, large clean roofs and public taps.

  3. Note what is upstream

    Mark farms, sewage pipes and fuel or chemical sites near each source.1

  4. Note who owns it

    Ask the owner now for permission to draw water in an emergency. Taking without permission can be a crime. See Scavenging: the Law & Ethics.

  5. Note the route and the carry

    Write the distance and how long the walk took with a full container. Pick the flattest route.

  6. Recheck after every flood

    Floods can contaminate wells, and floodwater can reach streams and lakes.15 Mark flooded sources as "test before use".

🎯 Practice now

Practice the carry this week

Fill a 19 L (5 gal) container, carry it 100 m (110 yd) and time yourself. Then try it with a cart. You will learn which containers, handles and routes work for your family before it matters.

🛒 Get it before you need it

Get these before you need them

  • Food-grade water containers with handles and tight caps, in sizes each person can carry
  • A wagon, garden cart or wheelbarrow
  • A funnel and clean cotton cloths for straining
  • A bucket with 10 m (33 ft) of rope, for reaching water from banks and open wells
  • A waterproof paper map, pencils and labels
  • Treatment supplies from Disinfecting Water
⚠️ Caution

What this chapter leaves out, and why

This edition gives no methods or yield figures for finding water by plant or animal signs, dew collection, solar stills or plant bags. We could not verify published figures for this printing, and this book does not print unverified numbers.

Plan your supply around stored water, rain and treated surface water. Official guidance covers all three.12

#🏊 Pools, spas and other urban water

CDC says pool and spa water can be used for personal hygiene and cleaning, but not for drinking.1 FEMA's emergency guide also lists pools and spas among the sources not to drink from.3

Pool chemistry is set for swimmers. Canadian pool rules keep free chlorine between 0.8 and 5.0 parts per million (ppm), a level judged not harmful to bathers who swallow some water.10 That is a swimming standard, not a drinking-water standard.

Pool water is already chlorinated, so it is safe to drink.

CDC limits pool and spa water to hygiene and cleaning.1 Pool chlorine levels are set for swimmers who swallow a little, not for daily drinking.10

Good uses for pool water

  • Washing hands and bodies
  • Cleaning floors, tools and surfaces
  • Laundry (see Laundry Without Machines)

Every bucket of pool water you use for cleaning is a bucket of treated drinking water you save.

Tanks in apartment and office buildings

If your building has water tanks, find out now what each tank is for. Ask the building manager whether it is on the drinking water line, the heating system or the fire system.

Water from heating systems, such as radiators and hot water boilers, is not drinking water.3 A water heater tank on the drinking water line is.2

⚠️ Caution

Neighbors' pools and tanks

Ask before you take. Agree now, in writing if you can, who may use a pool or tank in an emergency and for what. Shared rules prevent fights later. See Community & Mutual Aid.

#🌊 Floodwater: water everywhere, none to drink

Never drink floodwater. CDC says it can contain sewage, and eating or drinking anything contaminated by it can cause diarrheal disease such as E. coli or Salmonella infection.5

Floods also move containers of chemicals away from where they were stored.5 So floodwater can hold germs and chemicals at once, and boiling does not remove chemical pollutants.1

Touching floodwater is a smaller risk than swallowing it. CDC says skin contact alone does not pose a serious health risk; eating or drinking anything contaminated with floodwater is what causes disease.6

☠️ Danger

Floods poison wells and streams too

Well water can be contaminated during floods.1 EPA says private wells must be tested and disinfected after floodwaters recede.7 CDC also warns against bathing in rivers, streams or lakes contaminated by floodwater.5

If you must work in or near floodwater

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Cover up

    Wear rubber boots, rubber gloves and goggles.5

  2. Keep it out of your mouth

    Do not eat, drink or smoke with floodwater on your hands. Swallowing it is the main risk.6

  3. Do not bathe in it

    Not in floodwater, and not in rivers, streams or lakes it has reached.5

  4. Wash clothes hot

    Wash clothes that touched floodwater or sewage in hot water and detergent before wearing them again.5

  5. Check for advisories

    Ask your water utility or health department whether a boil water advisory is in force.7

👶 Babies & children

Babies and children

CDC says toys that were in floodwater must be disinfected before children play with them.5 Keep children out of floodwater and away from cleanup work.

Mix infant formula only with your best water: stored bottled water first.2 See Feeding Babies When Formula Runs Out.

🐕 Dogs & pets

Dogs

Floodwater can carry sewage and chemicals.5 Keep dogs out of it where you can, and give them the same treated water you drink. If a dog wades through floodwater, rinse its paws and coat with clean water before it licks itself. See Dog Health.

For the full picture of flood hazards, see Floods. For the diseases floodwater spreads, see Waterborne Diseases.

#🚫 Water you must never drink

Some water makes you worse off than drinking nothing. Learn these by heart and teach them to every child old enough to understand.

Seawater

Seawater is toxic to humans. USGS explains that your body cannot get rid of the salt: the kidneys cannot get enough fresh water to dilute it, and the body fails.8

About 3.5% of seawater's weight is dissolved salt.9 For every cup of seawater you drink, you would need at least the same amount of fresh water for your kidneys to flush that salt out.9

Urine

Do not drink urine. It carries salts and waste products your body has just removed. Drinking it hands that load back to your kidneys, which need more water to clear it again.11

Urine is also not sterile. It can contain bacteria, even in healthy people.11

Drinking your own urine stretches your water supply in an emergency.

Urine returns salt and waste to a body that is already short of water, so the kidneys need extra water to remove it again.11

Water with fuel or chemicals in it

CDC says do not use water contaminated with fuel or toxic chemicals. Boiling will not remove chemical pollutants.1 Find another source.

If you boil water long enough, any water becomes safe.

Boiling does not remove chemical pollutants such as fuel.1 Choose a different source when chemicals are possible.

Heating systems, waterbeds and toilet bowls

FEMA lists radiators, waterbeds, toilet bowls, and pools and spas as water not to use for drinking.3 Waterbed water can hold fungicides or chemicals from the vinyl.4

⏳ The 30-year view

The 30-year view

Stored water is a bridge, not a supply. Any supply that lasts decades has to come from rain, springs, wells or surface water, and CDC says all of those need treatment.1

Use year 1 to build lasting systems: rain catchment, a hand-pumped well if you can, and a biosand filter. Keep your source map up to date every year.
