⚡ The 30-second version

  • Never ration water. Drink what you need today and look for more for tomorrow. That is FEMA and Red Cross guidance.1
  • Ration food, not water. Healthy people can live on half their usual food for an extended period. Do not cut food for children or pregnant women.1

#🗓️ Your 30-day map First 30 days

The first 72 hours keep you alive tonight. Days 4 to 30 turn quick fixes into daily systems. If you have not done the first 72 hours yet, start with The First 72 Hours.

Work one week at a time. Each week has one main job. Finish it before you move on.

  1. Week 1 · Days 4–7

    Water and toilets. Build a daily water routine. Set up a toilet and a handwashing station. Do the first full inventory. Give everyone a role.

  2. Week 2 · Days 8–14

    Sanitation every day, rations set. Use the inventory numbers to set portions. Choose one warm or cool room. Meet your nearest neighbors.

  3. Week 3 · Days 15–21

    Security and community. Start a watch rota, build a neighbor network, repair what broke, recount.

  4. Week 4 · Days 22–30

    Long-term systems. Rain catchment, garden beds, a seed check, compost, and a written plan for month 2.

💡 Tip

One wall, one sheet

Tape the week's jobs, the roles and the inventory "days left" numbers on one wall. Everyone checks the wall, not their memory.

#💧 Week 1: Turn stored water into a water system

Never ration water. If supplies run low, drink the amount you need today and try to find more for tomorrow.1

Plan on at least 3.8 liters (1 gallon) per person per day. That is the CDC minimum to store. The CDC says to store more for pregnant women, sick people, pets and hot climates.3

Of that, a normally active person needs to drink at least 2 quarts (1.9 liters) each day.12 In very hot weather, water needs can double. Children, nursing mothers and sick people may need more.6 See How Much Water You Really Need.

Stored water is a bridge, not a plan. After Hurricane Maria in 2017, surveyed Puerto Rico households went an average of 68 days without water.9 Week 1 is when you find the next source and a way to treat it, while your stores still cover you.

Step by step · 6 steps

  1. Measure what you have

    Write the volume on every container. Add it up in liters and gallons.

  2. Work out days left

    Total water ÷ (number of people × 3.8 L or 1 gal). Write the answer on the wall.

  3. Choose your next source

    Rain, a well, a spring or a stream. See Finding Water When the Taps Stop.

  4. Choose and stock a treatment

    Boiling, bleach or a filter. See Disinfecting Water.

  5. Separate clean from dirty

    Mark some containers "DRINKING ONLY". Never pour untreated water into them.

  6. Log it every day

    One line a day: liters in, liters used, days left.

⚠️ Caution

Do not wait for the last container

Start collecting and treating from your next source while you still have a stored reserve. Then one bad day does not become a dry day.

🐕 Dogs & pets

Dogs drink from your stores too

Add a line for each dog on the water log. Measure what your dog drinks on a normal day now, so you have a real number. See Dogs in a Long Emergency.

#🚽 Weeks 1–2: Toilets and handwashing before anything else

Disease outbreaks after disasters follow conditions, not the calendar. A review in CDC's journal Emerging Infectious Diseases linked outbreak risk to displacement, crowding, safe water and sanitation, the population's underlying health, vaccination coverage and access to health care.4

Displacement is the main risk factor. Disasters that did not displace people rarely led to outbreaks.4 If your home is safe, making it livable keeps your family out of crowded temporary shelters. See Stay or Go?

Diarrheal outbreaks can follow contamination of drinking water.4 So the toilet and the water system are one job: waste must never reach what you drink.

The same review found the risk of diarrheal outbreaks after disasters is higher in developing countries than in industrialized countries.4 If your water or sanitation was weak before the outage, treat this section as urgent.

📊 Key fact

Your alarm is a condition, not a date

Act on sanitation the day any of these happens: tap water is no longer safe, the toilet stops flushing, or extra people move in. Each is a risk factor named in the review.4 For how fast each disease appears, see Waterborne Diseases.

Why sanitation matters by week 2

Illness shows up after a delay. The delay between swallowing a germ and the first symptom is called the incubation period.

Cholera can start within hours. Symptoms usually begin 2 to 3 days after exposure, but can start in a few hours or take up to 5 days.7 Hepatitis A takes 15 to 50 days, 28 on average.8

Do the arithmetic. A water or toilet mistake on day 4 can bring cholera by day 9, and hepatitis A anywhere from day 19 to day 54. A week 1 mistake can still make someone sick 7 weeks later.

How many toilets you need

Plan one toilet per 20 people, or one per household, when households share.5 In the first phase of an emergency, no toilet should serve more than 50 people.5

Put a handwashing station right next to every toilet. The Sphere humanitarian standards call for handwashing facilities close to latrines.5

Step by step · 6 steps

  1. Pick the method

    Flush with non-drinking water, or switch to a bucket or dry toilet. See Toilets Without Water.

  2. Pick the place

    Private, and away from food, water containers and any well. The Toilets chapter gives distances.

  3. Add handwashing

    Water, soap and a basin beside the toilet.5 See Handwashing & Personal Hygiene.

  4. Make one house rule

    Wash hands after the toilet and before touching food or drinking water.

  5. Give it an owner

    One person empties, cleans and restocks on a fixed schedule. Rotate the job weekly.

  6. Light the way

    Keep a lamp or a light ready on the path for night trips.

Dead bodies left after a disaster cause epidemics.

A 2007 review found no evidence that dead bodies cause epidemics. Deaths in rapid-onset disasters are mostly from blunt trauma, crush injuries and drowning.4 See Handling the Dead Safely.

👶 Babies & children

Check vaccines this month

Measles risk after disasters depends on how many people were already vaccinated, especially children under 15.4 Find your family's vaccination records now. See Vaccine-Preventable Diseases.

🌸 Women & girls

Safe toilets for women and girls

Put the toilet where women and girls can reach it safely at night: a short, lit path and a door that locks from inside. Pair up for night trips. See Personal Safety for Women & Girls.

🧓 Elderly & disabled

A toilet within reach

For anyone who cannot walk far, put a seat-height bucket toilet near where they sleep, with its own handwashing basin. See Caring for Elderly & Disabled People.

#📋 Day 4: The first full inventory

You cannot ration what you have not counted. Do the first full count on day 4. Recount every 7 days.

Turn every count into "days left". Days left = amount on hand ÷ amount used per day. One number per item is easy to read when you are tired.

Step by step · 6 steps

  1. Clear one shelf at a time

    Group like items. Work in pairs: one person counts, one writes.

  2. Count food in person-days

    One person-day is enough food for one person for one day at their normal eating.

  3. Count water

    Liters or gallons, from the water log.

  4. Count medicines per person

    Doses left ÷ doses per day = days left. Do this first for anyone on daily prescriptions.

  5. Count everything else

    Fuel, batteries, lamp oil, soap, bleach, diapers, formula, menstrual products and dog food.

  6. Post the numbers

    Write each "days left" on the wall sheet. Circle the lowest three. Those are next week's problems.

A clipboard, a paper notebook, pencils, a permanent marker and masking tape for labels. Paper inventory works with no power at all.

#🥫 Week 2: Rationing math (food yes, water no)

Food can be rationed. Water cannot. FEMA and the American Red Cross say food may be rationed safely, except for children and pregnant women.1

Healthy people can survive on half their usual food intake for an extended period, and without any food for many days.12

That guidance covers healthy people. For anyone with diabetes, kidney disease or another diet-sensitive condition, read Special Diets before you change what they eat.

Worked example: a family of 4

Two adults and two children have 120 person-days of food.

  • Everyone eats full portions: 4 person-days used a day. 120 ÷ 4 = 30 days.
  • Adults eat half, children eat full: 0.5 + 0.5 + 1 + 1 = 3 a day. 120 ÷ 3 = 40 days.

Halving adult portions bought this family 10 days. It did not make any food. Use those days to start growing and collecting it.

Do

  • Cut healthy adults' portions first
  • Keep children and pregnant women on full portions
  • Write portions down so everyone sees them
  • Recount every 7 days and adjust

Don't

  • Ration water
  • Cut food for children or pregnant women
  • Guess at stock levels
  • Let one person control food in secret
👶 Babies & children

Children are not rationed

FEMA and Red Cross guidance excludes children from food rationing.1 For babies on formula, see Feeding Babies When Formula Runs Out.

🌸 Women & girls

Pregnant women are not rationed

The same guidance excludes pregnant women from food rationing.1 For breastfeeding mothers, see Breastfeeding.

🐕 Dogs & pets

Dogs are on the sheet too

Count dog food in days like everything else. If it runs out inside 30 days, read Feeding Dogs Without Kibble now.

⏳ The 30-year view

The 30-year view

Rationing stretches a store. It never refills one. Every number on the inventory sheet reaches zero unless a system replaces it.

#🧭 Routine and roles

Give every job a person and every person a job. A shared routine means fewer decisions when everyone is tired.

In a household of 2, each person takes 2 or 3 roles. Write them down anyway. Swap the hardest jobs each week.

Many key jobs can be done sitting down: record keeper, inventory counter, teacher, night radio listener. Give elders a named role on the wall.

🎯 Practice now

Practice a no-power weekend

While help still exists, live by the routine table for one weekend without using grid power. Write down every problem, then fix it before you need to.

#🌡️ Week 2: One warm room or one cool room

Pick one room and live in it. One room is easier to keep warm or cool than a whole house, and easier to watch.

Heat for warmth competes with fuel for cooking and boiling water. Use the fuel "days left" from your inventory before you choose a method.

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Choose the room

    Use Staying Warm Without Heat or Staying Cool Without Air Conditioning to pick it.

  2. Move sleeping there

    Babies, elders and sick people get the best spots.

  3. Check fuel days left

    Take the number from the wall sheet. Keep enough fuel to treat water.

  4. Read the carbon monoxide chapter

    Do this before any flame or engine is used indoors or near the house.

  5. Check people twice a day

    Morning and evening, check babies, elders, the sick and dogs. See Hypothermia, Frostbite & Heat Stroke for warning signs.

☠️ Danger

Read this before you burn anything indoors

Heaters, stoves, lamps, grills and generators all burn fuel. Before you use any of them for heat, light or cooking, read Carbon Monoxide: The Blackout Killer.

🧓 Elderly & disabled

Who to watch most closely

See What Kills People First for the groups at highest risk from heat and cold, and check them first every day.

#🤝 Weeks 2–3: Security and neighbors

Your nearest neighbors are your first backup. They are close enough to help today, and you are close enough to help them.

Security in the first month is mostly quiet habits: locks, a watch rota, not showing what you have, and knowing who lives around you. Details are in Home Security & Deterrence and Keeping a Low Profile.

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Visit in daylight, in pairs

    Start with the two or three nearest homes.

  2. Share names and needs

    Who has babies, elders, medical devices or dogs.

  3. Swap skills

    List who can do first aid, repairs, gardening and child care. See Organizing Neighbors.

  4. Set a weekly meeting

    Same place, same day, same time.

  5. Agree on a help signal

    A sound, a flag or a runner, so anyone can call for help without power.

Do

  • Lock doors and gates at dusk
  • Keep supplies out of sight from doors and windows
  • Keep a written night-watch rota
  • Talk problems down. See De-escalation

Don't

  • Tell people outside your group how much you have
  • Post about your supplies online
  • Confront strangers alone
  • Take from homes or stores without permission. That is theft. See Scavenging vs Looting
🌸 Women & girls

Women and girls in the plan

Make sure women and girls have a say in where the toilet, water point and sleeping spaces go. Use a buddy for night trips and night watch.

🐕 Dogs & pets

Dogs on the rota

A dog that barks at strangers is an early alarm. Keep it fed, vaccinated and under control around neighbors. See Rabies & Diseases Animals Give Humans.

#🌱 Weeks 3–4: Start the long-term systems

Start long-term systems while stores still cover you. In the worked example, food lasted 40 days with adults on half portions. Anything you plant needs to be growing well before that day.

Seeds planted in week 4 do not feed you in week 4. Check each crop's days to harvest on the seed packet, and plan for the gap.

  1. Do the third inventory

    Around day 28, recount everything.

  2. Sort by days left

    Put the lowest numbers at the top of the list.

  3. Match each item to a system

    Use the table above. Some items have no replacement yet: note them.

  4. Give each system an owner

    Write a name and a start date next to it.

  5. Write the month-2 plan

    Put it on the wall. Then read Year 1 to Year 30.

The 30-year view

#🧠 Remember this

Should you ration water?

No. Drink what you need today and find more for tomorrow.

How little food can healthy adults live on for an extended period?

About half their usual intake. Never cut food for children or pregnant women.

How much water do you plan per person per day?

At least 3.8 L (1 gallon) stored, with at least 2 quarts (1.9 L) for drinking. Very hot weather can double the need.

What drives outbreaks after disasters?

Displacement most of all, plus crowding, water and sanitation, underlying health, vaccination coverage and access to health care.

How soon after bad water can cholera start?

Within a few hours to 5 days, usually 2 to 3 days. Hepatitis A takes 15 to 50 days.

How many people per shared toilet?

Plan 1 per 20 people (or 1 per household). Never more than 50 in the first phase.

What is the days-left formula?

Amount on hand ÷ amount used per day.

Do dead bodies cause epidemics?

No. A 2007 review found no evidence that they do.
