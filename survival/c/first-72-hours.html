⚡ The 30-second version

  • Light the house with flashlights, not candles. Never run a generator inside a home or garage, even with the doors and windows open.15
  • Fill every clean container with tap water in the first hour. In the 2003 blackout, Cleveland's main water pumps lost power and taps dropped to a trickle.9
  • Keep the fridge and freezer shut. A closed fridge keeps food safe for about 4 hours. A full freezer holds about 48 hours, a half-full one about 24.2
  • Read every medicine label for "refrigerate". Insulin in vials or cartridges can stay at 15–30°C (59–86°F) for up to 28 days.7
  • Keep phones charged and the gas tank full. Then check on your neighbors.1

#⏱️ The order of actions at a glance First 72 h

Work in this order: people, light, water, cold food, air, medicines, contact. The fastest dangers come first, and the slow ones wait their turn.

Every clock in this chapter comes from official power outage guidance: Ready.gov, the US Department of Agriculture (USDA), the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the Food and Drug Administration (FDA).12

Write down the time the power went out. Tape it to the fridge door. The food and medicine decisions below all count from that moment.

  1. Minute 0

    Safety check. Flashlights on, not candles. Count every person. Make sure no generator, grill or stove is running indoors.14

  2. Hour 1

    Fill water containers while the taps still run. Read medicine labels. Unplug electronics to protect them from surges when power returns.1

  3. Hour 4

    The closed fridge reaches its safe limit. If the outage will last longer, move fridge food into coolers with ice.2

  4. Hour 24

    A half-full freezer reaches its limit. Once the outage passes one day, Ready.gov's rule for refrigerated medicines applies.21

  5. Hour 48

    A full freezer reaches its limit with the door kept closed.2

  6. Hour 72

    Switch to the routine in The First 30 Days: water system, toilets, rationing and roles.

Print this page

Tape the timeline inside a kitchen cupboard. In a real outage, your phone may be dead and this book offline.

#🔦 Minute 0 to 15: the safety check

Use flashlights, not candles. Ready.gov lists candles as a fire risk during power outages.1

Keep a flashlight with extra batteries for every household member, including children.1

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Count everyone

    Call each name out loud. If someone is missing, write down where they were last.

  2. Turn on flashlights

    Give each child their own light and keep them in the same room as an adult.

  3. Check the air

    If a carbon monoxide (CO) alarm sounds, or anyone feels dizzy, sick or confused, get everyone outside into fresh air and get medical help.4

  4. Unplug electronics

    Power can come back with surges or spikes. Disconnect appliances and electronics to avoid damage.1

  5. Write the time

    Note the time the power went out on paper. Every food and medicine decision counts from it.

☠️ Danger

Never heat the house with a gas oven or stove

Ready.gov and the CDC both say not to use a gas stove or oven to heat your home. It is a carbon monoxide risk.14 See Carbon Monoxide: The Blackout Killer.

🎯 Practice now

Run a 15-minute blackout drill

One evening, switch off the lights and phones. Find the flashlights, count heads and fill the water containers. Time it, then fix whatever slowed you down.

#🚰 Hour 1: capture water while the taps still run

Fill every clean container now, before the pressure drops. City water can stop when the pumps that move it lose power.9

What happened in the 2003 blackout

The Northeast blackout began on August 14, 2003.13 Cleveland's water system shut down when its four major water supply pumps lost power. Taps gave no more than a trickle.9

About 1.5 million people in the Cleveland area lost water. Residents used bottled water for drinking and bathing.1011

The danger did not end when pressure came back. Residents were told to boil drinking water, because the system could have been contaminated when pressure was lost.1011

In Detroit, a boil-water alert covered 4.3 million customers. It was not lifted until Tuesday, August 19, 2003, five days after the blackout began.1213

📊 Key fact

No one can tell you how long your taps will run

The 2003 blackout caused loss of water service in some areas, not all.9 Treat every minute of pressure as borrowed time. Fill first, then deal with everything else.

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Fill drinking containers first

    Use clean bottles, jugs and pots with lids. Label them "DRINKING".

  2. Fill cooking pots and kettles

    Cover them so dust and insects stay out.

  3. Fill the bathtub and buckets

    Use this water for flushing and washing. Keep small children away from a full tub.

  4. Turn the taps off when flow slows

    Write down the time the pressure dropped.

  5. When water returns, follow boil-water notices

    Do not drink tap water until officials say it is safe, or until you have treated it.10 See Disinfecting Water.

How much does your family need? Use the calculator, then read Storing Water for Months & Years for safe containers and hidden water in the house.

👶 Babies & children

Babies: keep separate water for formula

Fill and label one covered container only for mixing formula. If a boil-water notice is issued, do not use tap water for formula until the notice is lifted or the water is treated. See Feeding Babies When Formula Runs Out.

#🧊 Hours 0 to 48: the fridge and freezer clock

Keep the doors shut. A refrigerator keeps food safe for up to 4 hours during a power outage if you do not open the door.23

A full freezer holds its temperature for about 48 hours with the door closed. A half-full freezer holds about 24 hours.23

Use appliance thermometers. The fridge should be at 4°C (40°F) or below, and the freezer at −18°C (0°F) or below.2

What to do when each clock runs out (USDA, Ready.gov)
ApplianceSafe forThen
Fridge, door closedAbout 4 hoursThrow out perishable food: meat, poultry, fish, soft cheeses, milk, eggs, leftovers and deli items2
Freezer, half fullAbout 24 hoursCheck each item for ice crystals or a temperature of 4°C (40°F) or below2
Freezer, fullAbout 48 hoursSame check as above2
Food at 4°C (40°F) or warmerUnder 2 hoursThrow it away after 2 hours. Also throw out any food with an unusual smell, color or texture1

If the outage will last more than 4 hours, move fridge food into coolers with ice.2

Perishable food left in a fridge with no power for more than 4 hours goes in the trash: meat, poultry, fish, soft cheeses, milk, eggs, leftovers and deli items.2

Frozen food that still has ice crystals, or is at 4°C (40°F) or below, can be safely refrozen.2

If it tastes fine, it is safe to eat.

USDA says never taste food to decide whether it is safe. When in doubt, throw it out.2

For which foods to eat first and what is safe at room temperature, see Food Safety Without a Refrigerator.

🛒 Get it before you need it

Two cheap thermometers

Buy one appliance thermometer for the fridge and one for the freezer. Without them, you are guessing at the 4°C (40°F) line.2

#💊 Medicines that need cold

Read every medicine label in the house. Look for the word "refrigerate". Do it in the first hour, while you can still reach a pharmacist.

If a medicine was not kept at its recommended temperature during an outage, the FDA says to check its storage instructions or ask a pharmacist, doctor or the maker whether it is still usable.8

The one-day rule: if the power is out for more than a day, discard medicine that should be refrigerated, unless the label says otherwise.1

If a life depends on that refrigerated medicine, talk to a doctor or pharmacist and use it only until a new supply is available.1

Insulin

Insulin does not fail the moment the fridge stops. Insulin in vials or cartridges, opened or unopened, may be left unrefrigerated at 15–30°C (59–86°F) for up to 28 days and still work.7

Pens and some brands have different limits. Check the label of your exact product.7

Above 30°C (86°F), the 28-day figure no longer applies. The FDA gives it only for 15–30°C (59–86°F).7 Read the label for the words "do not freeze" too, and if you keep insulin in a cooler, do not let it touch the ice.

When properly stored insulin is available again, the FDA advises replacing vials that were exposed to extreme conditions.7

Once the fridge stops, all insulin is ruined.

The FDA says insulin vials and cartridges keep working for up to 28 days at 15–30°C (59–86°F).7 Replace vials exposed to extreme conditions once properly stored insulin is available.7

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Gather every medicine

    Put all of them on one table, including children's and pets' medicines.

  2. Sort by the label

    Make two piles: "refrigerate" and "room temperature".

  3. Write a list

    For each cold medicine, write the name, the dose and how many days of supply are left.

  4. Call while you can

    Ask a pharmacist or doctor about each cold medicine while the phones work.8

  5. Watch the clock

    Once the power has been out for more than a day, apply the one-day rule above to everything that is not life-saving.1

🧓 Elderly & disabled

Medical devices and older adults

Talk to your medical provider now about a power outage plan for medical devices that run on electricity and for refrigerated medicines.1 Older adults and young children are especially vulnerable to extreme temperatures.1 See Chronic Conditions Without Pharmacies.

⏳ The 30-year view

The 30-year view

Insulin vials and cartridges last up to 28 days out of the fridge, and only at 15–30°C (59–86°F).7 In a grid-down life measured in years, anyone on daily cold medicines needs a written plan with their doctor before the outage, not after.

#☠️ Carbon monoxide: the danger you cannot see

Run a generator outdoors only, at least 6 m (20 ft) from doors, windows and vents. Never use one inside a home, garage or carport.4

The US Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) is blunt: never use a generator inside a home or garage, even if the doors and windows are open. Point the exhaust away from the house and other buildings.5

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Place it outside

    At least 6 m (20 ft) from any door, window or vent.4

  2. Point the exhaust away

    Aim it away from your home and other buildings.5

  3. Use a long cord

    An extension cord longer than 6 m (20 ft) keeps the generator at a safe distance.6

  4. Turn on a battery-powered CO alarm inside

    Use one in your home whenever a generator runs. Ready.gov advises CO alarms with battery backup on every level of the home.61

  5. Never move it indoors

    Not into the garage, not into the carport, not even with the doors open.54

A generator is fine in the garage if you leave the big door open.

The CPSC says never use a generator inside a home or garage, even if doors and windows are open.5

Do

  • Light rooms with flashlights1
  • Keep generators outdoors, 6 m (20 ft) from openings4
  • Run a battery-powered CO alarm6
  • If home is too hot or cold, go somewhere with heat or cooling, if it is safe to travel1

Don't

  • Use candles for light1
  • Heat the house with a gas oven or stove1
  • Run a generator in a garage or carport4
  • Leave a car engine running in a garage4

Know the warning signs. CO poisoning can cause headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain and confusion.4 If you suspect it, get to fresh air first, then get medical help.

For alarms, symptoms and safe heating, read Carbon Monoxide: The Blackout Killer.

#⛽ Fuel, cash and phone power

Keep phones and other electric equipment charged, and keep gas tanks full. That is Ready.gov's power outage advice, and it only works if you do it before the outage.1

Cash

Ready.gov's outage supplies start with flashlights with extra batteries for every household member, plus enough nonperishable food and water.1

Card readers and cash machines need electricity. Keep cash at home in small bills.

Phones and batteries

Find out now whether your home phone works in a power outage, and how long its battery backup lasts.1

Make a plan for batteries and other alternatives to meet your needs when the power goes out.1

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Charge everything now

    Phones, power banks, radios and flashlights with rechargeable batteries.1

  2. Stretch the battery

    Turn on low-power mode, dim the screen and close apps you do not need.

  3. Send one message out

    Tell your out-of-area contact that you are safe and when you will check in next.

  4. Charge from a car only outdoors

    Never run a car or generator in a garage or carport.4

  5. Switch off between check-ins

    Agree on fixed times to turn phones on, so the batteries last for days.

⚠️ Caution

Fuel is a fire and CO hazard

A fuel-burning generator belongs outdoors only, with its exhaust pointed away from buildings.5 For storage and safe refueling, see Generators & Fuel Storage.

#📞 Contact, neighbors and when to leave

Check on your neighbors. Ready.gov puts this on its list of things to do during an outage.1

Start with older adults and families with young children. They are especially vulnerable to extreme temperatures.1

If your home becomes too hot or too cold, and it is safe to travel, go to a place that has heat or cooling.1 Decide this early, while roads and daylight allow it.

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Use your family plan

    Follow your meeting points and contact list. No plan yet? See The Family Emergency Plan.

  2. Set check-in times

    Agree when everyone switches phones on. Write the times on paper.

  3. Listen for official updates

    Keep a battery or hand-crank radio on, so you are not relying on your phone alone.

  4. Visit, do not just call

    Knock on the doors of neighbors who live alone, use medical devices or have babies.1

  5. Keep a paper log

    Write the time of each event: power out, water pressure lost, fridge food thrown out.

👶 Babies & children

Babies and young children

Young children are especially vulnerable to extreme temperatures, so check them often.1 Give older children a job and a flashlight of their own: it keeps them busy and close to you.

🌸 Women & girls

Women and girls

Pack period products, pain relief and any pregnancy or contraceptive medicines into one bag in the first hour. Ready.gov advises a flashlight with extra batteries for every household member.1 Keep each one by its owner's bed so no one walks to the bathroom in the dark.

🐕 Dogs & pets

Dogs and pets

Fill and label a water container for each animal in the first hour. Count how many days of food you have. Keep pets out of garages and away from any running generator. See Dogs in a Long Emergency.

#🧠 Remember this

What comes first when the power goes out?

Safety: flashlights, count heads, check for CO. Then fill water.

How long is a closed fridge safe?

About 4 hours. Then throw out meat, poultry, fish, soft cheeses, milk, eggs, leftovers and deli items.

How long does a closed freezer hold?

About 48 hours if full, 24 hours if half full.

Where does a generator go?

Outdoors, at least 6 m (20 ft) from doors, windows and vents. Never in a garage, even with the door open.

How long can insulin vials go without a fridge?

Up to 28 days at 15–30°C (59–86°F). Check the label for pens.

Refrigerated medicine after more than a day without power?

Discard it unless the label says otherwise. If a life depends on it, ask a doctor or pharmacist and use it until new supply arrives.

Can you taste food to check it?

No. Never taste. When in doubt, throw it out.
