#⏱️ The countdown starts when the power stops First 72 h

A closed refrigerator keeps food safe for up to 4 hours. After 4 hours without power, throw out refrigerated meat, fish, eggs, milk and leftovers.2

A full freezer holds its temperature for about 48 hours if the door stays shut. A half-full freezer holds for about 24 hours.12

These times come from the US Department of Agriculture (USDA) and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). They only hold if the doors stay closed.1

Keep an appliance thermometer in both the fridge and the freezer. Safe readings are 4°C (40°F) or colder in the fridge and −18°C (0°F) or colder in the freezer.12

The first 30 minutes of an outage

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Write down the time

    Put it on the fridge door. Your 4-hour fridge clock and 24 to 48-hour freezer clock start now.2

  2. Keep both doors shut

    Open them only to read the thermometer or move food out. The safe times assume closed doors.113

    Tape a list of what is in the freezer to its door, so no one opens it to look.7

  3. Read both thermometers

    Write down the readings. The fridge should be 4°C (40°F) or colder and the freezer −18°C (0°F) or colder.2

  4. Pack the freezer tight

    Group frozen food together. Food packed close stays cold longer.1

  5. Get coolers and ice ready

    If the outage may last more than 4 hours, plan to move fridge perishables into a cooler with ice.1

⚠️ Caution

Two numbers, two different clocks

4 hours is how long a closed fridge stays cold enough. 2 hours is how long a perishable food may sit above 4°C (40°F).1

No thermometer? Use the 4-hour rule.2 Have a thermometer? Use the 2-hour rule on what it reads.3

💡 Tip

Freeze water now, while you have power

Freeze containers of water and gel packs ahead of time. They keep the freezer, fridge or cooler cold after the power goes.1 Full jugs also fill empty freezer space, and a full freezer holds twice as long as a half-full one.1

#🌡️ The danger zone and the 2-hour rule

Bacteria grow fastest between 4°C and 60°C (40°F and 140°F). USDA calls this range the "Danger Zone."4

In that range, bacteria can double in number in as little as 20 minutes.4

Never leave perishable food in the danger zone for more than 2 hours. If the air is above 32°C (90°F), the limit is 1 hour.56

Keep hot food at 60°C (140°F) or hotter, and cold food at 4°C (40°F) or colder. USDA lists chafing dishes, warming trays, slow cookers and ice as ways to do it.4

Why 2 hours matters: the math

At the fastest rate, one doubling every 20 minutes, 2 hours is 6 doublings.4 That turns 1 germ into 64.

  1. 0 min

    1 germ on the food

  2. 20 min

    2

  3. 40 min

    4

  4. 1 hour

    8 (the hot-weather limit above 32°C / 90°F)5

  5. 80 min

    16

  6. 100 min

    32

  7. 2 hours

    64 (the normal limit: throw out perishables)1

📊 Key fact

Hot weather cuts your time in half

Above 32°C (90°F), perishable food may sit out for only 1 hour, not 2.5 In a hot climate with no ice, plan every meal around this limit.

⚠️ Caution

Lost track of the time?

If you do not know how long food has been out of the cold, you cannot apply the 2-hour rule. Follow the official fallback: when in doubt, throw it out.2

#🧊 Stretching the cold: coolers, block ice and dry ice

If the power will be off longer than 4 hours, move fridge perishables into a cooler with ice or frozen gel packs.1 Do it before the 4 hours run out.

Keep the cooler at 4°C (40°F) or colder.6 Add ice as it melts to hold that temperature.

Pack a cooler

Step by step · 6 steps

  1. Pick the riskiest foods first

    Meat, poultry, fish, eggs, milk and leftovers. These are the foods USDA says to discard after 2 hours above 4°C (40°F).1

  2. Layer food with ice

    Use ice cubes, block ice or frozen gel packs.1

  3. Put a thermometer inside

    The target is 4°C (40°F) or colder.6

  4. Keep the lid shut

    Open it only to take food out or read the thermometer.

  5. Add ice as it melts

    Top it up before the reading climbs above 4°C (40°F).6

  6. Apply the 2-hour rule

    Perishables that sat above 4°C (40°F) for 2 hours or more go in the trash.1

Dry ice and block ice for longer outages

About 23 kg (50 lb) of dry ice should hold a full 510-liter (18-cubic-foot) freezer for 2 days.1

USDA advises getting dry ice or block ice if the power will be out for a long time.1 Find out now where you can buy it locally.

☠️ Danger

Dry ice burns skin

Never touch dry ice with bare hands.16 Use thick gloves or a towel. Keep it where children and pets cannot reach it.

🛒 Get it before you need it

Get these before you need them

  • 2 appliance thermometers: one for the fridge, one for the freezer1
  • A third thermometer for the cooler
  • At least one good cooler1
  • Gel packs and jugs to freeze water in1
⚠️ Caution

Running a fridge on a generator

A generator can keep a fridge cold, but it brings its own dangers. Read Carbon Monoxide before you start one.

#🍽️ Eat in this order

Eat the food that spoils fastest first. The American Red Cross order is: food from the refrigerator first, then food from the freezer, then non-perishable food.7

The official clocks explain why: about 4 hours for the fridge, 24 to 48 hours for the freezer.12

  1. First: hours 0–4

    Fridge perishables: meat, poultry, fish, milk, eggs, leftovers, cut fruit and cooked pasta. Cook and eat them, or move them to a cooler with ice.23

  2. Second: days 1–2

    Freezer food as it thaws. Food that still has ice crystals or is at 4°C (40°F) or colder can be refrozen or cooked.12

  3. Third

    Fridge foods the USDA chart lists as safe to keep even after 2 hours above 4°C (40°F): hard cheese, butter, whole fresh fruit, dried fruit, whole raw vegetables, bread, peanut butter and condiments.3

  4. Last

    Non-perishable pantry food: cans, dry grains, beans and other long-life stores.7 See Long-Term Food Storage and Shelf-Life Tables.

💡 Tip

Cook only what you will eat at one meal

Without a working fridge, leftovers are on the 2-hour clock (1 hour above 32°C / 90°F).5 Cooking smaller amounts means less to throw away. For safe ways to cook without power, see Cooking Without Power.

👶 Babies & children

Babies and young children

Opened baby formula is on the USDA discard list once it has been above 4°C (40°F) for more than 2 hours.3 Children under 5 are more likely to get seriously ill from food poisoning.11

Worldwide, diarrheal disease is the third leading cause of death in children aged 1 to 59 months.15

Give them the safest food first, and never the doubtful item. For feeding without power, see Feeding Babies When Formula Runs Out.

🐕 Dogs & pets

Dogs: do not feed them the trash pile

The USDA rules in this chapter are written for people, and no official rule here says thrown-out food is safe for dogs. Bag or bury discarded food so dogs cannot get it. See Feeding Dogs Without Kibble.

#✅ Keep or throw out: the USDA tables

Judge each food item separately. The official FoodSafety.gov power outage chart is built for exactly this.3

The chart's fridge rule applies to food that sat at 4°C (40°F) or above for more than 2 hours.3

When the power comes back (or at the 4-hour mark)

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Read the freezer thermometer

    At 4°C (40°F) or below, the frozen food is safe and may be refrozen.2

  2. Read the fridge thermometer

    No thermometer? Count the hours. Past 4 hours, perishables go.2

  3. Go item by item

    Use the table below. Each food gets its own decision.3

  4. Check smell, color, texture and warmth

    Anything that seems wrong or feels warm goes, even if the table says keep.3

  5. Bag the discards securely

    Keep them away from children and dogs. See Garbage & Waste Management.

Frozen food

Thawed food can be refrozen if it still has ice crystals or is at 4°C (40°F) or colder.13

If the freezer thermometer reads 4°C (40°F) or below when power returns, the food is safe and may be refrozen.12 No thermometer in the freezer? Check each package on its own.1

Frozen meat, poultry, fish and other perishables that thawed and stayed above 4°C (40°F) for more than 2 hours go in the trash.3

Any food, any time

Throw out any food with an unusual smell, color or texture, or that feels warm to the touch.313

Never taste food to decide if it is safe. When in doubt, throw it out.12

If it looks and smells fine, a small taste will tell you if it is safe.

USDA and CDC both say never taste food to decide if it is safe. When in doubt, throw it out.12

Food that has started to thaw must never be refrozen.

It is safe to refreeze if it still has ice crystals or is at 4°C (40°F) or colder.13

A closed fridge keeps food safe for a day or two.

A closed fridge keeps food safe for about 4 hours. After that, meat, fish, eggs, milk and leftovers go.214

Do

  • Write down when the power went off
  • Read the thermometers before you decide
  • Check each item on its own
  • Move perishables to a cooler before 4 hours pass
  • Throw out anything doubtful

Don't

  • Open the fridge "just to look"
  • Taste food to test it
  • Trust smell alone
  • Touch dry ice bare-handed
  • Leave cooked food out past 2 hours

#🤢 Food poisoning: symptoms and danger signs

The common symptoms are diarrhea, stomach pain or cramps, nausea, vomiting and fever.11

Food poisoning is common even with working fridges. A CDC study published in 2025 estimated that major germs in food caused about 9.9 million illnesses in the US in 2019.12

📊 Key fact

What the US numbers show (CDC study, 2025)

The same study estimated 53,300 hospital stays and 931 deaths from food eaten in the US in 2019.12 Norovirus alone caused about 5.5 million of the illnesses.12

☠️ Danger

Get medical help now for any of these

  • Bloody diarrhea11
  • Diarrhea with a fever over 38.9°C (102°F)11
  • Vomiting so often that you cannot keep liquids down11
  • Signs of dehydration11
  • Diarrhea that lasts more than 3 days11

Botulism and home-canned food are covered in Home Canning Safely. Danger signs in babies are in Sick Baby & Child: Danger Signs.

Watch for dehydration

Signs of dehydration include little or no urine, a very dry mouth and throat, and feeling dizzy when standing up.11

Replace lost fluid early. How to mix and give oral rehydration solution (ORS) is in Diarrhea, Dehydration & Oral Rehydration.

Who gets sickest

Some people are more likely to get food poisoning and to get seriously ill: adults 65 and older, children under 5, pregnant people, and people with weakened immune systems.11

Check people in these groups often for the danger signs above, and get help at the first one.11 Give them the safest food in the house.

When someone gets sick

Step by step · 4 steps

  1. Start fluids at once

    Drink plenty of fluids to prevent dehydration.11 Use ORS if you have it.15

  2. Check the danger signs

    Bloody diarrhea, diarrhea with fever over 38.9°C (102°F), can't keep liquids down, dehydration, or diarrhea past 3 days means get help.11

  3. Find and remove the suspect food

    Throw out anything else from the same batch so no one else eats it.

  4. Keep the sick person out of the kitchen

    Have someone else cook. Wash hands after every bathroom trip and cleanup; see Handwashing & Personal Hygiene.

🌸 Women & girls

Pregnancy

Pregnant people are in a higher-risk group for food poisoning.11 CDC advises a pregnant person with fever and flu-like symptoms to talk to a doctor.11 See Pregnancy Care Without a Hospital.

🧓 Elderly & disabled

Older adults

Adults 65 and older are more likely to get seriously ill from food poisoning.11 Check them often for dehydration and the other danger signs, and get help at the first one.11

#⏳ No fridge for years: the daily rules Long haul

Without power, a fridge is just an insulated box. It keeps food safe for about 4 hours after the power goes, and no longer.2

After the first days, the 2-hour rule governs every meal you cook. Perishable food may sit in the danger zone for 2 hours at most, or 1 hour above 32°C (90°F).5

Three safe choices for any cooked food

Step by step · 3 steps

  1. Eat it now

    Serve within 2 hours of cooking, or 1 hour in heat above 32°C (90°F).5

  2. Keep it hot

    Hold it at 60°C (140°F) or hotter, for example in a covered pot over low heat.4

  3. Or throw it out

    When time is up and it is not hot, discard it.1

For the long haul, your food has to be safe at room temperature from the start. These chapters cover the methods:

⏳ The 30-year view

The 30-year view

The rules in this chapter do not expire. The danger zone, the 2-hour rule and "never taste to test" apply in year 30 exactly as on day 1.45

What changes is your tools. Look after your thermometers, because they are the only way to know a temperature instead of guessing it.

🎯 Practice now

Run a cooler test this month

Fill a cooler with ice and a thermometer, then note the reading every few hours. Record how long it stays at 4°C (40°F) or colder in your climate.6 Now you know your real number, not a guess.

💡 Tip

Label cooked food with the time

Write the cooking time on the pot lid with chalk or tape. Then anyone can see when the 2-hour limit runs out.5
