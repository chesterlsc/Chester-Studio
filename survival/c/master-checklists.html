⚡ The 30-second version

  • Build in tiers: 3 days, then 2 weeks, then 1 year. The Red Cross sizes a 3-day supply for evacuation and a 2-week supply for home.2
  • Water: at least 3.8 liters (1 gallon) per person per day. That is 53 L (14 gal) each for 2 weeks, plus extra for heat, pregnancy, illness and pets.3
  • Food: plan 2,100 kcal per person per day. That humanitarian planning minimum adds up to 766,500 kcal per person per year.7
  • At least 7 days of every person's medicines go in their bag, with a written medication list.2
  • Rotate on a paper calendar. Self-filled water every 6 months, kits every year, carbon monoxide alarms every 5 years.3113

#🗺️ How the lists fit together

This chapter is the packing and shopping list for the whole book. Every list is tickable, and your ticks are saved on this device. Print the chapter and tape it inside a cupboard door.

Work from the smallest tier up. First a 3-day bag, then a 2-week home supply, then a year of storage. The Red Cross uses the first two sizes: 3 days to carry when you evacuate, 2 weeks to keep at home.2

Long outages are real. After Hurricane Maria in 2017, surveyed households in Puerto Rico averaged 84 days without power, 68 days without water and 41 days without cell service.24 Two weeks is a start, not a finish.

Quantities per person

Multiply by people, then by days. Count babies, children and elders as full people. Give each pet its own line.

One gallon a day covers all of a person's water needs.

The CDC calls 1 gallon a minimum.3 The Sphere humanitarian standard sets 15 L (about 4 gallons) per person per day for drinking, cooking and personal hygiene, and drinking and food alone take 2.5–3 L.5

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Count everyone

    Adults, children, babies and elders on one line each. Pets on separate lines.

  2. Pick the tier

    3 days, 14 days or 365 days.

  3. Multiply

    Amount per person per day × people × days. Use the table above.

  4. Write the total in pencil

    Families change. Ready.gov says to re-think your needs every year.1

  5. Tick what you have, buy the gaps

    Finish one tier before you start the next. Cheap ways to do it are in Prepping on a Budget.

#🎒 List 1: the 72-hour kit First 72 h

One bag per person, ready by the door. This list merges the Ready.gov and American Red Cross kit lists.12 Fit, weight limits and children's bags are in Go-Bags for Every Family Member.

Pack items in airtight plastic bags, in one or two easy-to-carry containers. Ready.gov also says to keep kits at home, at work and in the car.1

🧓 Elderly & disabled

Elders and disabled family members

The Red Cross lists hearing aids with extra batteries, glasses, contact lenses, syringes and a cane.2 Ready.gov adds contact lens solution and non-prescription medicines such as pain relievers, anti-diarrhea medicine, antacids and laxatives.1

Ask the medical provider now for a power outage plan for any device that runs on electricity.12 See Caring for Elderly & Disabled People.

🌸 Women & girls

Women and girls

Pack menstrual products for every girl and woman who menstruates; Ready.gov lists feminine supplies in the basic kit.1 The CDC says to store extra water for pregnant women.3 Nursing mothers need more water too.6 Reusable options are in Periods Without Stores.

#🏠 List 2: the 2-week home supply First 30 days

This is what lets you stay home safely when power and water stop. The Red Cross sizes it at 2 weeks.2 The CDC asks for at least 3 days of water, and 2 weeks if possible.3

0 / 14 done

Cash matters because payment systems need power. The April 28, 2025 blackout in Spain and Portugal knocked out ATMs, traffic lights and subways.23 Ready.gov and the Red Cross both put cash in the basic kit.12

Ration your water from day one to make it last.

FEMA and Red Cross guidance says never ration water: drink what you need today and find more for tomorrow. A normally active person needs to drink at least 2 quarts (1.9 L) a day.6

👶 Babies & children

Never ration children's food

Healthy adults can live on half their usual food for a long time. Food may be rationed safely, except for children and pregnant women.6 Plan their full share first.

⚠️ Caution

Medicines that need cold

Insulin in vials or cartridges, opened or unopened, can stay unrefrigerated at 15–30°C (59–86°F) for up to 28 days.11 For insulin pens, follow the label. For other cold medicines, Ready.gov says to discard them if the power is out more than a day, unless the label says otherwise.12

If a life depends on that medicine, ask a doctor or pharmacist and use it until a new supply arrives.12 See Medicines: Shelf Life & Expiration Facts.

Do

  • Store water at 10–21°C (50–70°F), out of sunlight3
  • Label each container "drinking water" with the date3
  • Keep boxed food in tightly closed plastic or metal containers1
  • Make sure everyone knows where the kits are1

Don't

  • Store water near gasoline or pesticides3
  • Reuse containers that held bleach or pesticides3
  • Keep the only kit in a car that might be gone
  • Hide supplies where only one person can find them

Containers, hidden water in the house and drum weights are in Storing Water for Months & Years. Fridge and freezer timings are in Food Safety Without a Refrigerator.

#📦 List 3: one year of storage Year 1

A year of storage is a bridge, not a destination. It buys time to get rain catchment, a garden and animals working. Build it slowly, from food your family already eats.

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Read the label

    Find kcal per serving and grams per serving.

  2. Work out kcal per kilogram

    kcal per serving ÷ grams per serving × 1,000.

  3. Pick the share

    Decide what part of the 766,500 kcal this food will cover, for example half.

  4. Divide

    Example: half is 383,250 kcal. A label reading 350 kcal per 100 g gives 3,500 kcal per kg, so you need about 109.5 kg (241 lb).

  5. Write it on the list

    Repeat for each food until the shares add up to 100%.

Which foods keep for years, and how to pack them, is in Long-Term Food Storage and Shelf-Life Tables. Per-person calorie targets are in Calories & Nutrition.

⏳ The 30-year view

The 30-year view

Thirty years at the minimum is 10,950 gallons (41,450 L) of water per person.3 At 2,100 kcal a day it is 22,995,000 kcal per person.7 That water alone fills about 41 cubic meters (1,464 cubic feet) for each person.

Use stored supplies to buy time while you build production. See Year 1 to Year 30, Feeding a Family Forever and Rainwater Harvesting.

☠️ Danger

The kit aspirin is not for everyone

Never give aspirin to children or teenagers. Adults should not take it if they are allergic to aspirin, have bleeding problems or take another blood-thinning medicine.10 Keep all medicines locked away from children.

🎯 Practice now

Skills before supplies

Take a first aid and CPR class while classes still run. Then empty the kit on a table and name every item. Steps are in CPR & Choking and Stopping Severe Bleeding.

#👶 List 5: the baby kit

A baby's supplies run out fastest, and most cannot be improvised safely. Ready.gov lists infant formula, bottles, diapers, wipes and diaper rash cream.1 The Red Cross adds baby food.2

0 / 9 done
👶 Babies & children

Safe water for formula

The CDC says to use bottled water for powdered formula until officials say tap water is safe. With no bottled water, boil water for 1 minute and let it cool.16

For babies under 2 months, premature babies or babies with weak immune systems, the water must still be at least 70°C (158°F) when you mix it. Then cool the bottle to body temperature before feeding.16 Boiling does not remove chemicals.16 See Feeding Babies When Formula Runs Out.

💡 Tip

Count your own baby's numbers

Count diapers and feeds for 3 days. Divide by 3 for the daily number. Multiply by 14 for 2 weeks, or by 365 for a year. Babies change sizes, so buy ahead in the next sizes. Full lists by age are in The Baby & Toddler Prep Kit.

#🐕 List 6: the dog kit

Your dog eats and drinks from the same stores you do. The CDC says to store more water when you have pets.3 Ready.gov's basic kit includes pet food and extra water for your pet.1

0 / 8 done
🐕 Dogs & pets

Measure, do not guess

Fill the bowl from a measuring jug for 3 days and write down every top-up. Weigh one day's food. Repeat in the hottest month. Those numbers × 14 and × 365 are your dog's 2-week and 1-year lines.

Temporary public shelters may not allow animals, and vet clinics and shelters scan pets for microchips.18 Give a dog medicine only when a veterinarian has told you to. See Dogs in a Long Emergency and Feeding Dogs Without Kibble.

#🛠️ List 7: tools

Tools turn stored supplies into working systems. Start with the utility and safety tools on the official lists. Then add hand tools for the long haul.

0 / 12 done
☠️ Danger

Never run a generator indoors

Not in a home or garage, even with doors and windows open. Run it outdoors more than 6 m (20 feet) from windows, doors and vents, with a battery CO alarm inside.13 See Carbon Monoxide: The Blackout Killer.

Which hand tools matter most, and how to keep them working for decades, are in The Essential Hand Tools and Sharpening & Maintaining Tools.

#📄 List 8: documents and cash

Pack copies, not originals, sealed against water. Ready.gov says to save records electronically or keep them in a waterproof, portable container.1 Keep paper too, because phones need charging.

0 / 11 done
📊 Key fact

FEMA's free document kit

FEMA and Operation HOPE publish the Emergency Financial First Aid Kit (EFFAK). It has sections for household information, advisors and health care providers, and a checklist of documents.19 If a document is missing, contact the company or agency for a copy.20

Split copies across adult bags so one lost bag does not lose everything. Paper that lasts decades is covered in Preserving Knowledge & Records. The contact card is in The Family Emergency Plan.

#🔄 The rotation schedule Long haul

A list only works if the items on it still work. Write these dates on a paper calendar, not only in a phone.

  1. Every day

    Keep the car at half a tank of fuel or more.21

  2. After any use

    Refill what you took and re-tick the list the same day.

  3. Every 6 months

    Replace water you bottled yourself.34 Practice your home escape plan, once by day and once at night.22

  4. Every year

    Re-think your needs and update every kit.1 Buy fresh bleach, because the EPA water method uses bleach less than a year old.14

  5. By the label date

    Replace store-bought water by its date.3 Replace used or out-of-date first aid items.9

  6. Every 5 years

    Replace each carbon monoxide alarm.13

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Unpack one kit at a time

    Lay everything on a table with this chapter open.

  2. Check every date

    Replace expired items.1 Write the earliest date left in the kit on its lid.

  3. Use the oldest first

    Eat stored food in normal meals before it expires. New stock goes to the back.

  4. Refit for growth and age

    New clothes sizes, new medicines, a new baby, an older dog.

  5. Re-tick the list

    Write the next check date on the calendar before you close the box.

🎯 Practice now

Test it for one weekend

Switch off the main breaker and live from List 2 for 48 hours. Note every item you wished you had. Add those to your lists.

⏳ The 30-year view

When the stores never reopen

Rotation then means using and replacing from your own production: eat stored food in daily meals and refill from the harvest. Choose items that do not expire, such as metal tools, pots and wool. Ready.gov says to keep canned food in a cool, dry place.1

What are the three storage tiers?

3 days (go-bag), 2 weeks (home), 1 year (storage).

Water per person per day?

At least 3.8 L (1 gallon). 53 L (14 gal) for 2 weeks.

Food planning figure?

2,100 kcal per person per day: 766,500 kcal a year.

How many days of medicine in each bag?

At least 7 (Red Cross).

How long can insulin vials stay out of the fridge?

Up to 28 days at 15–30°C (59–86°F).

Every 6 months.

Whose food is never rationed?

Children's and pregnant women's.

Every 5 years.

  6. FEMA and American Red Cross: Food and Water in an Emergency (n.d.). Never ration water; at least 2 quarts a day to drink; children, nursing mothers and ill people need more; healthy people can live on half their usual food; food may be rationed except for children and pregnant women.

  9. American Red Cross: Anatomy of a First Aid Kit (n.d.). Item list and quantities for a family of four; keep a kit at home and in the car; check expiration dates and replace used or out-of-date contents.

  11. FDA: Information Regarding Insulin Storage and Switching Between Products in an Emergency (n.d.). Insulin vials and cartridges, opened or unopened, may be left unrefrigerated at 59–86°F for up to 28 days.
  12. Ready.gov (FEMA): Power Outages (2025). Flashlights not candles, with batteries for everyone; batteries and charging plan; battery-backup CO detectors on every level; medical device plan; discard refrigerated medicine after more than a day unless the label says otherwise.
  13. CDC: Carbon Monoxide Poisoning Basics (2024). Battery-powered or battery-backup CO alarms, replaced every 5 years; generators outdoors more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents, never in a home or garage.
  14. EPA: Emergency Disinfection of Drinking Water (n.d.). Unscented bleach only; drops per gallon by strength; method uses bleach less than one year old.
  15. USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service: Keep Your Food Safe During Emergencies (n.d.). Appliance thermometers; 40°F and 0°F targets; frozen water and gel packs; coolers for long outages.
  16. CDC: Prepare and Store Powdered Infant Formula in an Emergency (2024). Bottled water until tap water is declared safe; boil 1 minute; water at least 70°C (158°F) for infants under 2 months, premature or immunocompromised; cool to body temperature before feeding; boiling does not remove chemicals.
  17. Ready.gov (FEMA): Prepare Your Pets for Disasters (n.d.). Include pets in the plan; microchip your pet and keep contact details current; pet kit with several days' food in an airtight, waterproof container, a water bowl and several days' supply of water, medicines, vaccination and medical records, and a picture of you with your pet.
  18. FDA: Take Care of Your Pets Before Disaster Strikes (n.d.). Temporary public shelters may not allow animals; vet clinics and shelters scan microchips.
  19. FEMA: Emergency Financial First Aid Kit, large print (FEMA P-2034) (2019). Free FEMA and Operation HOPE kit with household information, advisors and providers, and a documents checklist.
  20. Ready.gov: Emergency Financial First Aid Kit Checklists and Forms (2020). Document checklist including military ID and DD 214; contact the agency for missing copies.
  21. Ready.gov (FEMA): Evacuation (2025). Keep at least half a tank of gas at all times; fill up when evacuation seems likely.
  22. NFPA: How to Make a Home Fire Escape Plan (n.d.). Practice the plan twice a year, during the day and at night.
  23. PBS NewsHour (AP): Widespread power outage in Spain, Portugal knocks out subway networks, traffic lights, ATMs (2025). ATMs, traffic lights and subways stopped on April 28, 2025.
  24. New England Journal of Medicine: Mortality in Puerto Rico after Hurricane Maria (Kishore et al.) (2018). Survey of 3,299 households: averages of 84 days without power, 68 without water and 41 without cell service.
