#🧭 The whole check at a glance

Work in the same order every time, so you never have to stop and decide what comes next.

The chart shows the order this chapter teaches. Each step has its own section below.

#🦺 Scene safety: protect yourself first

Stop before you rush in. The American Red Cross starts every emergency the same way: check the scene for safety, form an initial impression, get consent and put on personal protective equipment (PPE) such as gloves.1

Keeping yourself safe is what lets you help others.2 If you get hurt too, your family has two patients and one less helper.

Look for anything that could hurt you

  • Fire, smoke or fumes from a stove, heater or generator
  • Walls, roofs, trees or vehicles that could fall or roll
  • Fast or deep water
  • Broken glass, sharp metal and needles
  • Blood, vomit and other body fluids: glove up first
  • A frightened or protective animal
  • An angry or violent person: back away and get help
Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Stop and look

    Scan the whole area before you step in. Is anything still dangerous?1

  2. Form a first impression

    As you walk up, notice: Is the person moving? Making sounds? Bleeding heavily? Is the skin an odd color?1

    If blood is pooling or spurting, control it first.5

  3. Ask permission

    Say your name and "I know first aid. Can I help?" Get consent before you touch them.1 For a child, ask a parent or caregiver if one is there.

  4. Protect your hands and eyes

    Put on gloves and eye protection if you have them.1

  5. Get help and gear moving

    Shout for help and send someone for the medical kit. The Red Cross says to get equipment and give care based on what you find, within your training.1

☠️ Danger

Collapsed and not breathing? Do not finish this chapter first

Check for a response and breathing for no more than 10 seconds. If the person is unresponsive and not breathing, or only gasping, that is cardiac arrest: start CPR and use an AED (automated external defibrillator) right away.319 See CPR & Choking.

Timer: Responsiveness and breathing check: 10 seconds at most. Interactive in the digital edition.

Do

  • Pause and scan before you go in
  • Ask "Can I help?" first
  • Wear gloves for any body fluid
  • Send someone for the kit

Don't

  • Run into smoke, water or a collapse zone
  • Touch blood with bare hands
  • Spend more than 10 seconds deciding about CPR3
  • Leave the person alone to fetch gear if someone else can go

#🔺 The primary survey: find the fastest killer

The primary survey is a quick first check for the most urgent problems. Fix each problem as you find it, then move to the next one.

ABC: the classic order

The Canadian Red Cross builds its first check around the ABCs: Airway, Breathing and Circulation.4

  • Airway: Is it open? Can they talk or cry?
  • Breathing: Is the chest rising and falling?
  • Circulation: Is there a pulse? Any major bleeding? What color is the skin?

MARCH: when there is heavy bleeding

Tactical Combat Casualty Care (TCCC) is the accepted standard for prehospital care on the battlefield.5 It divides care into three phases: Care Under Fire, Tactical Field Care and Tactical Evacuation Care.5

MARCH sets the order of care. While still in danger, the only treatment is control of life-threatening bleeding. Once you are safe, you work through all five letters.6

The five MARCH parts were shaped by battlefield limits such as delayed evacuation and limited supplies.7 Those are the same limits a household faces when no ambulance is coming.

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. M: Massive bleeding

    Look and sweep your gloved hands over the body for pooling or spurting blood. Stop it now: see Stopping Severe Bleeding.

  2. A: Airway

    Can they talk or cry clearly? If not, look in the mouth for anything blocking it. See CPR & Choking.

  3. R: Respiration

    Watch both sides of the chest rise. Count breaths for a full minute. Look for wounds on the chest and back.

  4. C: Circulation

    Feel the pulse, press a nail bed and look at the skin. A slow nail refill can point to shock.13

  5. H: Hypothermia and head injury

    Get them off cold ground and covered. Note how alert they are. See Hypothermia, Frostbite & Heat Stroke and Head & Spine Injuries.

First aid always starts with the airway.

It depends on the emergency. For a person who is unresponsive and not breathing, the Red Cross says to start CPR and use an AED immediately.3 The American Heart Association's 2025 guideline starts CPR with chest compressions.19 For an injured person, TCCC's MARCH puts massive bleeding before the airway.56

#💓 Vital signs with no machines

Vital signs are numbers you can count with a watch, your fingers and your eyes. Normal values change with age, sex, weight, fitness and overall health.8

So compare every reading with two things: that person's own normal, and the last reading you took.

Pulse

Step by step · 4 steps

  1. Find the pulse

    Use your index and middle fingers. Press gently with flat fingers on the inner wrist below the base of the thumb, or at the side of the neck beside the windpipe.10

    Check one side of the neck only. Pressing both sides at once can slow blood flow to the head and cause fainting.10

  2. Count for a full minute

    Use a watch or clock that shows seconds. Count every beat for 60 seconds.

  3. Judge rhythm and strength

    Write "regular" or "irregular", and "strong" or "weak".

  4. Compare

    Check the number against the age table below and against the person's own normal.10

Breathing

Watch or feel the chest. One rise and one fall is one breath. Count for a full 60 seconds.

Also note how hard they work to breathe: noisy breaths, flaring nostrils, or skin pulling in between the ribs. WHO uses fast breathing and chest indrawing as signs of pneumonia in young children.16

Timer: Count breaths or pulse: 60 seconds. Interactive in the digital edition.

⚠️ Caution

Short counts multiply your mistakes

Counting for 15 seconds and multiplying by 4 is quick, but miss just one breath and your answer is off by 4 per minute. When the number matters, count the full minute.

💡 Tip

Count breaths without moving

Keep your fingers on the wrist after you finish the pulse, and watch the chest. You can count breathing straight away without a break.

Capillary refill (the nail-bed test)

Press a fingernail until it turns white, then let go. Normal pink color should return in under 2 seconds.12

A time over 2 seconds can be a sign of dehydration, hypothermia, narrowed arteries in the limbs (peripheral vascular disease) or shock.1213

💡 Tip

Cold hands give slow results

Hypothermia is one of the listed causes of slow refill.13 If the hand is cold, warm it and test again, and test more than one finger. Remove colored nail polish first, or you cannot see the color change.12

Skin, alertness and the rest

Skin: write three words. Color (pink, pale, gray, blue, flushed), temperature (warm, cool, hot) and moisture (dry or sweaty).

Alertness: write one letter of AVPU. A = alert and talking. V = responds only to your voice. P = responds only to a painful touch. U = no response.

Temperature: if you have a thermometer, write the number and where you took it (mouth, armpit, ear or rectum).

Blood pressure: only with a cuff. The average healthy adult reads between 90/60 and 120/80 mmHg (millimeters of mercury).8

🛒 Get it before you need it

Assessment tools that need no power

  • A wind-up or mechanical watch with a second hand
  • Notebooks and pencils (several of each)
  • Disposable gloves and eye protection
  • A manual blood pressure cuff and stethoscope, if you will train to use them
  • A thermometer and a small flashlight

Full kit lists are in Building a Long-Term Medical Kit.

📊 Key fact

Children's "normal" ranges are softer than they look

A 2011 systematic review in The Lancet found that the reference ranges in common use for children were not based on evidence. It pooled 69 studies, with heart rate data from 143,346 children and breathing data from 3,881 children, to build new age charts.18

A pulse under 60 always means something is wrong.

Well-trained athletes can have a normal resting pulse of 40–60.10 That is why you record each person's own baseline while they are well.

👶 Babies & children

Babies and children

  • Count breaths when the child is calm, for a full minute, and write down if they were crying.
  • Sleeping children run slower. One school-age chart lists an awake pulse of 75–118 and a sleeping pulse of 58–90 for ages 6–11.14 Write down whether the child was awake or asleep.
  • Know the WHO fast-breathing levels: 50 or more breaths a minute at 2–11 months, 40 or more from 12 months to 5 years.1617
  • Babies under 2 months have their own danger signs. For the full lists, see Sick Baby & Child: Danger Signs.

#🗣️ SAMPLE: the questions to ask

Once the urgent problems are handled, ask questions. If the person cannot answer, ask family or bystanders, and look for medical ID tags, notes or medicine bottles.

Use the six letters of SAMPLE as a checklist. Write each answer down as you go.

The SAMPLE history: what to ask and write
LetterAskWrite down
S: Signs and symptoms"What is wrong? Where does it hurt?"What you see, and what they feel, in their words
A: Allergies"Are you allergic to any medicine, food or insect stings?"The allergy and what happened last time
M: Medicines"What do you take? When was the last dose?"Names, doses and times, including herbal products
P: Past history"Any heart, lung, diabetes, seizure or other health problems? Could you be pregnant?"Conditions, past operations, pregnancy
L: Last in and out"When did you last eat, drink and pee?"Times and amounts; for babies, last feed and last wet diaper
E: Events"What happened just before this started?"A short story of the event, with times
💡 Tip

Ask about pain the same way every time

Ask them to rate their pain from 0 (none) to 10 (the worst they can imagine). Write the number and the time. Repeat the same question later so you can compare like with like.

🌸 Women & girls

Women and girls

Ask about a possible pregnancy and the date of the last period in private, without family listening. Explain each step before you touch, offer a woman helper if one is there, and keep the person covered as much as you can.

#🔍 The head-to-toe check

Look, ask, then gently feel, working from the head down. Compare the left side with the right side as you go.

Do this after the primary survey, and only once any heavy bleeding is controlled.5 Tell the person what you are about to do before every step.

Step by step · 7 steps

  1. Head and face

    Look for cuts, bumps, bruises, and blood or clear fluid from the ears or nose. Look at both pupils: are they the same size?

  2. Neck

    Look and feel for wounds and swelling. If they fell, were hit or have neck pain, keep the head still and read Head & Spine Injuries.

  3. Chest

    Watch both sides rise together. Press gently on the ribs and ask about pain.

  4. Belly

    Press gently in each of the four quarters. Note pain, hardness or swelling.

  5. Hips

    Look for wounds and ask about pain. If they fell or were crushed, do not rock or squeeze the hips: keep them still.

  6. Legs and arms

    Check each limb for wounds, swelling or odd shape. Ask them to wiggle fingers and toes, and test feeling and nail refill on each side.

  7. Back

    Slide a gloved hand under the back where you can, then look at your glove for blood.

🧓 Elderly & disabled

Older and disabled people

Normal vital signs change with age and overall health.8 Ask a caregiver or family member what is normal for this person: their usual alertness, speech, mobility and pulse. Make sure hearing aids and glasses are in place before you judge confusion.

🐕 Dogs & pets

Dogs

A dog guarding a hurt owner can stop you helping. Ask someone the dog knows to hold it away while you work. Dogs have their own normal ranges and checks: see Dog First Aid.

#📒 Written patient notes

Write everything down, with the time. A written record shows whether the person is getting better or worse, and lets another caregiver take over without starting again.

Keep one page per patient. Start a new line every time you recheck.

What goes on a patient note
SectionWhat to write
WhoName, age, rough weight, date
What happenedThe SAMPLE answers, in short lines
Vital signsTime, pulse, breaths, nail refill, skin, AVPU, temperature if known
FindingsWhat the head-to-toe check found, side by side
Care givenEvery treatment and medicine, with dose and time
ChangesBetter, worse or the same, compared with the last check
Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Write the time first

    Every line starts with the clock time. Numbers without times cannot show a trend.

  2. Write what you counted, not what you guessed

    "Pulse 112, regular, weak" beats "fast pulse".

  3. Use their words for symptoms

    Write "crushing pain in chest" in quotes, exactly as they said it.

  4. Recheck and add a new line

    Repeat the vital signs and write them under the last set, so the change is easy to see.

  5. Keep the note with the patient

    If they move or a new caregiver takes over, the note goes with them.

⏳ The 30-year view

The 30-year view

Battlefield care was shaped around delayed evacuation and limited supplies.7 In a long grid-down life, your notebook becomes the only medical record. Keep a binder with one section per family member: allergies, medicines, past illnesses, baseline vital signs and every illness or injury.

🎯 Practice now

Record everyone's baseline now

Vital signs vary with age, sex, weight, fitness and health.8 Once a year, sit each family member down at rest. Count pulse and breathing for a full minute, test nail refill, and write the numbers with the date. A sick person's numbers mean far more when you know their healthy ones.

🎯 Practice now

Get hands-on training

The Red Cross teaches that you should give care based on what you find, within your level of training.1 Take a first aid and CPR course while classes still exist, and practice this whole chapter on each other.

#🧠 Remember this

What do you check before you touch anyone?

That the scene is safe for you. Then get consent and put on gloves.

How long do you spend checking a collapsed person for a response?

No more than 10 seconds. Not breathing or only gasping: start CPR.

What does MARCH stand for?

Massive bleeding, Airway, Respiration, Circulation, Hypothermia (and head injury).

Normal adult resting pulse and breathing?

Pulse 60–100. Breathing 12–20 a minute.

Normal nail refill time?

Under 2 seconds.

WHO fast breathing for a baby aged 2–11 months?

50 or more breaths a minute.

What does SAMPLE stand for?

Signs and symptoms, Allergies, Medicines, Past history, Last in and out, Events.

What goes next to every number in your notes?

The time.
