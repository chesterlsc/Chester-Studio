#♻️ Three questions before you reuse anything Year 1

Over 30 years, worn-out and salvaged things become your raw material. Repurposing works when the right material goes to the right job. It fails when the wrong material ends up next to food, water, fire or a child.

Ask three questions every time, in this order.

  • What is it made of? Plastic (find the code), glass, wood, metal, or something you cannot name.
  • What did it hold or touch? Food, water, fuel, chemicals, pesticides or floodwater.
  • What will it touch next? Drinking water, food, fire, skin, children or dogs.

The answers decide the job. The CDC rule for drinking water is short: use food-grade containers, and never use containers that held toxic chemicals such as bleach or pesticides.1

Plastic picks up what sits near it. University of Nebraska–Lincoln (UNL) Extension warns that fuel and pesticide vapors penetrate plastic.9 The CDC says to store water containers away from gasoline and pesticides.1

☠️ Danger

Chemical containers never hold food or water

The CDC says not to use containers that held toxic chemicals for drinking water.1 Boiling does not remove fuel or toxic chemicals from water.5

Mark these containers NOT FOR FOOD and give them a non-food job, or leave them.

⚠️ Caution

Salvage lawfully

Repurpose what you own, what you are given, or what you have clear permission to take. Taking other people's property is theft. Looting is a crime, and many places punish it more harshly during a declared emergency.

The law and the ethics are in Scavenging vs Looting.

#🧴 Plastics: read the code, then clean it

Turn the container over and find the number inside the recycling triangle. Utah's Division of Emergency Management says to look for the 2. Food-grade high-density polyethylene (HDPE #2) is its best choice for stored water.2

Oregon State University Extension also accepts heavy-duty, food-grade containers stamped PETE (#1, polyethylene terephthalate) or HDPE.3

The number alone is not enough. The CDC asks for food-grade containers.1 A #2 bucket that once held paint or pool chemicals is still a chemical container.

A well-rinsed milk jug is fine for storing water.

Ready.gov says milk and fruit juice containers keep protein and sugar residue that lets bacteria grow.4 Utah's guide adds that milk jugs break down and become brittle.2

Sanitize a reused food-grade container

This is the CDC method. You need soap, water and plain, unscented liquid chlorine bleach.1

Step by step · 6 steps

  1. Wash and rinse

    Wash the container with soap and water. Rinse it completely.1

  2. Mix the sanitizer

    Stir 1 teaspoon (5 mL) of unscented liquid chlorine bleach into 1 quart (about 1 liter) of water.1

  3. Coat every surface

    Pour it in, cap it and shake so the solution touches every inside surface.1

  4. Wait, then empty

    Wait at least 30 seconds, then pour the solution out.1

  5. Fill and cap

    Fill with safe water and close it tightly. Ready.gov's version of these steps is written for chlorinated tap water.14

  6. Label it

    Write "drinking water" and the date on it.1

💡 Tip

Write the job on the container

Use a permanent marker: WATER, FOOD or NOT FOR FOOD. For water, the CDC asks at least for "drinking water" and the date.1 A clear label helps a tired family member grab the right jug.

#🛢️ Drums, buckets and rain barrels

A clean, food-grade HDPE drum becomes a water barrel. Utah's guidance names food-grade HDPE #2 as the best plastic for stored water.2

Place it before you fill it. Water weighs 1 kg per liter (8.33 lb per gallon).8 So a full 208 L (55-gallon) drum weighs about 208 kg (458 lb). UNL Extension advises sturdy shelving for stored water because of its weight.9

Raise it off concrete and keep it out of the sun. Be Ready Utah says to raise containers off concrete for air flow. It also says blue barrels in direct sun can start to fall apart within 5 years.7 Utah State University Extension adds that plastic on concrete or dirt absorbs odors.11

Give it a lid and a tap. CDC safe-storage guidance prefers a container with a small opening, a lid and a spigot (tap). Wide-mouth vessels let hands, cups and dippers contaminate the water.12 The CDC also says to keep treated water in clean, covered containers.13

Turn a food-grade drum or bucket into a water station

Step by step · 7 steps

  1. Check its history

    It must be food-grade and must never have held chemicals, fuel or pesticides.1

  2. Wash and sanitize

    Use the CDC steps in the plastics section above.1

  3. Fit a lid and a tap

    Keep the lid on. Draw water from the tap so no hand or cup goes inside.12

  4. Set it up high and dry

    Put it on a pallet or boards, in shade, away from fuel and pesticides.71

  5. Fill with safe water

    For a full 208 L (55-gallon) drum, Oregon State gives 2 tablespoons (30 mL) of unscented household bleach.3 For other amounts, see Disinfecting Water.

  6. Leave room to freeze

    Where it can freeze, leave 2–3 inches (5–8 cm) of headspace.9

  7. Label and rotate

    Write "drinking water" and the date. Replace water you stored yourself every 6 months.1

Rain barrels

Rainwater must be treated before you drink it. The CDC lists rainwater among the outdoor sources that need treatment.5 Utah State University Extension says the same.11

A rain barrel is a food-grade drum under a downspout. Roof math, gutters, first-flush diverters, screens and mosquito control are in Rainwater Harvesting.

⚠️ Caution

Drum stoves and burn barrels

This chapter gives no plans for drum stoves or burn barrels. For heat, see Heating With Wood. Never cut, weld or heat a drum unless you know it never held fuel or chemicals.

If anything burns indoors, fit a battery-powered carbon monoxide (CO) alarm. The CDC says to replace CO alarms every 5 years.16 See Carbon Monoxide.

👶 Babies & children

Lids on every bucket and barrel

Drowning can happen quickly in less than 1 inch (2.5 cm) of water. Never leave a water container open and unattended.6

The US Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) counted 434 in-home drowning deaths of children under 5 in 2006–2010. 82% were under age 2. Its warning names buckets as well as bathtubs.15

🧓 Elderly & disabled

Size containers for the person lifting

A 19 L (5-gallon) jug of water weighs about 19 kg (42 lb).8 For older or disabled people, use smaller jugs, or a drum with a tap so nobody has to lift it.

Pallets

Use pallets and boards to lift water containers off concrete. Be Ready Utah and Utah State University Extension both advise it.711 Before you burn or build with any salvaged wood, read Salvage Hazards.

Bicycles

Keep bicycles whole and rideable. Do not strip a working bike for parts. Repairs, spare parts, trailers and handcarts are in Bicycles, Carts & Moving Loads.

Tires and car parts

Check the vehicle hazards first. Tires, wire, mirrors and seat fabric, plus battery acid and lead safety, are in Useful Parts From Vehicles.

Glass

Do not store water in glass where it can freeze. UNL Extension advises against glass for stored water. It says to leave 2–3 inches (5–8 cm) of headspace in containers that may freeze.9

Glass jars for dry foods are covered in Mylar, Buckets, Oxygen Absorbers & Pests. Handle broken glass with thick gloves and sweep it up at once.

Metal

Know the metal before it touches food or fire. Zinc-coated (galvanized) metal and its fumes are covered in Basic Blacksmithing & Metal Repair. Old paint is covered in Salvage Hazards.

#☠️ The red list: what not to reuse

When in doubt, keep it out of food, water and fire. Every rule below comes from the guidance cited in this chapter.

Do

  • Reuse food-grade #2 HDPE and #1 PETE for water23
  • Sanitize before the first fill1
  • Store water away from gasoline and pesticides1
  • Wear rubber boots, gloves and goggles for flood-soaked salvage10
  • Disinfect flood-soaked toys before children use them10

Don't

  • Put water in a container that held bleach, pesticides or other toxic chemicals1
  • Store water in milk or juice jugs4
  • Trust boiling to remove fuel or chemicals5
  • Store water in glass where it can freeze9
  • Leave plastic water containers in direct sun7

Boiling or bleach will make water from a fuel or chemical drum safe.

The CDC says not to use water that may hold fuel or toxic chemicals, because boiling will not remove them.5 Its infant-feeding guidance adds that chemical contamination cannot be removed by boiling, disinfecting or filtering.20

Floods move chemical containers. The CDC warns about this in its floodwater guidance.10 Treat any container that sat in floodwater as unknown until you know what it held.

The CDC also says to wash flood-soaked clothes in hot water with detergent.10 Do that before cloth goes to rags, bedding or patches.

Materials to identify first

Do not sand, cut, burn or cook on these until you have read the chapter named.

  • Old painted wood or metal: lead paint, in Salvage Hazards
  • Building materials and insulation: asbestos, also in Salvage Hazards
  • Zinc-coated (galvanized) metal: zinc fumes, in Basic Blacksmithing & Metal Repair
  • Car batteries: acid and lead, in Useful Parts From Vehicles
  • Wood of unknown treatment: do not burn it, and do not build food beds or animal feeders from it
🐕 Dogs & pets

Dogs and salvage

Keep salvaged chemical containers capped and out of a dog's reach. Disinfect anything that sat in floodwater before it becomes a dog bowl or toy, the same way the CDC advises for children's toys.10

#🧰 Organizing a salvage workshop

Run every item through the same line. A fixed path keeps unknown and dirty things apart from food, water and children.

Step by step · 6 steps

  1. Quarantine

    Bring salvage to an outdoor holding spot first. Wear rubber boots, gloves and goggles for anything flood-soaked.10

  2. Identify

    Find the resin code, the old label or the material. Ask what it held. If nobody knows, it is non-food.

  3. Clean

    Wash everything. Sanitize anything headed for food or water with the CDC method.1

  4. Sort

    Green bin: food and water. Yellow bin: non-food jobs. Red bin: marked NOT FOR FOOD, or discarded.

  5. Label and log

    Write the job and the date on the item. Record it in a paper notebook so the group knows what exists.

  6. Store and share

    Keep food and water containers away from fuel and pesticides.1 Share surplus through your group; see What to Salvage First.

Workshop safety rules

Keep generators outside. Never run one in a home, garage or carport. Place it at least 20 feet (6 m) from doors, windows and vents.17 The CPSC says never indoors, even with doors and windows open.18

Fit a battery-powered CO alarm anywhere a fire, stove or engine runs near people. Replace it every 5 years.16

Plan two ways out. The National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) home escape plan calls for two ways out.19 Keep both clear of stacked salvage.

🎯 Practice now

Practice this now

While hardware stores are open, sanitize one food-grade bucket and fit it with a tap. Then spend 10 minutes sorting your garage into green, yellow and red piles.

🛒 Get it before you need it

Get this before you need it

  • Food-grade buckets with tight lids, and drum taps (spigots)
  • A bung wrench for drum caps
  • Permanent markers and tape for labels
  • Rubber boots, heavy gloves and safety goggles
  • Battery-powered CO alarms, with spare batteries
  • Plain, unscented liquid chlorine bleach

#⏳ Keeping plastic useful for 30 years Long haul

Shade protects plastic. Be Ready Utah says light makes containers brittle and grows algae in stored water.7

Every container you protect is one you do not have to replace. Keep plastic out of the sun, off bare concrete and away from fuel.71

  1. Day 0

    Sanitize, fill and label with "drinking water" and the date.1

  2. Month 6

    Replace water you stored yourself.114 Check each empty container for cracks.

  3. Every year

    Look for brittle plastic and algae. Light causes both.7 Move brittle containers to non-food work.

  4. Year 5

    Blue barrels kept in direct sun can start to fall apart.7 Replace battery CO alarms.16

  5. Years 5–30

    Down-cycle: water container, then dry non-food storage, then parts bin. Re-label it at every step.

⏳ The 30-year view

The 30-year view

Your stock of food-grade containers is limited, so guard it. Never let a food-grade drum be borrowed for fuel, paint or pesticide. Once it holds a chemical, the CDC rule takes it out of food and water use for good.1

#🧠 Remember this

Which plastic code is best for stored water?

#2 HDPE, food-grade. Heavy-duty, food-grade #1 PETE is also accepted.

Can a rinsed milk jug store water?

No. Residue grows bacteria and the jug turns brittle.

Can boiling fix water from a chemical drum?

No. Boiling does not remove fuel or toxic chemicals.

How do you sanitize a container?

1 tsp (5 mL) unscented bleach per quart (1 L) of water. Shake to coat, wait 30 seconds, pour out.

How heavy is a full 55-gallon drum?

About 208 kg (458 lb). Place it before you fill it.

Why a tap instead of a dipper?

Hands, cups and dippers contaminate water in wide-mouth containers.

How much water can drown a toddler?

Less than 1 inch (2.5 cm). Lid every bucket.
