#📋 The priority list: what to look for first First 30 days

Salvage in the order that things kill. Records from long outages point to three big killers: no safe water, missed medical care and cold.12

After Hurricane Maria in 2017, a survey of 3,299 Puerto Rico households found they averaged 68 days without water and 84 days without power (NEJM, 2018).1 About one third of excess deaths were tied to delayed or interrupted health care.1

Texas linked 246 deaths to the February 2021 winter storm. About two-thirds were from cold exposure, mostly hypothermia.2

After Hurricane Helene in 2024, Asheville, North Carolina, went through a 53-day water crisis before its boil-water notice was lifted.3

So this book ranks salvage in that order: hold water, treat injury and illness, stay warm. Light, tools and materials come next. The ranking is guidance built from those records.

#🪣 Water containers: the number-one find

A clean container is worth more than the water in it. Rain, streams and wells can refill it again and again. Without one, you cannot carry, treat or store water.

Use only food-grade containers. The CDC says never to use a container that held toxic substances such as bleach or pesticides.4

Look for the number 2 inside the recycling triangle. Utah's emergency management agency calls food-grade high-density polyethylene (HDPE #2) the best choice.5 It also says milk jugs break down and become brittle.5

A lid and a tap beat an open bucket. CDC safe-storage guidance favors a small opening, a lid and a tap or spout. Hands, cups and dippers carry germs into wide-open containers.10

How many containers?

The Sphere humanitarian standard is 15 L (about 4 gallons) per person per day for drinking, cooking and hygiene.9 For a family of 4, that is 60 L (about 16 gallons) every day.

Arithmetic: two 20 L (5.3 gallon) jugs per person hold 40 L. That is about 2.5 days at the Sphere amount. Aim for enough containers to carry today's water and store tomorrow's.

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Ask what it held

    Read the label and smell it. Reject anything that held bleach, pesticides, fuel or other chemicals.4

  2. Check the plastic

    Look for HDPE #2.5 Reject milk jugs and anything brittle or cracked. Light makes containers brittle and leaky.6

  3. Wash and rinse

    Wash with soap and water, then rinse well.4

  4. Sanitize

    Stir 1 teaspoon (5 mL) of unscented liquid bleach into 1 quart (about 1 L) of water. Pour it in, cap, shake to coat every surface, wait at least 30 seconds, then pour it out.4

  5. Fill and label

    Fill with safe water. Write "drinking water" and the date on it.4

☠️ Danger

Fumes pass through plastic

Nebraska Extension warns that vapors from gasoline, kerosene and pesticides can penetrate plastic containers.7 Treat a jug found sitting beside fuel or pesticides as a non-drinking container. Store your water away from them.4

👶 Babies & children

Lids on, always, around toddlers

Drowning can happen quickly in less than 2.5 cm (1 inch) of water. Never leave a bucket or any container of water unattended.24 Salvaged buckets need tight lids before they come home.

Any big plastic jug or drum is fine for drinking water.

Only food-grade containers are safe, and never one that held bleach or pesticides.4 Milk jugs break down and turn brittle.5 Full storage rules are in Storing Water.

#🩹 Medical and hygiene supplies

Use the American Red Cross first aid list as your salvage list. It is written for a family of 4.12

  • Dressings: 2 absorbent compress dressings, 25 adhesive bandages, 10 sterile gauze pads, 2 roller bandages and 2 triangular bandages12
  • Tape and protection: 1 roll of adhesive cloth tape, 2 pairs of nonlatex gloves and a breathing barrier12
  • Ointments and wipes: antibiotic ointment, antiseptic wipes, hydrocortisone ointment and 81 mg aspirin12
  • Tools: tweezers, an emergency blanket, an instant cold compress, a non-mercury nonglass thermometer and an instruction booklet12

Most of the list is dressings and protection. Only four items are medicines or antiseptics.12 Sealed bandages, gauze and gloves are light, compact and worth taking in bulk. See Building a Long-Term Medical Kit.

Hygiene supplies prevent illness. Ready.gov lists soap, hand sanitizer, moist towelettes, garbage bags and plastic ties in its basic kit.13

Found medicines: read the storage line first

Insulin: the FDA says vials, opened or unopened, may be kept unrefrigerated at 15–30°C (59–86°F) for up to 28 days. Replace insulin that was exposed to extreme heat or cold.15

Other refrigerated medicines: Ready.gov says to throw them out if the power has been out for more than a day, unless the label says otherwise.16

The safety rules for found medicines, including expiration dates, are in Found Food & Medicine.

⚠️ Caution

Prescription medicines belong to one patient

A prescription was written for one person's body and illness. Before anyone takes a found prescription medicine, ask a doctor, nurse or pharmacist whenever one can be reached. Antibiotic facts are in Infections, Sepsis & Antibiotic Facts.

👶 Babies & children

Babies and children

Never give aspirin to children.17 Keep salvaged aspirin with adult supplies only.

Ready.gov's baby items are infant formula, bottles, diapers, wipes and diaper rash cream.13 Put them at the top of the list for any household with a baby.

🧓 Elderly & disabled

Elderly and disabled people

The Red Cross lists hearing aids with extra batteries, glasses, contact lenses, syringes and a cane.14 These are small, light and very hard to replace.

In the US, more than 3 million Medicare patients have claims for electricity-dependent medical equipment (HHS emPOWER).25 Batteries for that equipment are a top find.

#🔧 Tools, fasteners, fabric, glass and batteries

Hand tools

Take tools that need no power. Ready.gov lists a wrench or pliers to turn off utilities and a manual can opener.13 The Red Cross adds a multi-purpose tool.14

Saws, hammers, axes, shovels and files let one person do far more work. Choosing and caring for them is in The Essential Hand Tools.

Fasteners, tape and sheeting

Nails, screws, bolts, wire and hinges are small and light. Sort them by size into lidded jars or tins so you can find them in the dark.

Ready.gov lists plastic sheeting, scissors and duct tape in its basic kit for sheltering in place.13 Sheeting and tape also patch broken windows and leaking roofs until a proper repair.

Fabric and clothing

Blankets and sleeping bags come first. Ready.gov lists a sleeping bag or warm blanket, a complete change of clothes and sturdy shoes for each person.13 The Red Cross first aid list includes an emergency blanket.12

Wash anything that touched floodwater. CDC says to wash clothes that touched floodwater or sewage in hot water and detergent. Disinfect toys before children use them.19

Clean cotton cloth is the raw material for reusable pads and cloth diapers. See Periods Without Stores.

Glass

Glass jars and bottles store dry food, seeds and liquids. Do not use glass for water that may freeze. Use freezer-safe plastic instead.7

The Red Cross specifies a non-mercury, nonglass thermometer for first aid kits.12 Handle old glass thermometers and broken glass as hazards: see Salvage Hazards.

Batteries and light

Light the house with flashlights, not candles. Ready.gov lists candles as a fire risk in power outages.16 Ready.gov also lists extra batteries in its basic kit.13

Battery CO alarms are high-value finds. The CDC advises battery-powered or battery-backup carbon monoxide (CO) alarms, replaced every 5 years.22 Look for a date on the alarm before you trust it.

Car batteries and damaged batteries need special handling. See Salvage Hazards.

A generator is the best thing you can salvage.

Fuel pumps do not work without electricity (FEMA, 2025).20 A generator must run outdoors, more than 6 m (20 feet) from windows, doors and vents, and never in a home or garage.22 See Carbon Monoxide.

🌸 Women & girls

Women and girls

Ready.gov lists feminine supplies and personal hygiene items in its basic kit.13 Sealed pads, tampons and menstrual cups are light and high value. The CDC says pregnant women need more stored water, so containers matter even more.4

Salvage in pairs, never alone. See Personal Safety for Women & Girls.

#🏚️ Where to look, with permission

Start with your own home. Walk it room by room before you go anywhere else. List what you already own before you ask anyone else.

Then ask. This chapter covers lawful salvage only: your own things, things people give or trade you, and items covered by a community agreement. Taking from homes, stores or vehicles without permission is theft, and looting is a crime. The law is in Scavenging vs Looting.

Where to look, and what to watch for
PlaceLook forWatch for
HomesFirst aid kits, blankets, containers, water heater water11Fridge food is unsafe after 4 hours without power18
Garages and shedsHand tools, fasteners, tarps, buckets, cartsFuel and pesticide vapors penetrate plastic7
FarmsDrums, tanks, fencing wire, feed bucketsAny container that held pesticides is not for drinking water4
Hardware and farm storesFasteners, tools, sheeting, containersStock belongs to the business: agree terms with the owner

Flooded buildingsMetal tools, glass, sealed itemsFloodwater can carry sewage and move chemical containers19

Vehicle parts have their own chapter: Useful Parts From Vehicles. Ready.gov advises keeping a kit in the car, so ask the owner about the trunk first.13

Step by step · 6 steps

  1. Get permission

    Write down the owner's name, the date and what they agreed to. If no one can give permission, follow your community's agreement.

  2. Go in pairs

    Tell someone where you are going and when you will be back.

  3. Gear up

    Work gloves, a dust mask and sturdy shoes.13 In flood-damaged places add rubber boots, rubber gloves and goggles.19

  4. Check before you enter

    Look for structural damage, gas smells and spilled chemicals. If in doubt, stay out. See Salvage Hazards.

  5. Work the list from the top

    Water containers, then medical supplies, then warmth. Stop when your load limit is reached.

  6. Log it

    Record each item, the place and who allowed it, before it joins the group store.

🐕 Dogs & pets

Dogs

Leave dogs at home or on a short leash during salvage. Floodwater can carry sewage and chemicals.19

Worth finding for your dog: collar, leash, ID tag, food, carrier and bowl (Red Cross).14 Ready.gov adds extra water for your pet, so count the dog when you count containers.13

#⏳ What spoils fast and what lasts

Time decides value. Some finds are unsafe within hours. Others keep their value for years if you store them well.

Food in a dead fridge is the fastest to go. USDA says a fridge keeps food safe for about 4 hours if the door stays closed. A full freezer holds about 48 hours, a half-full one about 24.18 Days later, that food is not a salvage find.

Bleach weakens with age. EPA's emergency method calls for bleach stored at room temperature for less than one year.21 Use a found bottle with no date for cleaning, not for treating drinking water.

Plastic weakens in light. Be Ready Utah says sunlight, and even artificial light, makes containers brittle or leaky.6 A barrel that sat in a sunny yard is a lower-value find than one from a dark shed.

Short-lived: use now or leave

  • Food from a fridge or freezer without power18
  • Refrigerated medicines kept warm16
  • Undated bleach21
  • Milk jugs5

Keeps its value if stored well

  • Metal hand tools, kept dry and oiled
  • Glass jars and bottles
  • Fasteners in sealed tins
  • HDPE containers kept out of light6
  • Wool and cotton kept dry and away from pests
⏳ The 30-year view

The 30-year view

Even good finds wear out. Containers in sun can start to fall apart within 5 years.6 CO alarms need replacing every 5 years.22 Stored bleach is a first-year tool.21

Plan salvage as a cycle, not a one-time haul. Log a replace-by date for every item that wears out.

#⚖️ Weight vs value: carry it or leave it?

Water is the heaviest thing you will move. One liter weighs 1 kg (2.2 lb). One US gallon weighs 8.33 lb (3.8 kg).8

A 2018 study in Military Medicine notes that loads of 30% of body mass or less are recommended to avoid drops in performance.23 Those were fit soldiers. Treat 30% as a ceiling, not a target.

The 30% ceiling, and how much water that equals (arithmetic)
Body weight30% ceilingEquals this much water
50 kg (110 lb)15 kg (33 lb)15 L (4.0 gal)
70 kg (154 lb)21 kg (46 lb)21 L (5.5 gal)
90 kg (198 lb)27 kg (60 lb)27 L (7.1 gal)

Do the family math. At the Sphere amount of 15 L per person per day, a family of 4 needs 60 L (60 kg) of water a day.98 A 70 kg adult at the 30% ceiling carries 21 kg.23 That is 3 full loads every day. Carts and wheelbarrows are high-value finds. See Bicycles, Carts & Moving Loads.

Value per kilogram matters too. A pack of gauze, a pair of glasses or a box of batteries weighs little and is hard to replace. A heavy item you already own one of adds little.

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Is it ours to take?

    No permission, no agreement: leave it.

  2. Is it safe to handle?

    Chemicals, fuel, flood soaking or damaged batteries: leave it and mark the spot.

  3. Does it meet a top-4 need?

    Water, medical, warmth or safe light: take it first.

  4. Will it spoil before we use it?

    If yes, take only what you will use now. Check the chart above.

  5. Can we carry it safely?

    If it pushes anyone past 30% of body weight, come back with a cart or a second person.23

Do

  • Carry containers empty and fill them close to where you use the water
  • Use carts, wagons and wheelbarrows
  • Split heavy loads between people
  • Mark heavy finds and come back

Don't

  • Walk long distances with full glass jars
  • Give children adult loads
  • Carry fuel or chemicals home in food containers
  • Take more than you can store dry

#📒 Inventory and sharing Year 1

If it is not written down, you do not have it. A log stops double counting, shows what is running out, and proves where each item came from.

The Red Cross says to check first aid kit expiration dates and replace anything used or out of date.12 Ready.gov says to replace expired items and re-think your needs every year.13 The CDC says to label stored water with the date.4

Example salvage log (one line per item)
Item and countFrom, and who allowed itCheck or replace by
2 HDPE #2 jugs, 20 LNeighbor's garage; owner, 3 MayRefill water every 6 months4
1 bottle liquid bleach, datedOwn home1 year from the date21
1 battery CO alarmGroup store, by agreement5 years from its date22
  1. Every day

    Check lids, water levels and that every log entry has a place.

  2. Every 6 months

    Replace water you bottled yourself.4

  3. Every year

    Replace bleach that is a year old.21 Re-think the group's needs and replace expired items.13

  4. Every 5 years

    Replace CO alarms.22 Inspect barrels and jugs for brittleness.6

Sharing by need

Share water by need, drinking first. Sphere puts drinking and food at 2.5–3 L per person per day, within basic survival needs of 7.5–15 L.9 The CDC says pregnant women, sick people and hot climates need more.4

Share heavy tools, not just food. A group can keep one set of saws, ladders and carts with a sign-out sheet. That leaves more carrying capacity for water and medical supplies.

Write the sharing rules before you need them. See Organizing Neighbors. Turning leftovers into tools is in Repurposing Junk.
