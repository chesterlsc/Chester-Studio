#🧭 The official rule: plan for both, then follow orders

Plan for staying and for leaving, before anything happens. An earlier Ready.gov page (archived 2008) called stay-or-go the first important decision in an emergency, and told you to plan for both.8

Stay inside when the outside is the danger. Officials recommend sheltering in place when there is little time to react and being outside would be more dangerous.6

Leave when the hazard will reach your home. For hurricanes, a National Hurricane Center (NHC) official put it simply: "We evacuate from the water, not the wind, and we have evacuation zones."7

Orders come first

If officials order you to shelter in place, act immediately.6 Do not finish chores or go outside to look.

If you are told to evacuate, follow the recommended routes. Do not take shortcuts. They may be blocked.1

Leave early enough that severe weather cannot trap you. Plan now how you will leave and where you will go.1

💡 Tip

Find your zone today

Hurricane evacuations are ordered by zone.7 Look up the zones for your home, work and school while the internet still works. Write them on your paper plan. See The Family Emergency Plan.

⏳ The 30-year view

The 30-year view

If no official orders reach you, use the same tests officials use: get away from rising water, and get inside when the air outside is the danger.36

A safe, stocked home with water, food and a garden is a strong reason to stay. See Preparing Your Home for a Long Outage.

#🌊 When leaving is the right call

In hurricanes, water kills more people than wind. The NHC says storm surge and large waves pose the greatest threat to life and property along the coast.3

An NHC study of US storms from 1963 to 2012 found that 49% of deaths, nearly half, were blamed on storm-surge flooding.9

Inland, flooding is the major threat from tropical storms.3 See Floods.

#🏠 When staying is the right call: shelter in place

Shelter in place means you stay inside, in the safest room, until officials say it is safe to go out.4 It is the right move when being outside is more dangerous than being inside.6

Chemicals: go up. Radiation: go down or to the middle. Ready.gov guidance sends you above ground for a chemical release, but below ground for a radiological one.104

Radiation: get inside, stay inside, stay tuned

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Get inside now

    This is the first of the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) three rules: get inside, stay inside, stay tuned.4

  2. Go to the basement or the middle

    Get to the middle of the building or a basement, away from doors and windows.4

  3. Shut out the outside air

    Close and lock windows and doors. Turn off fans, air conditioners and forced-air heaters that pull in outside air.4

  4. Stay tuned

    Listen for updated instructions from officials.4 Without grid power, that means a battery or hand-crank radio.

  5. Stay until officials say it is safe

    Sheltering usually lasts at least 24 hours.13 Keep everyone inside, pets included.12

Chemical cloud: an interior room above ground, sealed

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Bring everyone in, pets too

    Pets should not go outside during a chemical emergency.12

  2. Shut out the outside air

    Close and lock windows and outside doors. Close the fireplace damper. Turn off fans, heating and air conditioning.12

  3. Go to an interior room above ground

    Take everyone into an interior room with no or few windows and shut the door.12 For a chemical cloud, shelter above ground, not in the basement.10

  4. Seal the room

    Seal windows, doors and air vents with plastic sheeting and duct tape.11 Ready.gov cites studies showing that sealing a room with common materials makes it safer against a chemical plume.10

  5. Listen and wait

    Keep the radio on. Leave the room only when officials say the air outside is safe.

🐕 Dogs & pets

Dogs and pets come inside with you

Pets should not go outside during a chemical or radiation emergency. Bring them into the shelter room.12 Keep a leash, a water bowl and waste bags in that room. See Dogs in a Long Emergency.

☠️ Danger

No generator near your shelter room

After Katrina and Rita, most generator CO poisonings involved generators placed outside but near homes.5 Outside is not enough. See Carbon Monoxide: The Blackout Killer.

🛒 Get it before you need it

Shelter-in-place kit

Plastic sheeting and duct tape for sealing a room.11 Cut the sheets to fit your chosen room's windows, door and vents now, and label each one. Add a battery or hand-crank radio so you can stay tuned.4

#🚗 What really happened on the road: Hurricane Rita, 2005

In September 2005, nearly 3.7 million people left the Texas coast in the days before Hurricane Rita made landfall.2 A 2018 review calls it the largest emergency evacuation in US history.2

The evacuation killed more people than the storm did. Over 100 of the at least 119 deaths attributed to Rita were linked to the evacuation itself, about 107 by that count.2

Heat in stuck cars was a major killer. At least 10% of evacuation deaths were linked to hyperthermia (dangerous overheating) in vehicles.2

Medical examiners suspected overheating or a worsened chronic illness in about another 50% of evacuation deaths.2

A bus carrying nursing-home residents caught fire in the evacuation traffic. About two dozen people died.2

Plans can be fixed. Texas revised its evacuation plans after Rita and used the changes successfully in later storms.2

Three lessons for your own plan

  • Leave early. Go early enough that severe weather cannot trap you.1 Long waits in traffic were where Rita evacuees overheated.2
  • Pack for a long, hot wait. Carry drinking water and a way to stay cool for every person and animal in the car. Overheating in vehicles was linked to at least 10% of evacuation deaths.2
  • Plan transport for frail people first. People with chronic illness were among those who died on the road.2 Arrange their ride and supplies before anyone else's.
  • No car? Arrange a ride now. Ready.gov says to make arrangements with family, friends or your local government.1
🧓 Elderly & disabled

Older, sick and disabled people

Rita's deaths show the danger: overheating and worsened chronic illness in vehicles, and a burning bus full of nursing-home residents.2

If someone in your home is frail, uses medical equipment or cannot cope with heat, plan to leave early, before roads fill.1 See Caring for Elderly & Disabled People.

👶 Babies & children

Babies and children in the car

Overheating in vehicles was a recorded cause of Rita evacuation deaths.2 Pack water, feeds and diapers for a long delay, not just the planned drive time. Never leave a child alone in a car. See The Baby & Toddler Prep Kit.

#⚖️ Risks of leaving vs risks of staying

Neither choice is free of risk. Compare the documented dangers for your hazard, your home and your people. Then pick the smaller danger.

Leaving is always the safe choice.

In the Hurricane Rita evacuation, the evacuation itself was linked to over 100 of at least 119 deaths.2 Leave when your zone is ordered out, and leave early.1

A generator outside the house cannot hurt anyone inside.

After Katrina and Rita, most generator CO poisonings involved generators placed outside but near homes. At least 78 people were poisoned and 10 died.5

Do

  • Leave early when your zone is ordered out
  • Follow the recommended routes
  • Keep half a tank of fuel, always
  • Get inside fast for radiation or chemicals
  • Follow official instructions on a battery radio

Don't

  • Take shortcuts that may be blocked
  • Wait until severe weather traps you
  • Run a generator near the house
  • Go outside to look during a release
  • Drive into flooded areas

Each rule above comes from Ready.gov, the CDC or Ready Illinois guidance.1456

#⛽ If you go: fuel, timing and routes

Keep at least half a tank of fuel at all times. Ready.gov advises this in case you must leave without warning. Fill the tank when an evacuation seems likely.1

Do not count on buying fuel on the way. Gas stations may be closed during emergencies and unable to pump gas during power outages.1

Take one car per family if you can. Ready.gov advises this to reduce congestion and delay.1

Know your range before you need it. Multiply the fuel in the tank by your car's fuel economy. Use your own car's measured figures, not the sticker.

The formula: gallons × miles per gallon = miles. In metric: liters ÷ (liters per 100 km) × 100 = km.

The evacuation, step by step

Step by step · 7 steps

  1. Know your zone and destination

    Plan how you will leave and where you will go.1 Hurricane orders go by zone.7

  2. Keep half a tank, always

    That covers an unexpected order to leave.1

  3. Fill up when evacuation looks likely

    Do it before the order, not after.1

  4. Grab go-bags and leave early

    Leave early enough to avoid being trapped by severe weather.1 See Go-Bags for Every Family Member.

  5. Use the recommended routes

    Do not take shortcuts. They may be blocked. Never drive into flooded areas.1

  6. Follow official instructions

    Listen to a battery-powered radio and follow local evacuation instructions.1 Keep a paper map with your route marked.

  7. Tell your out-of-area contact

    Say where you are going, your route and when you expect to arrive.

  1. Every day

    Fuel stays at half a tank or more.1

  2. Evacuation looks likely

    Fill the tank.1 Load go-bags, medicines, documents and pet supplies.

  3. Your zone is ordered out

    Leave. Use the recommended routes.1

  4. On the road

    Watch for washed-out roads and downed power lines. Do not drive into flooded areas.1

  5. Shelter-in-place order instead

    Get inside immediately.6

🎯 Practice now

Drive it and drill it

Ready.gov advises getting to know alternate routes out of your area.1 Drive your evacuation route and one alternate route in daylight. Note the time it takes and where the route could flood.

Run a shelter-in-place drill: everyone, including the dog, into the chosen room with the door shut. Time it, then improve it.

#📍 Where to go: relatives, shelters, rural or urban

Decide where you will go before you need to. Ready.gov tells you to plan where you will go if you are told to evacuate.1

Pick several destinations in different directions. Ready.gov advises this so you still have options in an emergency.1

Your options, in planning order

  • Friends or relatives outside the hazard zone. Agree now, in writing, that you can come. Say how many people and animals.
  • Public shelters opened by officials. Listen to official instructions for which shelters are open. Temporary public shelters may not allow animals, so plan where your dog will stay.14
  • Any safe place you can reach on half a tank, by your own range math above.1

Rural or urban? What the evidence supports

We found no reliable study that ranks rural or urban places as safer in a long outage. So this book makes no such claim.

Instead, test any destination against facts you can check yourself:

  • Is it outside storm-surge and flood zones?3
  • Can you reach it on the fuel you will have?1
  • Does it have water you can make safe? See Finding Water When the Taps Stop.
  • Do people there know you and expect you?
  • Can your baby, your oldest relative and your dog cope with the trip?
🌸 Women & girls

Women and girls

Before you arrive, ask how sleeping areas and toilets work at your destination or shelter. Travel and use facilities in pairs. Pack menstrual supplies and any prescribed medicines in each go-bag. See Personal Safety for Women & Girls.

⏳ The 30-year view

Leaving for good

In a multi-year outage, leaving can mean leaving your stores, water system and garden behind. Weigh that against a hazard that will reach the house, such as storm surge.3

#🧠 Remember this

When do you shelter in place?

When officials order it, or when being outside is more dangerous than being inside.

In a hurricane, what do you evacuate from?

The water. Storm surge is the greatest threat to life on the coast.

Radiation emergency: the three rules?

Get inside, stay inside, stay tuned. Basement or middle of the building.

Chemical cloud: which room?

An interior room above ground, sealed with plastic sheeting and duct tape.

How much fuel should be in the car?

At least half a tank always. A full tank when evacuation looks likely.

What did Hurricane Rita teach?

Can you count on buying fuel on the way?

No. Gas stations may be closed or unable to pump during power outages.

Not near the house. Most CO poisonings after Katrina and Rita came from generators outside but near homes.
