⚡ The 30-second version

  • Store at least 1 gallon (3.8 L) per person per day. That is the CDC and Ready.gov (FEMA) minimum. Keep at least 3 days, and 2 weeks if you can.12
  • In very hot weather, plan for double. Official guidance says heat can double water needs.2
  • Store extra for pregnant and breastfeeding women, children, sick people and pets. The CDC names pregnant women, sick people and pets; extension guidance adds children and nursing mothers.16
  • One gallon is a floor, not a full day. The humanitarian Sphere standard is 15 L (4 gallons) per person per day for drinking, cooking and hygiene.3
  • Thirty years cannot come out of storage. At 1 gallon a day, a family of 4 uses 1,460 gallons (5,527 L) a year. You need a source that refills.1

#📏 The official numbers, side by side

Agencies give different numbers because they answer different questions. The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and Ready.gov tell households what to store at home for a short emergency.1 Sphere sets minimum standards for humanitarian aid after a disaster.4

Know both. The home number tells you what to store. The humanitarian number tells you what a household uses once drinking, cooking and washing all count.

Home storage: CDC and FEMA First 72 h

Store at least 1 gallon (3.8 L) of water per person per day. The CDC says to keep at least a 3-day supply, and a 2-week supply if possible.1

Ready.gov, the FEMA preparedness site, gives the same figure of 1 gallon per person per day.2 The CDC says this water covers drinking, cooking and brushing teeth.1

Notice the words "at least". Both agencies present the gallon as a minimum, and both say hot climates need more.12

Disaster relief: Sphere and UNHCR First 30 days

The Sphere standard is 15 L (about 4 gallons) per person per day for drinking, cooking and personal hygiene (Sphere Handbook, 2011 edition).3 That is almost 4 times the CDC home minimum.

Sphere training material says 7.5 L (2 gallons) per person per day may be appropriate only for a short time, in the acute phase of a drought.4 It also says a minimum is never a maximum.4

UNHCR, the UN refugee agency, plans for 20 L (5.3 gallons) per person per day for domestic needs and personal hygiene in refugee settings.5

#🍽️ Where a day's water goes

Sphere splits basic daily water into three uses: drinking (including water in food), basic hygiene and basic cooking.3 Seeing the split shows why one gallon runs out fast.

📊 Key fact

What is left of a gallon after drinking

Sphere puts drinking and food water at 2.5–3 L per person per day.3 Take that out of a 3.8 L gallon and only 0.8–1.3 L (about 1 quart) is left for cooking and washing. Sphere's own range for those two uses is 5–12 L (1.3–3.2 gal).3

When water is short, drinking comes first. Sphere calls drinking and food water "survival needs", while hygiene and cooking are "basic" needs.3

Do not plan for hygiene water to be zero for long. Clean hands and safe toilets are covered in Handwashing & Personal Hygiene.

#🔥 Who needs more water

The 1 gallon minimum is not enough for everyone. The CDC says to store more for pregnant women, people who are sick, pets, and anyone living in a hot climate.1

University of Florida (UF/IFAS) Extension guidance adds children and nursing mothers to the list.6

Heat, hard work and exercise

In very hot temperatures, water needs can double.2 Ready.gov also says people in warm climates may need more water.2

FEMA and the Red Cross say a normally active person needs to drink at least 2 quarts (1.9 L) a day.7 They add that needs vary with age, physical condition, activity, diet and climate.7

Hauling water, cutting wood and long walks are more than normal activity. Plan extra drinking water for whoever does that work.

For a family of 4, that turns a 2-week store of 56 gallons (212 L) into 112 gallons (424 L).12 Heat exhaustion and heat stroke are in Hypothermia, Frostbite & Heat Stroke.

🌸 Women & girls

Pregnant and breastfeeding women

The CDC lists pregnant women among the people to store extra water for.1 UF/IFAS Extension guidance adds nursing mothers.6

Give her a personal bottle with liter marks. Then everyone can see how much she has had, and nobody has to ask.

👶 Babies & children

Babies and children

Count every child as a full person in your plan. UF/IFAS Extension guidance says homes with children should store more, not less.6

A baby on powdered formula needs safe water for every bottle. The CDC says to use bottled water for formula until tap water is declared safe.9 Add it to the plan, and see Feeding Babies When Formula Runs Out. Dehydration warning signs in babies are in Sick Baby & Child: Danger Signs.

🧓 Elderly & disabled

Sick and older relatives

The CDC says to store more for people who are sick.1 UF/IFAS Extension guidance says the same.6

Put any relative with a long-term illness in the "needs more" group. Keep a filled cup within their reach, and refill it on a schedule.

🐕 Dogs & pets

Dogs, cats and other pets

The CDC lists pets as a reason to store more water than the people's minimum.1 Ready.gov says a pet's emergency kit needs a water bowl and several days' supply of water.10 Your dog's needs are real numbers you can measure, so measure them now.

Fill the bowl from a measuring jug. Each time you top it up, write down how much you added. Average 3 days. Do it again in the hottest month. Dog dehydration and heatstroke are in Dog First Aid.

Do

  • Count babies, children and older relatives as full people
  • Plan double for very hot weather
  • Add extra for pregnancy, breastfeeding and illness
  • Measure each pet's real daily drinking

Don't

  • Plan for healthy adults only
  • Use the same number in summer and winter
  • Forget the dog
  • Assume one gallon covers washing and cooking too

#🧮 Your household's number: 3 days, 2 weeks, 1 year

Work out one number and write it on your water store. Follow the steps, then check them with the calculator.

Step by step · 7 steps

  1. Count everyone

    Adults, children, babies and older relatives. Add anyone who may shelter with you. Write pets on a separate line.

  2. Multiply by 1 gallon (3.8 L)

    That is the minimum per person per day.1

  3. Adjust for heat

    If your summers are very hot, double the number for those months.2

  4. Add for higher-need people

    Pregnant or breastfeeding women, children and anyone sick get extra.16

  5. Add your pets

    Use the daily amount you measured for each animal.1

  6. Multiply by days

    3 days at least. 14 days if you can.1

  7. Write it down

    Mark the total on your containers and in the margin of this page.

The weight of water

One liter of water weighs 1 kg; one US gallon weighs about 3.8 kg (8.3 lb).11 Two weeks of minimum water for a family of 4 is 212 L, so it weighs about 212 kg (467 lb).

That fits in eleven 20 L (5.3 gal) jerrycans, or one 55-gallon (208 L) drum plus a little more. Container choice, placement and weight are in Storing Water for Months & Years.

💡 Tip

Store in sizes you can lift

A full 20 L jerrycan weighs 20 kg (44 lb).11 A full 55-gallon drum holds 208 L, so it weighs over 200 kg (450 lb): far too heavy to carry. Keep some water in small containers so a child or an older adult can pour a drink.

#🚩 Dehydration and "days without water"

Drinking water is a survival need, not a comfort. Sphere lists 2.5–3 L a day for drinking and food under "survival needs".3

Sphere also says that amount depends on the climate and the individual's body.3 That is why heat matters so much: very hot weather can double needs.2

Warning signs: the WHO scale

The World Health Organization (WHO) sorts dehydration into three levels: none, some and severe. It uses these signs to check people with diarrhea.8

WHO signs of dehydration (two or more signs place a person in a level)
LevelSigns
SevereVery sleepy (lethargic) or unconscious; sunken eyes; cannot drink or drinks poorly; pinched skin goes back very slowly (2 seconds or more)
SomeRestless or irritable; sunken eyes; drinks eagerly, thirsty
NoneNot enough signs for "some" or "severe"

Signs from the WHO diarrheal disease fact sheet (2024).8

☠️ Danger

Severe dehydration is an emergency

If anyone shows two or more severe signs, get medical care at once. Children are at high risk: WHO lists diarrheal disease as the third leading cause of death in children aged 1 to 59 months.8

⚠️ Caution

Why this page prints no "days without water" number

This book prints only numbers it can check against an official or peer-reviewed source. A reliable figure for how long people survive with no water at all could not be confirmed for this edition, so none is given here.

Plan so that nobody in your household ever goes a day without drinking. That is the whole point of the numbers above.

A short-term minimum is fine to live on for months.

Sphere says 7.5 L per person per day may be appropriate only for a short time in an acute drought, and that a minimum is never a maximum.4 Its standard for drinking, cooking and hygiene is 15 L.3

One gallon a day covers everything a person needs.

The CDC calls 1 gallon a minimum and says to store more for pregnant women, sick people, pets and hot climates.1 Sphere's drinking figure alone is 2.5–3 L, leaving little of the gallon for anything else.3

#⏳ The 30-year math: why storage alone cannot last

A year of minimum water for one person is 365 gallons (1,382 L). That is 1 gallon a day for 365 days.1

Stored water is a bridge. It carries you from the first hour until a source that refills is working.

Real outages have lasted far longer than 2 weeks. After Hurricane Maria in 2017, a survey of 3,299 households in Puerto Rico found they went an average of 68 days without water service (New England Journal of Medicine, 2018).12 That is almost 5 times the CDC 2-week goal.1

  1. Day 3

    3 gallons (11 L) per person. The CDC minimum stock.1

  2. Day 14

    14 gallons (53 L) per person. The CDC goal if you can store it.1

  3. Year 1

    365 gallons (1,382 L) per person. A family of 4 needs 1,460 gallons (5,527 L), about 27 drums of 55 gallons.

  4. Year 1 at Sphere level

    A family of 4 at 15 L each uses 21,900 L (5,785 gal), about 105 drums.3

  5. Year 30

    10,950 gallons (41,450 L) per person at the minimum. A family of 4 needs 43,800 gallons (about 165,800 L).

⏳ The 30-year view

The 30-year view

No household can store 30 years of water. The plan is a renewable source plus treatment, with stored water as the buffer for droughts, freezes and breakdowns.

Start with Rainwater Harvesting and Finding Water When the Taps Stop. Treat every new source using Disinfecting Water.

Size your source with the same math. If your household needs 840 L (222 gal) every 2 weeks at the Sphere level, your source and tanks must deliver at least that, every 2 weeks, all year.3

Then add a margin for the hot season, when needs can double.2

#📓 Track it, plan it, never guess

A written water log tells you how many days you have left. Write it down so nobody has to remember it or guess.

☠️ Danger

Never ration drinking water

FEMA and the Red Cross say: never ration water. Drink the amount you need today, and look for more for tomorrow.7 The log is there to warn you early, not to cut what people drink.

The rule is one division: liters left ÷ (people × liters per person per day) = days left. Example: 56 gallons (212 L) for 4 people at 1 gallon each per day lasts 14 days.1

The example assumes 4 people at the CDC minimum, about 15 L (4 gal) a day for the household, so 197 ÷ 15.2 = 12.96, rounded down to 12.1 Round "days left" down, never up.

🎯 Practice now

Do a 24-hour water audit now

While the taps still work, live for one day only on water you pour from marked containers. Write down every liter you use for drinking, cooking, washing and cleaning.

Compare your total with 3.8 L and 15 L per person.13 Your real number is the one to store and to plan your source around.

Step by step · 5 steps

  1. Mark your containers

    Write the volume in large numbers on each one, plus the fill date.

  2. Give each person a marked bottle

    Fill it each morning. You can see at a glance who is drinking too little.

  3. Drink first, then cook, then wash

    Sphere calls drinking water a survival need.3 Cooking and hygiene come next.

  4. Log every evening

    Measure what is left and update "days left".

  5. Act at 7 days left

    Start collecting and treating water from another source before you run out.

🛒 Get it before you need it

Get these before you need them

  • A 1 L (1 quart) measuring jug
  • A marker pen and tape for labels
  • One marked bottle per person
  • Enough containers for your 14-day number (see Storing Water)
