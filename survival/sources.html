3. The First 72 Hours: Exact Order of Actions

  1.   2. USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service: Keep Your Food Safe During EmergenciesFridge safe up to 4 hours unopened; discard refrigerated meat, poultry, fish, soft cheeses, milk, eggs, leftovers and deli items after 4 hours without power; full freezer about 48 hours, half full 24; 40°F and 0°F targets; appliance thermometers; coolers for outages over 4 hours; refreezing; never taste food.

  1.   2. American Red Cross: Food and Water in an EmergencyRed Cross edition of the same brochure: never ration water; a normally active person needs at least one-half gallon of water a day just for drinking.

13. Stay or Go? Sheltering in Place vs Evacuating

  1. Ready.gov (FEMA): EvacuationHalf a tank of gas at all times, full tank if evacuation seems likely; gas stations may be closed or unable to pump during power outages; one car per family; follow recommended routes, no shortcuts; watch for washed-out roads and downed lines, do not drive into floods; listen to a battery radio; know alternate routes and several destinations in different directions; arrange rides if you have no car; leave early.
  2.   3. NOAA National Hurricane Center: Hurricane HazardsStorm surge and large waves are the greatest threat to life and property along the coast; flooding is the major threat for people living inland.

16. Storing Water for Months & Years

  1. CDC: How to Create an Emergency Water Supply1 gallon per person per day, 2-week supply; more for hot climates, pregnancy, illness; bottled water safest; food-grade containers; no bleach or pesticide containers; 1 tsp bleach per quart sanitizing method; label and date; replace every 6 months; 50–70°F, no sunlight, away from gasoline and pesticides.
  2. Ready.gov (FEMA): Preparing Your Own Containers of WaterBuy food-grade containers; reuse 2-liter soft drink bottles, not milk or fruit juice containers, whose protein and sugar residue lets bacteria grow; chlorinated utility tap water needs nothing added, untreated well water needs bleach.
  3. Regional Water Providers Consortium: Emergency Water SourcesA home water heater holds 30–80 gallons; toilet tank water can be used for flushing.
  4. FEMA: What are emergency water sources if I'm running out of clean water?Water heater draining steps (power off, cool, drain valve); toilet tank, not bowl, only if clear and free of chemicals such as colored tablets, and disinfected before use.
  5. National Weather Service: Hurricane Safety ChecklistFill bathtub and large containers; use bathtub water for cleaning and flushing only; do not drink it.
  6. CPSC: Children Drowning in Bathtubs, Bath Seats and Buckets2006–2010: 434 in-home drowning deaths of children under 5; 82% under age 2; 81% involved bathtubs or bath products.
  7. USGS Water Science School: Facts About WaterWater weighs about 8.3 pounds per gallon and 1 kilogram per liter (used to calculate container weights).
  8. WHQR Public Radio: Asheville taps are clear to drink; boil water notice lifted53-day water crisis after Hurricane Helene; notice lifted after 120 samples were tested.
  9. EPA: City of Asheville lifts systemwide boil water notice issued after Hurricane HeleneBoil water notice lifted for all customers on November 18, 2024.
  10. Utah Division of Emergency Management: Water Storage GuideFood-grade HDPE #2 is the best choice; look for the 2 in the recycling triangle; milk jugs break down and become brittle.
  11. Ready.gov (FEMA): WaterReplace stored water every 6 months if it is not commercially bottled.
  12. University of Nebraska–Lincoln Extension: Preparing Water for Use in an EmergencyFuel and pesticide vapors penetrate plastic; sturdy shelving because water weighs over 8 lb per gallon; leave 2–3 inches headspace for freezing; avoid glass.
  13. Utah State University Extension: Water Storage TipsWater from wells, rivers or rainwater must be treated; keep plastic off concrete or dirt because it absorbs odors.
  14. EPA: Emergency Disinfection of Drinking Water8 drops of 6% or 6 drops of 8.25% bleach per gallon, 2/3 tsp (6%) or 1/2 tsp (8.25%) per 8 gallons; double for cloudy, colored or very cold water; stir, wait 30 minutes, check for a slight chlorine smell, else repeat and wait 15 minutes; bleach stored under one year; no scented, color-safe or cleaner-added bleach.
  15. CDC: Prepare and Store Powdered Infant Formula in an EmergencyUse bottled water until tap water is declared safe; boil 1 minute (3 above 6,500 ft); water of at least 70°C (158°F) for infants under 2 months, premature or immunocompromised; cool to body temperature and test before feeding; chemical contamination cannot be removed by boiling, disinfecting or filtering.

  1.
