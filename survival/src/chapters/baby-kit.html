🎒 The kit at a glance: eight bins

Pack the kit by job, not by store aisle. When you are tired and it is dark, you want to grab "the diaper bin", not search five boxes.

Start from the official lists. Ready.gov names infant formula, bottles, diapers, wipes and diaper rash cream.8 The Red Cross adds baby food and says to copy important documents.1

Ready.gov also says to keep supplies in airtight plastic bags, packed in one or two easy-to-carry containers.8 One bin per job does that for a baby.

  • Bin 1, Feed: formula or nursing supplies, bottles or cups, brush, soap, pot, fuel
  • Bin 2, Diapers: diapers in two sizes, wipes or washcloths, rash cream, soap, lidded pail
  • Bin 3, Clothes: current size, next size up, warm layer, sun layer
  • Bin 4, Sleep and night: sleep space, headlamp, spare batteries, battery CO alarm
  • Bin 5, Medicine: children's pouch, thermometer, ORS packets, dose card
  • Bin 6, Carry: carrier, car seat manual, photos of car seat labels
  • Bin 7, Papers: child ID card, copies of records, health card, cash
  • Bin 8, Water: bottled water marked "formula only"

Label the outside, three times

Write the bin number, its job and the clothing size or date on two sides and the lid. You should be able to read it by flashlight from across the room.

A 3-year kit, not a 3-day kit

A newborn becomes a 3-year-old who walks, eats family food and may be out of diapers. Ready.gov says to re-think your needs every year and update the kit as your family changes.8

In a long outage there is no calendar reminder. Tie the full review to a fixed date, such as the child's birthday.

📦 How much: 2 weeks, 3 months, 1 year

Use three tiers, and tie each one to a real event.

  • 2 weeks: the Red Cross advises a 2-week supply for home and 3 days for evacuation.1
  • 3 months: after Hurricane Maria in 2017, surveyed Puerto Rico households averaged 84 days without power and 68 days without water.2
  • 1 year: power came back to the whole island in August 2018, about 11 months after the storm.9

Short outages happen too. In the February 2021 Texas freeze, more than 4.5 million people in Texas lost power, some for up to 4 days.10

Measure, then multiply

Your child's real daily count is the only number that fits your child. Get it in 3 days.

  1. Count for 3 days

    Tally every diaper, wipe, feed, outfit and bib. Tape the tally sheet inside a cupboard door.

  2. Find the daily average

    Add the three days and divide by 3. Round up, never down.

  3. Multiply for each tier

    Times 14 for 2 weeks, times 91 for 3 months, times 365 for 1 year.

  4. Fill the 2-week tier first

    Finish one tier before you start the next. A full 2 weeks beats a half-filled year.

  5. Re-count every month

    Write the new count and date on the tally sheet. Adjust the next purchase.

Water for the baby

Count the baby as a full person in your water plan. CDC advises at least 4 liters (1 gallon) per person per day, for 2 weeks if possible, and more for pregnant women, sick people and hot climates.11

University of Florida Extension says to store more water when children or nursing mothers live in the home.12 Ready.gov says children, nursing mothers and sick people may need more, and very hot weather can double water needs.13

The arithmetic per person: 1 gallon (3.8 liters) a day is 14 gallons (53 liters) for 2 weeks, 91 gallons (344 liters) for 3 months and 365 gallons (1,382 liters) for a year. Storage methods are in Storing Water for Months & Years.

🍼 Feeding supplies

Pack for how your baby eats today, and pack a backup. Feeding changes fast between birth and age 3.

Breastfed babies

Direct breastfeeding needs no mixing water, no bottles and no fuel. The nursing mother does need extra water, so plan more for her.12

The facts on supply, latch, blocked ducts and relactation are in Breastfeeding: The Survival Superpower.

The mother is part of the baby kit

Put her water bottle, snacks, breast pads and nursing clothes in the feeding bin, within reach of where she feeds. UF Extension lists nursing mothers among the people who need more stored water.12

Formula-fed babies

Formula is only as safe as the water you mix it with. CDC says to prepare formula with bottled water until officials say tap water is safe.3

If you must boil water, CDC says to boil it for 1 minute (3 minutes above 2,000 m, or 6,500 ft). For babies under 2 months, babies born early and babies with a weak immune system, mix powdered formula with water of at least 70°C (158°F).3

Water that hot can scald. CDC says to cool the made-up bottle to body temperature and test it before feeding.3

Boiling does not remove chemicals. CDC says chemically contaminated water cannot be made safe by boiling, disinfecting or filtering.3

So the formula bin holds more than formula:

  • Formula of the exact type your baby uses, with the use-by date written on the lid
  • Bottled water marked "formula only"
  • A pot with a lid, a stove and fuel to boil water
  • Bottles or cups, a bottle brush, dish soap and a washing basin
  • A clean towel and a lidded box for drying clean parts

The full method, and what to do when formula runs out, is in Feeding Babies When Formula Runs Out.

Stored water has a date

CDC says unopened commercially bottled water is the safest stored water. Label and date water you store yourself and replace it every 6 months. Keep all of it at 10–21°C (50–70°F), out of sunlight.11 Check the "formula only" water on the same day you rotate the rest.

Toddlers and solid food

The Red Cross lists baby food in the kit.1 Add spoons, bowls and an open cup your child can hold. First foods and foods to avoid are in First Foods for Babies Without Stores.

Learn infant choking first aid before solids start. The 2025 AHA guidelines use back blows and heel-of-hand chest thrusts for infants.25 Practice the steps in CPR & Choking.

Do

  • Keep a separate stock of bottled water for formula3
  • Store fuel for boiling with the feeding bin
  • Pack two of every bottle part
  • Empty and turn over the washing basin after use

Don't

  • Mix formula with water that may hold fuel or chemicals, even boiled3
  • Leave a basin of wash water standing where a toddler can reach it7
  • Switch formula types in a crisis without a reason

🧷 Diapers, wipes and hygiene

Diaper supplies are health supplies. Ready.gov lists diapers, wipes and diaper rash cream in the basic kit.8

A healthy-looking child can still spread germs. CDC says hepatitis A spreads by the fecal-oral route, and 70% of infections in children under 6 cause no symptoms.16

So wash your hands with soap after every change. WHO estimates that unsafe water, sanitation and hygiene caused 1.4 million deaths in 2019, including 395,000 children under 5.17 Poor hand hygiene alone caused close to 400,000 diarrhea deaths that year.17

What goes in the diaper bin:

  • Diapers in the current size and the next size up
  • Wipes, or washcloths and a small water bottle
  • Diaper rash cream8
  • Bar soap and a handwashing jug
  • A wipeable changing mat
  • A pail with a tight lid, plus trash bags

If the baby seems well, you can skip handwashing after a diaper change.

Most hepatitis A infections in children under 6 (70%) have no symptoms, and the virus spreads by the fecal-oral route.16 Wash every time.

Baths, pails and basins can drown a baby

CPSC counted 434 in-home drowning deaths of children under 5 in 2006–2010. 82% were under age 2, and 81% involved bathtubs or bath products. Buckets were also named.6

KidsHealth says drowning can happen quickly in very shallow water. Never leave water containers unattended.7 Stay with a baby for the whole bath, put a lid on every pail and empty basins straight away. More in Keeping Kids Safe.

Cloth stash math, washing with little water and rash treatment are in Cloth Diapers & Baby Hygiene.

👕 Clothes that grow with the child

Children grow out of clothes, so store sizes ahead. Ready.gov says to re-think kit needs every year and update the kit as your family changes.8

Temperature is the reason clothes matter. Ready.gov notes that young children are especially vulnerable to extreme temperatures during power outages.14

How many outfits

Outfits needed = outfits used per day times days between washes, plus spares for wash day. Example: 3 a day, washing every 3 days, is 9 outfits plus spares.

Use your 3-day tally for the "per day" number. If you wash less often, the outfit count goes up by the same arithmetic.

Sizes, by fit

  • Pack by fit, not by the age on the label. Try clothes on the child when you can.
  • Weigh and measure monthly. Write the numbers on the health card.
  • Keep outgrown clothes clean and labeled. They serve a younger child, a trade or cloth for repairs.
  • Warm layers and sun layers both. Cold-house methods are in Staying Warm Without Heat.
  1. Every day

    Dress the child for the room you are in, not for the season on the calendar.

  2. Every month

    Weigh, measure and check fit. Shift bins when clothes get tight.

  3. Every season

    Before the cold or hot season, check the layers you need.

  4. Every birthday

    Do the full kit review Ready.gov advises each year.8

Buy bigger sizes while stores exist

Secondhand children's clothes in the next sizes up cost little now. Wash, label by size and pack them in airtight bags, as Ready.gov advises for kit supplies.8

😴 Sleep, light and air at night

Night feeds and changes need light that cannot start a fire. Ready.gov advises flashlights, not candles, in a power outage.14 A headlamp keeps both hands free for the baby.

What goes in the night bin:

  • A headlamp and a flashlight, with spare batteries
  • The baby's own sleep space, set up to the safe-sleep rules
  • Sleep clothing for the season
  • A battery carbon monoxide (CO) alarm

Put a battery CO alarm near where the baby sleeps. CDC advises battery-operated or battery-backup CO alarms near sleeping areas, and says to replace them every 5 years.15

Generators stay outside

CDC says to run generators outdoors, at least 6 m (20 feet) from windows, doors and vents, and never in a home or garage.15 CDC also says infants are at higher risk from CO, and a baby cannot tell you they feel ill.15 Read Carbon Monoxide: The Blackout Killer.

Safe-sleep facts on the sleep surface, position and bedding are in Newborn Care: The First 28 Days. Read them before you choose what goes in this bin.

🚗 Car seats, carriers and moving with a child

Your car kit is a second baby kit. Ready.gov advises keeping kits at home, at work and in the car.8

Car seats

  • Photograph every label on the seat. Keep the photos with your kit papers.
  • Keep the manual in a zip bag in the car kit. It is the only instruction book written for that exact seat.
  • Write the seat's limits on the health card, next to the child's latest weight and height. Compare them at each monthly weigh-in.
  • If a used seat has no labels or manual, you cannot check its limits. Do not rely on it.

Carriers and loads

A carrier keeps your hands free, but the baby counts as load. Military research recommends loads of 30% of body mass or less to avoid a drop in performance.22 That figure comes from studies of trained soldiers, so treat it as a ceiling, not a target.

  1. Weigh yourself

    Use a bathroom scale. Write the number down.

  2. Weigh the full load

    Step on again holding the baby in the carrier and wearing the bag.

  3. Subtract and divide

    The difference divided by your weight is your load share.

  4. Lighten if over 30%

    Move items to another adult or a cart.22

Example arithmetic: 30% of 60 kg (132 lb) is 18 kg (40 lb). A 9 kg (20 lb) baby plus a 9 kg (20 lb) bag reaches that line.

A walking toddler can carry a little. The National Safety Council says a child's backpack should weigh no more than 5–10% of the child's weight.23 For a 14 kg (31 lb) child, that is 0.7–1.4 kg (1.5–3 lb).

What a toddler can carry

A small water bottle, a snack, a comfort toy and a copy of their ID card. Keep it within the 5–10% line.23 Full family bags are in Go-Bags for Every Family Member.

💊 Infant medicines, thermometer and health card

Give children's medicines only as the label or a clinician says. Doses depend on the product and the child, so this chapter lists none. Use professional care whenever it exists.

Never give aspirin to children.4 Keep the adult kit's aspirin out of the children's pouch.

Why ORS is in every baby kit

WHO lists diarrheal disease as the third leading cause of death in children aged 1–59 months, with about 443,832 deaths in children under 5 in 2021.18 How to use ORS is in Diarrhea, Dehydration & Oral Rehydration.

WHO keeps a separate Essential Medicines List for children (EMLc). The 2025 edition lists 374 medicines.20 Use it as a map when you talk with your clinician, not as a shopping list. See Building a Long-Term Medical Kit.

Store and check

Check dates at every kit review. The Red Cross advises checking expiration dates and replacing used or out-of-date items.5

If a medicine was not stored at the right temperature, FDA says to check its storage instructions or ask a pharmacist.21 Ready.gov says to throw out refrigerated medicine after an outage of more than a day, unless the label says otherwise.14 If a life depends on that medicine, do not just throw it out. Ready.gov says to ask a doctor or pharmacist and use it until a new supply is available.14

The health card

Write one card per child and keep it in the medicine pouch.

  • Name, date of birth, latest weight and the date it was taken
  • Allergies and medical conditions
  • Each medicine: name, strength, label dose for this child, times given
  • Normal temperature and breathing rate, measured when the child is well
  • Vaccine dates and clinician contacts
  • Car seat limits and the latest height

Record "normal" while the child is healthy, so you can compare when they are sick. Danger signs are in Sick Baby & Child: Danger Signs. Fever care is in Fever, Coughs & Common Childhood Illnesses.

Practice with the thermometer now

Take your child's temperature while they are well, by daylight and then by headlamp. Write the reading and time on the health card. A skill you have practiced is easier at 3 a.m.

🪪 Papers, ID and comfort

Pack proof of who your child is and who they belong to. After Hurricanes Katrina and Rita, 5,192 children were reported missing. Reuniting all of them with family took more than six months.24

  • A child ID card: recent photo, name, parents' names and an out-of-area contact
  • Copies of the birth certificate and vaccination record (the Red Cross says to copy important documents)1
  • The health card
  • Extra cash, which the Red Cross includes in its kit list1
  • A familiar comfort toy for awake time

Update the photo at every kit review. Reunification planning is in Keeping Kids Safe.
