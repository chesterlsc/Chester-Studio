🔢 The planning number: 2,100 kcal a day First 30 days

Start every food plan at 2,100 kilocalories (kcal) per person per day. In this book, "calories" means kilocalories.

The Food Security Cluster, which coordinates humanitarian food aid, calls 2,100 kcal the minimum planning figure for general rations and asks partners to align with Sphere.1 Its 2025 emergency guidelines call it the Sphere standard and ask all groups to harmonize around it.2

A 2002 National Academies report on emergency relief food also used 2,100 kcal per day as its planning basis.5

It is a floor for planning, not a perfect fit for each body. The guidance says to adjust the requirements to the context.1 WFP and UNHCR guidance calls 2,100 kcal the initial reference value for the early stage of an emergency, when people have no other food.3

One average, many bodies

2,100 kcal is a planning average for a whole group. Adult needs alone run from 1,600 to 3,000 kcal a day, depending on sex, age and activity.4 Plan the group total first, then check each person.

The 30-year view

One person eating 2,100 kcal a day for 30 years needs about 23 million kcal (22,995,000).1 Plan stored food as the bridge while you build food production. Read Feeding a Family Forever: The Land Math.

👪 Real needs: sex, age and workload

Adult women need about 1,600–2,400 kcal a day. Adult men need about 2,000–3,000 kcal a day.4 Where a person falls in that range depends on age and how physically active they are.4

The US Dietary Guidelines give estimates by age, sex and three activity levels: sedentary, moderately active and active.6 In the 2015–2020 edition this table is Appendix 2, "Estimated Calorie Needs per Day, by Age, Sex, and Physical Activity Level".14

The estimates are rounded to the nearest 200 kcal. One person's real needs may be higher or lower than the average in the table.6

The gaps are simple subtraction: 2,400 − 2,100 = 300, and 3,000 − 2,100 = 900.4

Averages can hide a hungry worker

A household that averages 2,100 kcal a day can still leave its most active man up to 900 kcal short every day.4 Set a target for each person, not just for the group.

Without power, many chores are done by hand: hauling water, cutting wood, washing clothes, digging. Put anyone who does them every day at the active end of their range when you plan.

  1. List everyone

    Write each person's name, age, sex and body weight on one page.

  2. Pick an activity level

    Mark each person sedentary, moderately active or active.6 Be honest about the work they do now, not last year.

  3. Look up the estimate

    Find their age, sex and activity level in the printed calorie table.13

  4. Add the household up

    Total the daily targets. Compare the total with 2,100 × the number of people.

  5. Plan with the bigger number

    Use whichever total is higher for buying and storing.

  6. Recheck every season

    Redo the list when work, ages, pregnancies or body weights change.

🥩 Protein: the floor and the new target

Adults need at least 0.8 g of protein per kg of body weight per day. This US recommended dietary allowance (RDA) has changed very little since the RDA idea began in 1941.9

The number comes from nitrogen balance studies. They gave an estimated average requirement (EAR) of 0.66 g/kg and an RDA of 0.83 g/kg, which was rounded down to 0.8.9

The same 0.8 g/kg RDA applies to every adult age group from 19 to over 70.12

The 2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines for Americans raise the target to 1.2–1.6 g/kg a day, adjusted to each person's calorie needs.78 That is 50–100% more than the old RDA (1.2 ÷ 0.8 = 1.5; 1.6 ÷ 0.8 = 2).

The new guidelines appeared in January 2026.11 Harvard's Nutrition Source points out that protein needs vary a lot between people, that the National Academies set wider ranges, and that needs are best set with a health professional.11

Protein foods named in the 2025–2030 guidelines: eggs, poultry, seafood and red meat, plus beans, lentils, nuts and soy.7 The guidelines say to put a high-protein food in every meal.8

For which of these keep for years, read Long-Term Food Storage.

Use kilograms for protein math

Divide pounds by 2.2 to get kilograms. A 154 lb adult is 70 kg, so the RDA is 70 × 0.8 = 56 g a day.9

🧈 Fat, carbohydrate and the Sphere split

Sphere puts 10–12% of a ration's energy from protein and 17% from fat.2 The Food Security Cluster calls these the minimum nutritional requirements for planning general rations.1

In a 2,100 kcal day, that is 210–252 kcal from protein and 357 kcal from fat.2 The remaining 1,491–1,533 kcal (71–73%) come from carbohydrate foods.

Calories and protein are not the whole diet

This chapter covers energy and protein. Vitamins and minerals need their own plan. Read Deficiency Diseases: Scurvy, Pellagra, Beriberi & More before you finish your food list.

🧮 From people to kilograms: label math

Turn your calorie total into kilograms of food with the label on each package. A calorie plan only helps once you know how many kilograms (or pounds) of each food to store.

  1. Read the label

    Find the energy per serving (Calories or kcal) and the serving size in grams.

  2. Work out kcal per kg

    kcal per serving ÷ grams per serving × 1,000.

  3. Choose the food's share

    Decide what part of your calories this food will supply, for example one third.

  4. Work out the kilograms

    Total kcal × share ÷ kcal per kg. Multiply by 2.2 for pounds.

  5. Round up and write it down

    Write the target on your inventory sheet and on the storage bin.

How many days will your food last?

Days of food = total kcal you have stored ÷ your household's kcal per day. Count every food, using the kcal per kg from its label.

Example: a family of 4 at 2,100 kcal each needs 8,400 kcal a day.1 If the inventory adds up to 500,000 kcal, that is 500,000 ÷ 8,400 = 59.5, so 59 full days.

  1. Day 1

    List everyone with age, sex, weight and activity level. Set each person's calorie and protein target.

  2. Week 1

    Do the label math (kcal per kg) for every stored food. Write it on each container.

  3. Month 1

    Add up total kcal on hand. Divide by the household's daily total to get days of food.

  4. Every season

    Reweigh people, update targets for work and pregnancy, and redo the days-of-food count.

The calculator multiplies people × days × kcal per person per day. Change the kcal box to match your household's own targets.

Practice the label math this week

Pick three foods you already store. Work out kcal per kg for each and write it on the container with a marker. Next time you shop, you can compare foods by kcal per kg and price. See Prepping on a Budget.

🤰 Who needs extra: pregnancy, breastfeeding, children and elders

Pregnant and breastfeeding women need more protein than other adults. The US reference tables set 71 g a day in pregnancy and in breastfeeding, for ages 19–30 and 31–50.10

Those tables base adult protein on 0.8 g per kg of a reference body weight.10 An AHRQ research protocol lists pregnancy and lactation at 1.1–1.3 g/kg a day, above the adult 0.8 g/kg.12

For a 60 kg (132 lb) woman, 0.8 g/kg is 48 g. The 71 g figure is 23 g more each day.10

These protein reference values were published in 2005. AHRQ notes they had not been updated for nearly two decades.12

Plan her extra share first

When food is short, set aside the extra protein for pregnant and breastfeeding women before you split the rest of the household's food. Read Pregnancy Care Without a Hospital and Breastfeeding: The Survival Superpower.

Babies are not small adults

Do not plan a baby's food from the adult numbers in this chapter. Read Feeding Babies When Formula Runs Out and the First Foods chapter. For children, use the age rows in the printed calorie table.13

Older adults: protein does not shrink with age

The adult protein RDA stays at 0.8 g/kg from age 19 to over 70.12 Calorie needs change with age and activity, so check each older person's row in the table.4 For diabetes, kidney disease and other special needs, read the Special Diets chapter.

Dogs need their own plan

The human calorie and protein numbers in this chapter do not apply to dogs. Plan their food separately with Feeding Dogs Without Kibble.

📋 Share food fairly and keep records

Write down each person's daily target and weigh what they actually get. A plan on paper does nothing if portions are guessed.

Weigh people on the same scale, at the same time of day, about once a month. Write it in the food log next to their target. A falling weight tells you to recheck their share and their workload.

Do

  • Set a calorie and protein target for each person
  • Plan active workers at the top of their range
  • Set aside extra protein for pregnant and breastfeeding women first
  • Weigh portions with a scale
  • Keep a dated food log and inventory
  • Re-plan when the work or the season changes

Don't

  • Give everyone the same portion by habit
  • Plan below 2,100 kcal a head without a reason
  • Use adult tables for babies or dogs
  • Guess food stocks by eye
  • Forget to count yourself in the plan
