🏘️ Why your neighbors are your first backup

In a long outage, outside help is slow and far away. After Hurricane Maria in 2017, a survey of 3,299 households in Puerto Rico found that, up to the end of that year, they went an average of 84 days without power, 68 days without water and 41 days without cell service.2

Hurricane Helene left 5.9 million customers without power across 10 states in 2024.6 In Asheville, North Carolina, the water crisis that followed lasted 53 days.7

Without electricity, fuel pumps do not work. Outages also knock out lighting and payment systems.8 Driving away to find help is often not an option.

The people who die are often the ones nobody checked on. In the February 2021 Texas winter storm, 246 people died. About two-thirds of those deaths came from cold exposure, mostly hypothermia (dangerously low body temperature).4

After Maria, the poor and the elderly had the highest risk of dying.3 Those are the neighbors a street can reach on foot every day.

Most households are not ready

FEMA's 2023 national survey found that only 37% of US adults made an emergency plan in the past year, down from 41% in 2022.5 Expect most of your neighbors to start with little. Organizing early turns their gaps into a shared plan.

For what research shows about how people behave in disasters, see How People Really Behave in Disasters. For group size and roles, see Building Your Group.

🗺️ Map your block: the first meeting First 72 h

Start with the homes you can walk around in a few minutes. A small block that actually meets beats a big network that only exists on paper.

Everything you learn goes on one paper block sheet and one hand-drawn map. Paper works when phones are dead.

  1. Knock and introduce yourself

    Go in daylight, with a partner. Give your name and house, and invite them to a short block meeting.

  2. Pick one meeting place

    Choose a spot everyone can reach. FEMA's family plan includes a neighborhood meeting place and says meeting places should be accessible to people with disabilities.10

  3. Hold a 30-minute meeting

    Agree on three jobs: check on each other, share news, keep watch. Keep it short so people come back.

  4. Fill in the block sheet

    For each home: adults, children, babies, elders, pets, medical needs, skills, and tools they are willing to lend.

  5. Draw the map

    Houses, the meeting place, water sources, and who checks on whom (buddy pairs).

  6. Choose a block captain and a backup

    The captain keeps the sheet. The backup keeps a copy in a different house.

  7. Set the next date

    Meet often at first, then settle into a fixed weekly time.

Keep the block sheet private

Medical details and supply lists stay on paper with the captain and the backup. Do not post them on the notice board or online. See Keeping a Low Profile.

Ask, do not demand

Let each household choose what to share. People who feel respected at the first meeting come to the second one.

📡 Talking without phones or power

Text instead of calling while the network still works. The FCC and FEMA advise texting for non-emergency messages, keeping calls short and not redialing again and again, because networks get congested in emergencies.9

Limiting calls also saves your phone battery.9 Then plan for no phones at all: after Maria, homes averaged 41 days without cell service.2

🔦 Watch rotas people can keep up First 30 days

A block watch is eyes and ears, not a fighting force. Its job is to notice, write it down, report, and wake people up when something is wrong.

The rota must be fair and simple, or it falls apart within weeks. Do the math before you start.

Rota math for your block

  • Person-shifts per day = number of shifts × people per shift. Six 4-hour shifts × 2 people = 12.
  • Shifts per adult = person-shifts per day ÷ adults on the rota. 12 ÷ 24 adults = 0.5, so one shift every 2 days.
  • With only 12 adults, everyone stands one 4-hour shift every day, on top of water, food and child care.

  1. Choose your posts

    One or two spots that see the street entrances and the meeting place. Fewer posts means a lighter rota.

  2. Always work in pairs

    One watches while the other writes or goes for help. Nobody stands a night shift alone.

  3. Keep a logbook

    Time, what you saw, what you did. Plain facts, no guesses about people.

  4. Hand over properly

    The next pair arrives a few minutes early and reads the log before the old pair leaves.

  5. Report, then rest

    Tell the captain anything unusual. Nobody stands two shifts in a row.

Do

  • Watch, write it down, report
  • Work in pairs with a whistle
  • Wake the captain when unsure
  • Rotate shifts fairly, including weekends
  • Give tired people a night off

Don't

  • Chase, follow or confront anyone
  • Punish or detain people yourselves
  • Stand a post alone at night
  • Talk about supplies at the post
  • Warm up with a grill or heater in a closed shed

Observe and report: never confront

The watch calls people together; it does not fight. Avoidance and calm talk come first. See De-escalation & Avoiding Violence.

Cold watch posts and carbon monoxide

Never run a generator, grill, camp stove or fuel heater in a shed, garage, tent or car to stay warm. Carbon monoxide (CO) is colorless and odorless, and sleeping people can die before they feel symptoms.21 Dress warmly instead, and swap shifts more often in the cold.

Women and girls

Put women on the planning team and let them say which posts, paths and pairings feel safe. Nobody, and especially no woman or girl, should walk alone at night to a post, water point or latrine. See Personal Safety for Women & Girls.

🧰 Sharing tools, skills and supplies Year 1

Not every house needs every tool. One shared, logged collection keeps each saw, ladder and wheelbarrow working for the whole block.

The same goes for skills. A nurse, a mechanic and a gardener on one street can cover gaps that no single household could.

Set up a tool library

  1. List what people will lend

    Each household writes down tools it is willing to share. Owners keep ownership.

  2. Mark every tool

    Paint, scratch or tag the owner's house number on the handle.

  3. Keep a sign-out log

    One notebook, kept by one keeper. Every loan gets a line.

  4. Set three rules

    Return it clean. Report damage the same day. Bring it back by the due date.

  5. Hold a repair day

    Once a month, sharpen, oil and fix the shared tools together.

🍲 Running a communal kitchen safely

One kitchen for several homes means one fire, one cook rota and one set of hygiene rules. It also means one mistake can make everyone sick at once.

Food safety numbers for a shared kitchen (USDA, FoodSafety.gov)
RuleMetricUS
Danger zone: bacteria grow fastest154–60°C40–140°F
Hold hot food at or above1560°C140°F
Keep cold food and coolers at or below15164°C40°F

Bacteria in the danger zone can double in as little as 20 minutes.15 Cook in batches you can serve hot, right away.

Plan portions with real numbers. Humanitarian food aid uses 2,100 kcal per person per day as its minimum planning figure.17 A kitchen feeding 30 people needs 30 × 2,100 = 63,000 kcal a day.

  1. Cook outdoors, away from homes

    Ready.gov says camp stoves and charcoal grills belong outdoors, at least 6 m (20 ft) from windows.1

  2. Wash hands at a set station

    Soap and water before cooking, after the latrine and after handling raw meat. See Handwashing & Personal Hygiene.

  3. Sick cooks stay out

    Anyone with diarrhea or vomiting does not cook or serve. Keep them on jobs away from food for a few more days after they recover.

  4. Serve hot, serve fast

    Keep food at 60°C (140°F) or above until it is served.15

  5. Do not keep risky leftovers

    Perishable food that sat out more than 2 hours, or 1 hour above 32°C (90°F), gets thrown out.1516

  6. Clean and dry everything

    Wash pots, ladles and boards with hot soapy water and let them dry in the air.

Disasters always cause epidemics.

A review in the CDC journal Emerging Infectious Diseases found that disasters which do not displace people rarely lead to outbreaks. The risk rises with crowding, poor water and sanitation, poor nutrition and lost access to health care.19 A clean, uncrowded shared kitchen helps keep that risk low.

Shared toilets need numbers too. UNHCR's target is 1 latrine per 20 people (or 1 per household), and no latrine should serve more than 50 people in the first phase of an emergency.20 Put latrines well away from the kitchen and water. See Toilets Without Water.

Babies, children and pregnant women

Children under 5, pregnant people and adults 65 and older are at higher risk from food poisoning.18 Serve them first, straight from the hot pot. Rope off the fire area so toddlers cannot reach it, and give one adult the job of watching children during cooking.

Dogs at the kitchen and meeting place

Keep dogs leashed and out of the cooking area. Feed them separately, away from people eating. Carbon monoxide poisons animals as well as people,21 so never shut a dog in a garage or shed with a running engine or heater.

Never cook indoors with a grill or camp stove

Ready.gov says to use generators, camp stoves and charcoal grills only outdoors, and never to use a gas stove or oven to heat a home.1 See Carbon Monoxide.

🧓 Nobody left behind: vulnerable neighbors

Make a private list of every neighbor who could die without help. Then make sure someone checks on each of them every day.

More than 3 million US Medicare beneficiaries have claims for electricity-dependent medical equipment. Counting dialysis, oxygen and home health services, it is more than 4.6 million.12

In a June 2024 CDC survey, 26% of adults with disabilities said they had nowhere to go in an evacuation, against 15% of adults without disabilities. 20% named the cost of evacuating, against 14%.13

Moving frail people can kill them too. Nearly 3.7 million people evacuated ahead of Hurricane Rita in 2005. Over 100 of at least 119 deaths were linked to the evacuation itself, including heat in vehicles and a nursing-home bus fire.14

The daily check-in walk

  1. Walk the route every morning

    Two people, same time each day. Look for the OK or HELP sign in each window.

  2. Knock at priority homes first

    Elders living alone and anyone on powered equipment. Ready.gov names older adults and young children as most at risk from extreme temperatures.1

  3. Talk, do not just wave

    Ask about warmth, water, food, medicines and sleep. Look inside if you can.

  4. Write it on the sheet

    Name, time, how they are, what they need.

  5. Act the same day

    Bring in the medic, move the person somewhere warmer or cooler, or call help if it exists.

Elders and disabled neighbors

After the 2021 Texas freeze, about two-thirds of the 246 deaths came from cold exposure.4 A warm shared room at night and a daily visit cost little. See Caring for Elderly & Disabled People.

Children: ID and a reunion plan

After Hurricanes Katrina and Rita in 2005, 5,192 children were reported missing. Reuniting the last of them with family took more than six months, until March 2006.2526 Give every child a card with their name, parents' names, house and the block meeting place. Ready.gov's kit list includes formula, bottles, diapers, wipes and diaper rash cream:11 pool spares on a shared baby shelf.

Dogs and pets

Temporary public shelters may not allow animals, and vet clinics and shelters scan microchips to find owners.24 Keep a "lost and found pets" corner on the notice board, with a photo of each block pet.

🩹 Train the block and keep it going Long haul

Every adult on the block should know how to stop bleeding and do CPR. Teach it at block meetings and practice it on a schedule. See CPR & Choking.

You need a certificate before you are allowed to do CPR.

An American Heart Association survey found many people still wrongly believe CPR requires special training.23 Learn it now, but never hold back in an emergency because you lack a card.

A generator is safe as long as it is outside.

After two 2005 hurricanes, at least 78 people were poisoned and 10 died from portable generators, and most cases involved generators outside but near the home.22 Keep them at least 6 m (20 ft) from windows, doors and vents,27 and count every house on the street, not just your own.

  1. Day 1

    Check on the neighbors on each side and across the street. Put up OK or HELP signs.

  2. Week 1

    First block meeting. Block sheet, map, captain, backup, meeting place.

  3. Week 2

    Watch rota starts. Daily check-in walk. Notice board up.

  4. Month 1

    Shared kitchen and latrine rules. Tool library log. Skills board.

  5. Year 1

    Monthly first aid practice. Rotate captains. Recount adults and redo the rota math.

  6. Year 5 and beyond

    Teenagers learn each role. Written rules and records outlast any one leader.

Practice this now while help still exists

Ask your local emergency management office whether it runs a Community Emergency Response Team (CERT) course, and ask your Red Cross chapter what first aid training it offers. Go with a neighbor. Then run one practice: a 30-minute block meeting and one full day of the check-in walk.

The 30-year view

Over decades, people tire, fall ill, move away and die. Rotate every role, write the rules down, and keep the block sheet updated each season. When disputes come, use agreed rules, not the loudest voice. See Group Rules, Decisions & Resolving Disputes.
