🚫 Rule one: every flame cooks outside First 72 h

Every cooking fire makes carbon monoxide (CO). The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) lists stoves, lanterns, grills, fireplaces and gas ranges among the things that make it whenever fuel burns.2

You cannot see or smell CO. It builds up in enclosed or partly enclosed spaces and poisons the people and animals who breathe it.2

Camp stoves and charcoal grills go outdoors only, at least 6 m (20 ft) from windows.1 A garage, carport, tent or camper is an enclosed or partly enclosed space, not "outdoors".23

If your gas range still works, use it for cooking only, then turn it off. Never use a gas stove or oven to heat the home.13

Outside but close is not enough

After Hurricanes Katrina and Rita in 2005, portable generators poisoned at least 78 people in Alabama and Texas and killed 10. Most of those generators were outside, but near the home.21

Stoves and grills make CO too.2 Keep the full 6 m (20 ft) from windows,1 and set up so the smoke blows away from the house.

Know the warning signs

CO poisoning causes headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain and confusion.3 CDC notes that people often describe these as "flu-like".2

If anyone feels this way while a stove, grill or fire is burning nearby, get everyone into fresh air first. Then get medical help.3

Fit CO alarms with battery backup,1 and replace each alarm every 5 years.2 The full story is in Carbon Monoxide: The Blackout Killer.

  1. Pick the spot

    Open air, at least 6 m (20 ft) from windows.1 Keep it away from doors and vents too.

  2. Check the wind

    Set up so smoke blows away from the house and from where people sit.

  3. Clear the ground

    Bare soil, gravel or paving. Nothing that can burn underneath or hanging overhead.

  4. Mark a no-go zone

    Lay a rope or a ring of stones around the cooker. Children and dogs stay outside it.

  5. Keep fire tools within reach

    A metal lid that fits your pan, a bucket of sand or soil, and a flashlight.

  6. Never leave it alone

    An adult stays with a lit stove or fire until it is fully out.

Rain is not a reason to cook inside

When the weather is too wet to cook outdoors, eat a no-cook meal instead. Keep a few days of ready-to-eat food for exactly this. See Long-Term Food Storage.

🔥 Your cooking options, side by side

You have five main ways to cook without the grid. Three of them burn fuel and make CO. A solar cooker and a retained-heat box burn nothing themselves, because CO comes from burning fuel.2

Camp stove (propane, butane or liquid fuel)

Best for: boiling water and quick meals in the first days and weeks. Rule: outdoors only, 6 m (20 ft) from windows.1

Limit: its fuel comes from stores. Fuel pumps are among the systems FEMA lists as hit by power outages (2025).13

Charcoal grill

Same rule as the camp stove: outdoors only, at least 6 m (20 ft) from windows.1 Never bring it indoors to keep warm or to finish cooking.

Once bought charcoal runs out, you can make your own from wood. See Charcoal, Lime, Ash & Useful Low-Tech Materials.

Wood fire and rocket stove

Wood is a cooking fuel you can gather locally, year after year. A rocket stove is a small stove with an insulated, L-shaped burn chamber that you feed with sticks.

Wood smoke still carries CO: CDC lists fireplaces and stoves among CO sources.2 Cook on wood outdoors. For indoor wood stoves and chimneys, read Heating With Wood. To light fires for decades, read Fire Starting Methods That Last Decades.

Solar cooker

No fuel, no flame, no CO.2 It cooks only while the sun shines on it.

Retained-heat box ("haybox")

You boil the pot on a stove, then let insulation finish the cooking. No flame goes in the box. The safety rules are in the no-flame section below.

Do

  • Cook in the open air, 6 m (20 ft) from windows1
  • Keep a lid on every pot
  • Use the sun on sunny days
  • Finish long dishes in an insulated box
  • Keep a battery CO alarm by every bed2

Don't

  • Use a grill or camp stove in a home, garage, tent or camper
  • Heat the house with a gas stove or oven1
  • Leave a fire alone
  • Let kids or dogs inside the no-go ring

⛽ Fuel: count it, store it, stretch it

Stored fuel is all you can count on. FEMA lists fuel pumps, lighting and payment systems among the systems hit by power outages (2025).13

Outages can last months. After Hurricane Maria in 2017, surveyed households in Puerto Rico went an average of 84 days without power.14 Hurricane Helene in 2024 cut power to 5.9 million customers across 10 states.15

So measure how much fuel your family uses per day, while you still have a scale and time to practice.

  1. Weigh the fuel

    Weigh a full canister, bag of charcoal or bundle of dry wood on a kitchen scale. Write it down.

  2. Boil 1 liter (about 1 quart)

    Lid on. Time how long it takes to reach a rolling boil.

  3. Weigh again

    The difference is your fuel per liter boiled.

  4. Cook a normal day

    Breakfast, dinner and drinking water. Weigh the fuel before and after.

  5. Do the math

    Fuel stored ÷ fuel per day = days of cooking you have.

  6. Post the number

    Write "days of fuel left" on the fuel shelf and update it every week.

Worked example (use your own numbers)

These numbers are made up to show the math. Say your test shows one day uses 300 g (0.66 lb) of fuel, and you have 10 kg (22 lb) stored.

10 kg ÷ 0.3 kg per day = about 33 days of cooking.

Store fuel away from water and food

Fuel vapors can pass through plastic. University of Nebraska–Lincoln Extension warns that gasoline, kerosene and pesticide vapors can penetrate plastic water containers.16 Keep stored water, and anything you eat or drink from, well away from fuel.

For storing and rotating liquid fuels, read Generators & Fuel Storage.

Fuel handling habits

  • Change or refill fuel canisters only when every flame nearby is out.
  • Close valves fully after each use.
  • Store fuel where children cannot reach it.
  • Keep matches and lighters in a closed metal tin.

The 30-year view

Bought fuel runs out first. Plan to move, step by step, to fuels you can renew: dry wood you cut and season, charcoal you make, and the sun.

Every liter of fuel you save with a lid, a solar cooker or an insulated box is a liter you still have next winter.

💧 Water first: the biggest fuel job

Boil drinking water at a rolling boil for 1 minute. Above 2,000 m (6,500 ft), boil it for 3 minutes.7

Longer is not needed. CDC's Yellow Book says boiling kills organisms quickly, and the full minute already adds a margin of safety.8 Every extra minute burns fuel you need later.

Boiling does not remove fuel or chemicals.7 For other methods, read Disinfecting Water.

You must boil drinking water for 10 minutes or more.

CDC says 1 minute at a rolling boil, or 3 minutes above 2,000 m (6,500 ft).7 The full minute already includes a safety margin.8

Cooking needs water too

Plan 3–6 L of water per person per day just for cooking. That is the Sphere humanitarian figure, on top of 2.5–3 L for drinking and food.9

The CDC home minimum of 1 gallon (3.8 L) per person per day covers drinking, cooking and brushing teeth.10 Sphere's cooking figure alone, 3–6 L, can use up that whole gallon.9

Cooking water for a family of 4

4 people × 3–6 L = 12–24 L (3.2–6.3 gal) a day, using the Sphere range.9

Over 30 days that is 360–720 L (95–190 gal), just for cooking.

Keep fuel for the baby's water

For powdered formula, CDC says to use bottled water until officials say tap water is safe. With no bottled water, boil water for 1 minute (3 minutes above 2,000 m or 6,500 ft).11

For babies under 2 months, premature babies or babies with weak immune systems, mix the formula with water that is still about 70°C (158°F).11 Cool the bottle before feeding. Set aside fuel that is only for this. See Feeding Babies When Formula Runs Out.

🌡️ Cook it safe: heat, time and leftovers

Bacteria grow fastest between 4°C and 60°C (40°F and 140°F). USDA calls this the "Danger Zone". In that range, bacteria can double in as little as 20 minutes.4

Keep hot food at 60°C (140°F) or hotter until you serve it.4

Cooked food left in the danger zone has 2 hours. If the air is above 32°C (90°F), it has 1 hour.5 After that, throw it out.

Never taste food to check whether it is safe. When in doubt, throw it out.6

  1. 0 min: off the heat

    Serve it now, or keep it at 60°C (140°F) or hotter.4

  2. 20 min

    In the danger zone, bacteria can already have doubled.4

  3. 1 hour

    Limit in hot weather, above 32°C (90°F).5

  4. 2 hours

    Limit in cooler weather. Throw out what is left.5

Leftover starches count too. FoodSafety.gov lists cooked pasta among foods to discard after more than 2 hours above 4°C (40°F) in a power outage.22

Cook one meal at a time

Without a fridge, cook only what your household will eat at that meal. Write the time on the pot lid with chalk or tape, so anyone can see when the 2-hour limit runs out.5 For storing food without power, read Food Safety Without a Refrigerator.

Get two food thermometers

A thermometer shows whether food is still at 60°C (140°F) or hotter.4 Buy a dial or glass one that needs no battery, plus a spare.

When food makes someone sick

Get medical help for these signs: bloody diarrhea, fever over 38.9°C (102°F), frequent vomiting, signs of dehydration, or diarrhea that lasts more than 3 days.12

Some people get much sicker: adults 65 and older, children under 5, pregnant people and people with weak immune systems.12 For fluids, read Diarrhea, Dehydration & Oral Rehydration.

📦 No-flame cooking: insulated boxes and the sun

Retained-heat ("haybox") cooking

Bring the pot to a full boil on a safe outdoor stove, then put the lidded pot into a well-insulated box. The insulation holds the heat in, so you stop burning fuel to keep it simmering.

The one rule: the food must stay at 60°C (140°F) or hotter. That is USDA's hot-holding temperature. Below it, bacteria grow fastest.4

  1. Build the box

    Use a box, basket or cooler. Line every side, the bottom and the top with thick insulation: blankets, pillows, a sleeping bag, straw or crumpled paper.

  2. Choose the pot

    A pot with a tight lid and handles, that fits the hollow with insulation pressed all around it.

  3. Boil it hard

    Bring the food to a full rolling boil on the outdoor stove. Stir so it is hot all the way through.

  4. Move it fast

    Lid on, straight into the box, insulation over the top. Close every gap.

  5. Leave it closed

    Do not open it to peek. Every opening lets heat out.

  6. Check before eating

    60°C (140°F) or hotter: serve it.4 Cooler, and for under 2 hours: boil it again, then eat. Longer, or you do not know: throw it out.56

Keep the box away from flames

Blankets, straw and paper burn. Take the pot off the stove before it goes into the box, and keep the box well away from the fire.

Test your box with water now

Boil 2 L (about 2 quarts) of water, box it, and check it with a thermometer every hour. Write down how many hours it stays at 60°C (140°F) or hotter.4

That is your box's safe time. Never leave food in it longer than that.

Beans and grains in the box

Always bring them to a full boil first. Do not leave them in the box overnight unless your water test shows the box holds 60°C (140°F) or hotter that long.4

Solar cookers

A solar cooker burns nothing, so it makes no CO.2 It needs direct sun and a cook who checks the food.

  • Set it up in full sun, outside the children's and dogs' play area.
  • Use a dark pot with a lid.
  • Turn the cooker to face the sun as it moves across the sky.
  • Do not stare into the shiny reflector.
  • Check the food with a thermometer before you eat it: 60°C (140°F) or hotter.4
  • If clouds come and the food cools, the 2-hour clock starts.5 Finish it on the stove.

🫘 Beans, rice and grains on less fuel

Humanitarian food planning starts at 2,100 kcal per person per day.19 The 2025–2030 Dietary Guidelines list beans and lentils among protein foods.20

Your fuel decides how often you can cook them. Use the habits below, then measure what works with your own stove and food.

Six fuel-saving habits

  1. One pot, one fire

    Cook beans, grain and vegetables together, or one after another on the same fire.

  2. Lid on, always

    Keep the lid on from the moment the water goes in.

  3. Boil, then box

    Bring the pot to a full boil, then finish the long, slow part in a retained-heat box, using the safety checks above.4

  4. Use only the water you need

    Every extra liter heated is fuel wasted. Cooking water is already 3–6 L per person per day.9

  5. Cook while the sun is out

    Put slow dishes in the solar cooker on clear days, and save stove fuel for water and bad weather.

  6. Cook only what you will eat

    Leftovers have 2 hours, or 1 hour above 32°C (90°F).5 Food you throw away is fuel thrown away.

Run a soak test now

Take two equal cups of the same dried beans. Soak one in clean, treated water overnight; leave the other dry.

Cook both on the same stove. Write down the minutes until soft and the fuel used for each. Now you know, for your beans and your stove, what soaking saves.

Soak and cook with safe water. CDC counts cooking in the clean water you need each day.10 If you are unsure about a source, treat it first with the methods above.

For growing and processing these crops, see Growing & Processing Grains and Beans.

👪 Children, elders, women and dogs at the cooking fire

The cooking spot puts flames, hot pots, fuel and CO in one place. Plan for each person who comes near it.

Babies and children

Infants are among those at higher risk from CO.2 Keep babies and young children indoors or well away from the cooker, never inside the no-go ring.

Give older children safe jobs away from the flame: carrying water, washing vegetables, setting out bowls. Cook full portions for children; FEMA and the American Red Cross say food may be rationed except for children and pregnant women.17 See Survival Skills Kids Can Learn by Age and Burns.

Elders and people with heart or lung disease

Older people and people with heart disease, anemia or breathing problems are at higher risk from CO.2 Seat them upwind and away from the smoke.

Adults 65 and older are also more likely to get seriously ill from food poisoning.12 Serve them food hot, straight from the pot.

Pregnancy

Pregnant people are in a higher-risk group for food poisoning.12 Give them freshly cooked, hot food, not leftovers.

Do not cut a pregnant woman's portion: FEMA and the Red Cross exclude pregnant women from food rationing.17 CDC also says to store extra water for pregnant women.10

Dogs

CO poisons animals as well as people.2 If the dog and people feel unwell at the same time while something is burning nearby, treat it as CO and get everyone into fresh air.3

Keep dogs out of the no-go ring, tied or shut away while you cook. For feeding dogs from the family pot, read Feeding Dogs Without Kibble.

If a fire gets away

Plan two ways out of every room. The National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) says to practice the plan twice a year, during the day and at night.18

You may have only 1 to 2 minutes to escape once a home fire starts. Get out and never go back inside.18

Use flashlights, not candles, for light while you cook and clean up after dark.1
