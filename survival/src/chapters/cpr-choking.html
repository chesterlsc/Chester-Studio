🆕 What changed in the 2025 guidelines

This chapter follows the newest rules. The American Heart Association (AHA) published its 2025 Guidelines for CPR and Emergency Cardiovascular Care (ECC) on October 22, 2025, in the journal Circulation.318

CPR means cardiopulmonary resuscitation. It is chest compressions, with or without rescue breaths, for a person whose heart has stopped pumping.1

The guideline for babies and children was written by the AHA together with the American Academy of Pediatrics (AAP).2 International experts in the International Liaison Committee on Resuscitation (ILCOR) reviewed the same science in 2025.7

🔎 Spot cardiac arrest and get help

Check fast, then act. A person in cardiac arrest does not respond and is not breathing normally, or is only gasping.1

  1. Make sure the scene is safe

    Look for danger to you first: traffic, fire, live wires, deep water, smoke. The AHA choking algorithm starts with this check too.10

  2. Tap and shout

    Tap their shoulders hard and shout, "Are you OK?" No answer means unresponsive.1

  3. Look at the chest and face

    No breathing, or only gasping, means start CPR.1

  4. Shout for help

    Send one person for help and for an automated external defibrillator (AED), if one exists.5

  5. Start pushing right away

    Do not wait for help to arrive. Begin chest compressions now.5

Gasping is not breathing

Slow, noisy gasps can happen after the heart stops. Treat "only gasping" the same as "not breathing" and start CPR.1

Do not hunt for a pulse

The public is not asked to check a pulse. Unresponsive and not breathing normally is enough to start.1 Feeling for a pulse wastes seconds.

In a long outage, "call for help" may mean a two-way radio, a whistle or a runner. Plan this now in Radios: Ham, GMRS, CB & Listening. If the person is breathing, use Assessing a Sick or Injured Person instead.

👐 Adult CPR, step by step

Your hands do the pumping. Good CPR is deep, fast, steady and has very few pauses.14

  1. Lay them on their back on something firm

    A floor or flat ground works. Compressions work best on a firm surface.1

  2. Place your hands

    Put the heel of one hand on the lower half of the breastbone, in the center of the chest. Put your other hand on top.1

  3. Lock your arms

    Kneel close. Keep your elbows straight and your shoulders right over your hands, so your body weight does the work.

  4. Push deep and fast

    Push at least 5 cm (2 in) but not more than 6 cm (2.4 in), 100 to 120 times a minute.1

  5. Let the chest rise all the way

    Do not lean on the chest between pushes. Full recoil lets the heart refill.1

  6. Add breaths if you can

    If you are trained and willing, give 2 breaths after every 30 pushes.1 If not, keep pushing without stopping.5

Push to a song

The AHA says people remember the right pace better when they train to the beat of "Stayin' Alive" or another familiar song at 100–120 beats a minute.6

Do

  • Push on the lower half of the breastbone
  • Use a firm floor or the ground
  • Let the chest come all the way back up
  • Swap pushers about every 2 minutes
  • Keep pauses for breaths very short

Don't

  • Lean on the chest between pushes
  • Push shallow "polite" compressions
  • Push deeper than 6 cm (2.4 in) on an adult
  • Stop to check for a pulse
  • Do CPR on a soft mattress

Every item above comes from the 2025 adult guideline.1 Keep any pause under 10 seconds.1

Swap pushers about every 2 minutes. That keeps compressions strong as people tire.1 Swap fast, during the breaths if you can.

UK guidance matches

The Resuscitation Council UK 2025 adult guideline also sets a depth of at least 5 cm and no more than 6 cm.8

CPR on women and girls

The hand position is the same for everyone: the center of the chest.5 Do not let worry about touching a woman's chest delay CPR. Push on the breastbone, between the breasts.

🫁 Hands-Only CPR or CPR with breaths?

Hands-Only CPR has two steps. Call for help (or send someone), then push hard and fast in the center of the chest.5

The AHA recommends it for teens and adults who suddenly collapse outside a hospital, such as at home, at work or in a park.5

Some people need breaths as well. The AHA recommends compressions plus breaths for all infants (up to age 1), children (up to puberty), drowning victims, drug overdose victims and people who collapse from breathing problems.5

If you are trained and willing, give 30:2 to adults too.1 ILCOR still suggests the 30:2 ratio in its 2025 review.7

How to give rescue breaths

  1. Open the airway

    Tilt the head back with one hand on the forehead. Lift the chin with two fingers of the other hand.1

  2. Seal your mouth over theirs

    For an adult or child, pinch the nose shut. For a baby, cover the mouth and nose with your mouth.2

  3. Breathe for 1 second

    Blow just enough to see the chest rise.1

  4. Give a second breath, then push again

    Go straight back to compressions. The two breaths should take less than 10 seconds.1

Opioid overdose

For a suspected opioid overdose, the 2025 guidelines include naloxone use by the public.3 Give naloxone if you have it, and give CPR with breaths.5 See Poisoning & Toxic Exposures.

You need a certificate before you are allowed to do CPR.

Hands-Only CPR is two steps: call for help, then push hard and fast in the center of the chest.5 An AHA consumer survey found many people still wrongly believe CPR requires special training.13

CPR on TV shows you how it is done.

The AHA reports that TV depictions of Hands-Only CPR are often misleading.12 Learn from the official guideline numbers and hands-on practice instead.

Get these before you need them

  • A CPR face shield or pocket mask for every first aid kit
  • A CPR training manikin, adult and infant if you can
  • Naloxone, if anyone in your group uses opioids, through a doctor or pharmacy where it is legal
  • A printed copy of this chapter in each kit

Alone with no phone? If you did not see the child collapse, give about 2 minutes of CPR before you leave to get help.2

Infant CPR (under 1 year)

  1. Lay the baby on a firm, flat surface

    A table or the floor works. Compressions need a firm surface.2

  2. Wrap your hands around the chest

    Put both thumbs side by side on the center of the chest, just below the nipple line. Wrap your fingers around the back.2

  3. Or use the heel of one hand

    If your hands cannot reach around the chest, push with the heel of one hand.2

  4. Push about 4 cm (1.5 in)

    That is at least one third of the chest depth, at 100–120 a minute. Let the chest rise fully.2

  5. Give gentle breaths

    Cover the mouth and nose. Puff just enough to make the chest rise. Use 30:2 alone, 15:2 with two rescuers.2

Push on a baby's chest with two fingers.

The 2025 guidelines removed the two-finger method because it often failed to reach the right depth.29 Use two thumbs with your hands around the chest. If you cannot reach around, use the heel of one hand.2

Newborns at birth are different

A baby who needs help in the first minutes after birth follows a separate neonatal guideline. There, the two-thumb method with hands wrapped around the chest is preferred.11 See Newborn Care: The First 28 Days.

Practice on a doll, not a baby

Never practice compressions or back blows on a real child. The AHA's Infant CPR Anytime kit has a practice infant manikin and was updated for the 2025 guidelines.15

🆘 Choking: adults and children over 1

Severe choking is silent. The person cannot speak, cough or breathe. They may clutch their throat with their hands.1

Mild choking is noisy. If they can cough hard or speak, let them keep coughing and stay with them. Act if the cough becomes weak or stops.8

  1. Ask "Are you choking?"

    If they nod and cannot speak, act now. Tell them you are going to help.

  2. Shout for help

    Send someone for help. Do not leave the person.10

  3. Give 5 back blows

    Stand behind and to the side. Support their chest with one hand and lean them forward. Strike firmly between the shoulder blades with the heel of your other hand.8

  4. Give 5 abdominal thrusts

    Stand behind them and wrap your arms around the waist. Put a fist just above the belly button, well below the breastbone. Grab it with your other hand and pull sharply in and up.1

  5. Repeat 5 and 5

    Keep alternating until the object comes out or the person stops responding.3

  6. If they go limp, start CPR

    Lower them to the ground and begin compressions. Each time you open the airway, look in the mouth. Remove an object only if you can see it.117

Back blows are outdated. Only use abdominal thrusts (the "Heimlich").

Since 2025 the AHA says to alternate 5 back blows with 5 abdominal thrusts for awake adults and children.3 The UK guideline also starts with back blows.8

Pregnant and choking

Do not press on a pregnant belly. Give chest thrusts instead of abdominal thrusts: hands on the center of the breastbone, pulling straight back.110

Larger or seated people

If you cannot get your arms around someone's waist, use chest thrusts instead.1 See Caring for Elderly & Disabled People.

No blind finger sweeps

Do not poke your fingers into the throat to search for the object. You can push it deeper. Remove it only if you can see it.12

🍼 Choking: babies under 1 year

Babies get back blows and chest thrusts only. Abdominal thrusts are not recommended for infants.3

  1. Lay the baby face down on your forearm

    Hold the jaw with your hand to support the head. Keep the head lower than the chest. Rest your arm on your thigh.2

  2. Give 5 back blows

    Strike firmly between the shoulder blades with the heel of your hand.3

  3. Turn the baby face up

    Support the back of the head and sandwich the baby between your arms. Keep the head lower than the chest.2

  4. Give 5 chest thrusts

    Use the heel of one hand on the center of the chest, just below the nipple line.32

  5. Repeat 5 and 5

    Keep going until the object comes out or the baby stops responding.3

  6. If the baby goes limp, start infant CPR

    Use the infant CPR steps above. Look in the mouth before each set of breaths and remove only what you can see.2

Never give abdominal thrusts to a baby

For any baby under 1 year, use back blows and chest thrusts only.3 The chest thrusts now use the heel of one hand, not two fingers.2

Prevent it first

Rescue is the backup plan. The prevention side (food sizes, small objects, supervision at meals) is in Keeping Kids Safe: Drowning, Fire, Poison, Strangers.

Dogs are not small people

All the numbers in this chapter come from human guidelines.12 Dog CPR and dog choking use different positions and methods. A frightened, choking dog may also bite. Learn the dog methods in Dog First Aid, and keep children back.

⏱️ How long to keep going, and when to stop

Keep pushing until help takes over. The AHA tells the public to continue chest compressions until help arrives.5

If an AED arrives, turn it on and follow the voice prompts.5 Keep pushing while someone else sets it up.

If the person wakes, moves with purpose or starts breathing normally, stop compressions. Stay with them and watch their breathing closely.1 A breathing person who is not awake can be rolled onto their side in the recovery position.8

You may stop CPR when

  • The person shows clear signs of life, such as normal breathing or purposeful movement1
  • A trained helper takes over5
  • An AED is ready: follow its voice prompts5
  • The scene becomes unsafe for you10
  • You are too exhausted to continue and no one can take over8

The public guidance gives no time limit. It says to keep going until help arrives.5 It does not set a rule for a place where no help can come. Decide in advance, as a household, who will make that call.

Use teams. Swap pushers about every 2 minutes so compressions stay strong for as long as you continue.1

The 30-year view

CPR is one link in a chain of survival that the AHA now uses for every age and setting.3 If no ambulance or hospital can be reached, the links after CPR are not available. Back blows, thrusts and CPR need nothing but trained hands, so train every capable person.

Talk about wishes now

Some older or very ill people have clear wishes about resuscitation. Ask while they can tell you, and write it down. When CPR does not work, see Death, Dying & Handling the Dead Safely.
