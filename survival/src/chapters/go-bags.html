🎒 What a go-bag is for First 72 h

A go-bag is the bag you grab when you must leave home fast. Fire, flood, a chemical leak or a damaged house can all force you out in minutes. The American Red Cross says every household should pack one.3

It is not your home supply. The Red Cross uses two sizes: a 3-day supply to take when you evacuate and a 2-week supply to keep at home.2 Your long-term stores stay at home. See Storing Water and Long-Term Food Storage.

Ready.gov says to keep supplies at home, at work and in the car.1 At work, be ready to shelter for at least 24 hours, and keep comfortable walking shoes with your supplies.1

Ready.gov also says to store items in airtight plastic bags and keep the whole kit in one or two easy-to-carry containers.1 For a family on foot, that means one backpack for each person who can carry one.

Whether to leave at all is a separate decision. It is covered in Stay or Go?

A go-bag should hold everything you need to survive.

Official lists size it for 3 days of evacuation. The 2-week supply stays at home.2 Carrying heavy loads causes injuries: foot blisters, stress fractures and back strains are among the most common.10

Do

  • Pack one bag per person who can carry one
  • Keep bags in one known spot near the exit
  • Weigh every bag on a bathroom scale
  • Put the heavy shared gear on the strongest adults

Don't

  • Pack one giant family bag that only one person can lift
  • Hide bags where only one person knows to look
  • Guess the weight
  • Borrow from the bags for daily use and forget to refill

Water is the heavy part

One liter of water weighs 1 kg (2.2 lb). So 1 US gallon (3.8 L) weighs about 3.8 kg (8.3 lb).

The Red Cross 3-day evacuation supply is 3 gallons (11.4 L) per person.2 That is 11.4 kg (25 lb) of water for one person, before food, clothes or anything else.

For a 60 kg (132 lb) adult, that water alone is 19% of body weight. Compare that with the limits in the next section.

Split the water load

Ready.gov says to keep a kit in the car too.1 If you leave by vehicle, the extra water rides there. On foot, carry what you can and pack a way to treat water you find. See Disinfecting Water and Water Filters.

Women and girls

Ready.gov lists feminine supplies and personal hygiene items in the basic kit.1 Pack at least 3 days of products for every girl and woman who menstruates, plus spares and a few sealable bags for used products.

Reusable options are in Periods Without Stores. Safety while displaced is in Personal Safety for Women & Girls.

Start with what you already have

The Red Cross suggests starting with what you already own, including a backpack or duffel bag, then adding a few useful items as your budget allows.4 A school backpack with two wide straps is a fine first go-bag.

⚖️ How heavy is too heavy?

Weigh every bag, then compare it with the carrier's body weight. Most research on carrying loads comes from soldiers. It gives clear ceilings you can use.

Adults

A U.S. Army report gives the Infantry School's recommended limits: a fighting load of 22 kg (48 lb), about 30% of body weight, and an approach-march load of 33 kg (72 lb), about 45%.11

A 2018 study in Military Medicine notes that loads of 30% of body mass or less are recommended to avoid drops in performance.9 It tested soldiers on a 10 km (6.2 mi) mixed-terrain course carrying 30% and 45% of body mass.9

It found that standard equations underestimate the energy cost of carrying loads over rough ground.9 A pack that feels fine on a flat road costs more on trails, mud or rubble.

These numbers come from trained, fit soldiers. Treat 30% as a ceiling for healthy adults, not a target.

Children

The National Safety Council (NSC) says a child's backpack should weigh no more than 5–10% of the child's body weight.8

KidsHealth, from the Nemours children's health system, gives a wider range of 10–20%. Its example: an 80 lb (36 kg) child should carry no more than 8–16 lb (3.6–7.3 kg).7 For a long walk, use the lower number.

What too much weight does

A 2004 review of soldier load carriage lists the common injuries: foot blisters, stress fractures, back strains, metatarsalgia (pain in the ball of the foot), rucksack palsy (numbness or weakness from strap pressure on nerves) and knee pain.10

The same review says loads are easier to carry when they are lighter, better distributed and carried in better-designed equipment.10

Narrow straps cause numbness

Tight, narrow straps that dig into the shoulders can cause tingling, numbness and weakness in the arms and hands.7 Choose wide, padded straps and loosen them if hands start to tingle.

  1. Weigh the person

    Step on a bathroom scale without the bag. Write the number down.7

  2. Weigh them wearing the bag

    The difference is the pack weight. Do this for every bag, including the dog's.

  3. Work out the limit

    Multiply body weight by 0.10 for a child or 0.30 for an adult ceiling. Example: 60 kg × 0.30 = 18 kg (40 lb).

  4. Cut the weight

    Remove duplicates. Move water to the car kit. Give heavy shared items to the strongest adults.

  5. Walk with it on real ground

    Rough ground costs more energy than standard equations predict, so test on hills, grass or gravel.9

Walk your route with the bags

Once a season, walk part of your evacuation route with everyone wearing their bag. Note who struggles and rebalance the loads. Drills and meeting points are in The Family Emergency Plan.

🧒 Children's go-bags

A child who walks well can carry a small, light bag of their own. It holds personal items. Adults carry the water and heavy gear.

Ready.gov lists books, games, puzzles or other activities for children.1 The Red Cross adds games and activities, plus family and emergency contact information.2

  • A contact card with parents' names, phone numbers and an out-of-area contact
  • A complete change of clothes and sturdy shoes1
  • A whistle to signal for help1
  • A small flashlight, a water bottle and a few snacks
  • One comfort item and one small game or book

Fit rules

Use two wide shoulder straps. Bags slung over one shoulder, across the chest, or with only one strap do not spread the weight as well.7

The pack should rest evenly in the middle of the back and should not sag down to the buttocks.7

Lighten or adjust the pack if a child struggles to put it on or take it off, has back pain, or leans forward to carry it.7

Children grow out of their bags

Clothes, shoes and safe pack weight all change as a child grows. Ready.gov says to re-think your needs every year and update the kit as your family changes.1 Re-weigh the child and the bag at every check.

Skills children can learn by age are in Survival Skills Kids Can Learn by Age.

👶 The baby's bag (carried by an adult)

A baby cannot carry anything, so the baby's supplies add to an adult's load. If you can, have one adult carry the baby and another carry the baby's bag.

Ready.gov lists infant formula, bottles, diapers, wipes and diaper rash cream.1 The Red Cross lists bottles, formula, baby food and diapers.2

  • 3 days of formula or baby food, plus bottles and a way to clean them
  • 3 days of diapers, wipes and diaper rash cream
  • Spare clothes and a warm layer
  • A sling or soft carrier, so your hands stay free
  • The baby's medicines, a thermometer and a copy of the vaccination record

Count the baby in the carrier's load. Weigh yourself holding the baby and wearing the bag, then compare the difference with your 30% ceiling.9

Full lists and safe feeding

Quantities for 2 weeks, 3 months and 1 year are in The Baby & Toddler Prep Kit. Safe formula preparation without power is in Feeding Babies When Formula Runs Out.

🧓 Elderly and disabled family members

Pack the small medical items people forget. The Red Cross lists hearing aids with extra batteries, glasses, contact lenses, syringes and a cane.2

Ready.gov adds prescription eyeglasses, contact lens solution, prescription medicines, and non-prescription medicines such as pain relievers, anti-diarrhea medicine, antacids or laxatives.1

How many days of medicine?

The current Red Cross survival kit list says a 7-day supply of medications and medical items.2 Its power outage checklist says the same.5

Another Red Cross emergency supply checklist says a 1-month supply.6 Ask the doctor or pharmacist how to build an emergency supply. See Chronic Conditions Without Pharmacies.

Pack a written medication list with medical information. The Red Cross lists it among the documents to copy.2

If they cannot carry the bag

Weigh the bag while they wear it. Watch for the same warning signs used for children: struggling to put it on or off, back pain, or leaning forward.7 If you see them, move the weight to someone else.

Mobility aids, oxygen and dementia care are covered in Caring for Elderly & Disabled People.

🐕 The dog's go-bag

Your dog needs its own supplies. The Red Cross lists pet supplies: collar, leash, ID, food, carrier and bowl.2 Ready.gov adds pet food and extra water for your pet.1

Pack current photos of you with your pet.3 If you are separated, a photo helps you show that the dog is yours.

  • Collar with ID tag, plus a spare leash
  • 3 days of food in an airtight bag
  • Water and a collapsible bowl
  • Any medicines your dog takes
  • Copies of vaccination and medical records
  • Waste bags

The dog's food and water add to an adult's load. Weigh them into someone's budget.

📄 Documents and cash

Pack copies of documents, sealed against water. The Red Cross lists copies of these: medication list and medical information, proof of address, deed or lease, passports, birth certificates and insurance policies.2

Ready.gov lists copies of insurance policies, identification and bank account records, saved electronically or in a waterproof, portable container.1

FEMA's financial first aid kit

The US Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) and Operation HOPE publish the Emergency Financial First Aid Kit (EFFAK) for free.12 It has sections for household information, professional advisors and health care providers, and a checklist of legal documents and financial statements.12

Its checklist includes items such as military ID and discharge papers (DD 214). If a document is missing, it says to contact the company or agency for a copy.13 Ready.gov's financial preparedness page links the kit and its forms.14

  1. Gather

    Use the EFFAK checklist to find each document.13 Ask the agency for copies of anything missing.

  2. Copy

    Photocopy each document. Add the family contact list and a current photo of each pet with you.3

  3. Seal

    Put the copies in airtight plastic bags or a waterproof container.1

  4. Split

    Give a packet to each adult bag, so one lost bag does not lose everything.

  5. Back up

    Ready.gov mentions saving records electronically.1 Keep paper as well, because phones need charging.

Cash

Both official lists include cash. Ready.gov lists cash or traveler's checks; the Red Cross lists extra cash.12

Pack small bills

Ready.gov warns that ATMs and card machines may not work in a power outage.15 Small bills mean you do not depend on anyone having change. Split the cash between adult bags.

Deeds, birth records and identity papers matter for decades, long after phones fail. See Preserving Knowledge & Records for paper and ink that last.

🔁 Checking and rotating bags

A bag only works if what is inside still works. Ready.gov's maintenance advice is short:1

  • Keep canned food in a cool, dry place
  • Store boxed food in tightly closed plastic or metal containers
  • Replace expired items as needed
  • Re-think your needs every year and update the kit as your family's needs change

Make sure every family member knows where the kits are kept.1

  1. Today

    Start with what you already have, including a backpack or duffel bag.4

  2. Then

    Add a few useful items at a time, as your budget allows.4

  3. Each check

    Replace expired items.1 Write the earliest expiry date on a tag on the outside.

  4. Every year

    Re-think everyone's needs and update the bags.1

  5. After any use

    Refill the bag before you put it away.

  6. Growth spurt

    Re-weigh the child and the bag against the 10% limit.8

The 30-year view: bags without stores

When stores are gone, you cannot buy replacements. Eat bag food at home before it expires and refill from your home stores. Choose bag items that do not expire, such as tools, whistles, cups and clothing.

The bag's job stays the same for decades: 3 days away from home. What changes is the family: children grow, elders need more help, and dogs age.
