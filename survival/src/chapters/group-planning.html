🏚️ Why one household struggles alone Year 1

Long outages last months, not days. After Hurricane Maria hit in 2017, it took about 11 months before power was restored to the whole of Puerto Rico.7

The people with the least suffered the most. A George Washington University study, commissioned by Puerto Rico's governor, counted 2,975 excess deaths from September 2017 to February 2018. The poor and the elderly were at the highest risk.5

A household has a fixed number of hands. Every hour spent hauling water is an hour not spent cooking, nursing, teaching or keeping watch.

The daily workload, in numbers

Water is the heaviest daily job. UNHCR plans for 20 liters (about 5 gallons) per person per day for household use and personal hygiene.1 One liter of water weighs 1 kilogram (2.2 lb).

For a family of 5, that is 100 liters (26 gallons), or 100 kg (220 lb), every single day. Over a year it adds up to 36,500 liters (about 9,600 gallons).

Food is the second big job. The 2010 US Dietary Guidelines estimate that adult women need about 1,600–2,400 kilocalories (kcal) a day and adult men about 2,000–3,000, depending on age and activity.10 Two adults eating 2,000 kcal each need about 1.46 million kcal a year.

Then add cooking, firewood, washing, toilets, gardening, night watch, child care and nursing the sick. Each of those jobs takes hours every day. A bigger group shares them out.

Most households have no plan yet

In a June 2024 survey by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), only 46% of US adults without a disability and 48% of adults with a disability had an emergency plan.8 The Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) 2023 national survey found that 37% of adults made a plan in the past year, down from 41% in 2022.11

So more than half of US adults had no emergency plan in 2024. Do not assume your neighbors have supplies or skills. Ask. How to start that conversation is in Organizing Neighbors: Mutual Aid & Watch.

The 30-year view

Over 30 years, every person in your group changes role. Babies become workers. Strong adults become elders who need care. Review your group plan every year, the same way Ready.gov tells you to re-think your emergency kit every year.12

📋 The skills inventory: who can do what

Write down every person's skills, honestly. A paper inventory shows you the gaps before an emergency does.

Use six areas: medical, food, mechanical, child care, security and teaching. For each person, mark one of three levels: can lead, can back up, or is learning.

  1. List every person

    Include babies, elders, guests and dogs. Everyone has needs. Most people also have skills.

  2. Ask, then watch

    Ask each adult what they can do. Then watch them do it once: dress a wound, fix a leak, cook a meal for ten.

  3. Fill the grid

    One row per person, one column per skill area. Mark lead, backup or learning in each box.

  4. Circle the gaps

    A column with no lead is your top training priority. A column with only one name means one illness can stop that job.

  5. Train to fill them

    Book classes while instructors and hospitals still exist. Start with the table below.

  6. Review every year

    People age, move away and learn new things. Redo the grid on the same date each year.12

🩺 Medical: the skill everyone needs some of

Bleeding control is a course anyone can take. The Stop the Bleed course teaches direct pressure, wound packing and tourniquets. The American College of Surgeons counts more than 5 million people trained.2

Children 12 and older can learn CPR, according to the American Heart Association's 2025 guidelines, published on October 22, 2025.13 Train your teenagers, not just the adults.

Everyone acts only within their training. The Red Cross first aid steps say to give care based on what you find, within your training.14 Your grid tells the group who that is.

🍲 Food and 🔧 mechanical

The food lead keeps people from getting sick. They know rules like these: a closed fridge keeps food safe for about 4 hours without power, a full freezer about 48 hours and a half-full one about 24.15 More is in Food Safety Without a Refrigerator.

The mechanical lead keeps engines and fuel from killing anyone. Generators run outdoors only, more than 20 feet (6 meters) from windows, doors and vents.16

Never run a generator indoors

Never use a generator inside a home or garage. Fit battery-powered carbon monoxide (CO) alarms and replace them every 5 years.16 Make CO safety part of the mechanical lead's job, and teach every backup.

👶 Child care, 🛡️ security and 📚 teaching

Child care is a full job, not a side task. The person minding small children should not also be on watch or at the stove.

In this book, security means awareness, deterrence and calm talk. The security lead runs the watch rota and knows how to talk a tense person down. See De-escalation & Avoiding Violence.

Teaching keeps skills alive. Every lead teaches a backup. Children also need reading and math every week.

🔄 Roles, backups and rotation

Every job gets a lead and a named backup. The National Fire Protection Association (NFPA) home fire escape advice assigns a helper to each person who needs help getting out, plus a backup in case the helper is not home.3 Use the same idea for every job in your group.

The backup steps in when the lead is sick, injured, asleep or away. Write both names on the rota so nobody has to guess.

Rotate the hardest jobs

Swap heavy and light jobs on a set day each week. Water hauling, night watch, nursing and small-child care are the hardest. Nobody should be stuck with one of them for months.

Match the job to the body. Pregnant women, elders and people getting over an illness can lead planning, teaching, record keeping and inventory while others carry loads.

Do

  • Write the rota on paper where everyone sees it
  • Name a backup for every job
  • Rotate the hard jobs on a set day each week
  • Pair each learner with a lead
  • Hold a short meeting every week

Don't

  • Let one person be the only medic or mechanic
  • Leave the same person on night watch for weeks
  • Give child care to someone who is also on watch
  • Hand out jobs by habit instead of by skill
  • Keep the plan only in one person's head

The person who never stops

Watch for the lead who skips meals, sleep and their turn off. Take them off the rota for a day and let the backup run the job. Signs that someone is not coping are in Mental Health: Grief, Trauma & Suicide Prevention.

Different brains, different roles

Give each person roles with a clear checklist and a result they can see. How to match roles to people with ADHD or autism is in Surviving With ADHD, Autism & Different Brains.

👵 Elders, disabled people, children and dogs come first

Build the plan around the people who cannot fend for themselves. Ready.gov warns that older adults and young children are vulnerable to extreme temperatures, and tells you to check on neighbors during an outage.4

Elderly and disabled people

Disabled adults face bigger barriers to leaving. In the June 2024 CDC survey, 26% of adults with disabilities said they had nowhere to go, against 15% of adults without. 20% named the cost of evacuating, against 14%.8

They had also lived through more disasters: 80% of adults with disabilities had experienced a natural disaster, against 70% without.8

Millions depend on power to stay well. More than 3 million US Medicare beneficiaries have claims for electricity-dependent medical equipment, and more than 4.6 million when dialysis, oxygen and home health services are included.9

  • Name a lead carer and a backup for each person who needs help.3
  • Make meeting places reachable with a wheelchair or walker. FEMA's family plan says to make them accessible for people with disabilities.17
  • List every powered device, medicine and supply each person uses, and how long it lasts without power. Ready.gov tells you to plan for medical devices that need power.4

Elders hold skills too

Put elders' skills in the inventory, not just their needs. Teaching, mending, cooking, record keeping and minding children are jobs that need no heavy lifting. Care details are in Caring for Elderly & Disabled People.

Babies and children

Children are not small adults in the plan

FEMA and the Red Cross say healthy people can survive on half their usual food for an extended period, but food should not be rationed for children and pregnant women.18

Name one adult responsible for each child in any emergency, plus a backup. Reunion plans are in The Family Emergency Plan.

Children grow into helpers. Teenagers 12 and older can learn CPR.13 Age-by-age jobs are in Survival Skills Kids Can Learn by Age.

Women and girls

Store extra water for pregnant women: the CDC names them among those who need more.19 Do not ration a pregnant woman's food.18

Make sure women and girls help decide where toilets, water points and sleeping areas go. Use a buddy for night trips. See Personal Safety for Women & Girls.

Dogs and other pets

Ready.gov says to include pets in your family emergency plan and to have your vet microchip them.20

Do not count on a public shelter for your dog. The FDA warns that temporary public shelters may not allow animals.6 FEMA's 2024 Pet Preparedness Toolkit says to find pet-friendly shelters before an emergency.21

Every dog gets a lead and a backup

Name who feeds, waters, walks and treats each dog. The CDC says to store extra water for pets.19 Count dogs in your water and food math. See Dogs in a Long Emergency.

👥 How big should your group be? Do the math

This book does not give a magic number. Every extra person adds hands and adds needs. Do your own arithmetic with official planning figures.

Three figures set the floor for any group:

  • Water: 20 liters (about 5 gallons) per person per day for household use and hygiene.1 The CDC says to store at least 1 gallon (3.8 liters) per person per day for emergencies.19
  • Toilets: UNHCR's target is 1 latrine per 20 people, or 1 per household. No latrine should serve more than 50 people in the first phase of an emergency.22
  • First aid: The Red Cross lists kit contents sized for a family of four.23

🤝 Forming the group and practicing together First 30 days

Start small and start now. A group is easier to build while phones, shops and classes still work.

  1. Start with who you know

    Family first, then your nearest neighbors, then friends who have skills you lack.

  2. Talk about needs first

    Each household lists its people, health needs, medicines, pets and supplies.

  3. Fill the skills grid together

    Agree on a lead and a backup for every job. Write the gaps down.

  4. Write simple agreements

    Who decides what, how work and food are shared, and how disputes are settled. Put them on paper.

  5. Practice together

    The Red Cross advises practicing your plan at least twice a year.24 NFPA advises fire drills twice a year, once by day and once at night.3

  6. Review every year

    Update the grid, the rota and the numbers. Replace expired supplies.12

Run a 24-hour no-power weekend

Pick a weekend. Use only stored water and food, run the rota and stand a night watch. Write down every problem, then fix one each week.

  • One family-size first aid kit per 4 people, checked for expiry dates23
  • Battery-powered CO alarms, replaced every 5 years16

⏳ How the group changes over 30 years Long haul

Your group ages one year every year. Plan the roles for the people you will have, not only the people you have today.

  1. Year 0

    Babies and toddlers need full-time care. Older adults and young children are most at risk from heat and cold.4

  2. Year 1

    Every lead has a trained backup. Every hard job rotates. The skills grid has no empty columns.

  3. Year 10

    A child who was 2 at the start is now 12: old enough to learn CPR under the 2025 guidelines.13 Teenagers become backups.

  4. Year 20

    Today's 50-year-olds are 70. Move them from heavy work to teaching, records and child minding.

  5. Year 30

    Children born after the outage are adults and leads. Skills must be taught, not just stored on paper.

How to pass skills on is in Passing On Skills to the Next Generation. Which trades to rebuild first is in Rebuilding Local Trades & Economy.

  • Circle every skill with no lead, or with only one person
  • Book a Stop the Bleed, CPR or first aid class for at least two adults
  • Sign up children 12 and older for a CPR class
