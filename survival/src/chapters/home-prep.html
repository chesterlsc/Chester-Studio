🏠 Your house has to stand on its own First 72 h

In a long outage, your home must keep you warm, keep fire out and hold your water. This chapter shows how to set it up before you need it.

In February 2021, more than 4.5 million people in Texas lost power, some for up to 4 days.11 Texas later linked 246 deaths to the storm. About two-thirds were from cold exposure, mostly hypothermia.9

The water failed too. Water system problems affected more than 14 million Texans during the same storm.12

Outages can last for months. After Hurricane Maria in 2017, a survey of 3,299 Puerto Rico households found that homes went an average of 84 days without power by the end of 2017.10

Hurricane Helene, 2024

Helene left 5.9 million customers without power across 10 states.13 That was one storm, in one recent year.

Plan to live at home for at least 2 weeks. The American Red Cross advises a 2-week supply for home and a 3-day supply if you must leave.14 For a 30-year outage, that is only the base you build on.

Water comes first: see Storing Water for Months & Years. If your house is not safe to stay in, read Stay or Go?

🛋️ Choose one warm, safe room First 72 h

Live, sleep and keep watch in one room. Close off the rest of the house and use it for storage.

For chemical and radiation emergencies, the CDC's shelter-in-place advice is an interior room with no or few windows, with everyone inside, pets included.1 Pick your warm room by the same rule, and one room can do both jobs.

Add one rule from fire safety: know two ways out of every room.2 In a small room, plan on the door plus a window that opens.

Set up the room

  1. Pick it today

    Walk the house with the table above. Write the room into your Family Emergency Plan.

  2. Close off the rest

    Shut the doors to rooms you are not using. Lay rolled towels along the gaps under them.

  3. Cover the windows at night

    Hang blankets or heavy curtains, but keep the exit window easy to open. Ready.gov's basic kit includes plastic sheeting and duct tape.16

  4. Insulate the floor

    Put rugs, mats or folded blankets under every sleeping place.

  5. Fit the alarms

    Add a CO alarm with battery backup.3 Add a battery smoke alarm too.

  6. Move everyone in

    Babies, elders and sick people get the warmest spots, away from any stove or heater. Dogs sleep inside too.

Stop drafts and cold air

Close and lock every window and outside door. These are among the CDC's shelter-in-place steps for keeping outside air out.1

Close the fireplace damper, which the CDC also lists.1 Do it only when the fireplace is cold, with no fire or embers left.

Tape plastic sheeting over windows you will not use as an exit. Ready.gov's basic kit includes plastic sheeting and duct tape.16 Leave the exit window clear so it opens in seconds.

Any flame changes the rules

Never seal a room around a flame or an engine. Before you use any stove, heater, lamp or generator for warmth, read Carbon Monoxide: The Blackout Killer. For warmth with no heat at all, see Staying Warm Without Heat.

Babies and small children

Ready.gov warns that young children and older adults are vulnerable to extreme temperatures.3 Give small children a warm place in the warm room, where an adult can see them.

For safe sleep and keeping newborns warm, see Newborn Care.

Elders and people on medical devices

Check on older neighbors. Ready.gov lists older adults as vulnerable to extreme temperatures.3 More than 3 million US Medicare beneficiaries have claims for medical equipment that runs on electricity.17

Ready.gov says to plan now for any medical device that needs power.3 See Caring for Elderly & Disabled People.

Dogs come inside

The CDC's shelter-in-place advice brings pets into the room with you.1 Ready.gov says to include pets in your family plan, with a bowl and several days of water in their kit.18

Temporary public shelters may not take animals.19 Your warm room may be your dog's only shelter. See Dogs in a Long Emergency.

Hot climate?

In the tropics you may need a cool room more than a warm one. The safety rules stay the same: two ways out, working alarms, and the generator far from the house. See Staying Cool Without Air Conditioning.

🔥 Heat and light without fire or fumes First 72 h

Use flashlights, not candles. It is one of Ready.gov's basic power outage rules.3 For lamps and lanterns that last for decades, see Light Without the Grid.

Never heat your home with a gas stove or oven. Ready.gov and the CDC both say so.34

Fit CO alarms with battery backup.3 The CDC says to replace each CO alarm every 5 years.5 Write the date you fitted it on the back.

Generators kill, even outside

Never run a generator inside a home or garage, even with the doors and windows open.15 The CDC adds carports, and says to keep it at least 6 m (20 ft) from doors, windows and vents.4

After the 2005 hurricanes in Alabama and Texas, at least 78 people were poisoned by generator CO and 10 died. Most cases involved generators placed outside, but near the home.20

Do

  • Use flashlights for light3
  • Fit CO alarms with battery backup3
  • Run a generator outside, 6 m (20 ft) from openings4
  • Point generator exhaust away from buildings15
  • Unplug electronics during the outage3
  • Check on older neighbors3

Don't

  • Light the house with candles3
  • Heat with a gas stove or oven34
  • Run a generator in a home, garage or carport4
  • Trust open doors or windows to clear the fumes15

A generator in the garage is fine if the garage door is open.

The US Consumer Product Safety Commission says never use a generator inside a home or garage, even if doors and windows are open.15

🚪 Fire safety: alarms and two ways out

A home fire can leave you as little as 1–2 minutes to escape.2 Every flame you add for heat, light or cooking is one more way for a fire to start.

Plan as if no fire truck is coming. Prevention, working alarms and a practiced escape are your fire service now.

  1. Know two ways out of every room

    Usually a door and a window. Check that both open from the inside.2

  2. Practice twice a year

    Run the drill by day and by night.2

  3. Get low and close doors

    Crawl low under smoke. Close doors behind you to slow the fire.2

  4. Meet outside

    Go straight to your outside meeting place and count heads.2

  5. Never go back in

    Not for people, pets or things.2 Tell rescuers who is missing instead.

Alarms that work without the grid

Put a battery smoke alarm in the warm room and outside every sleeping area. Press the test button at every drill so everyone knows the sound.

Keep spare batteries for every alarm, sealed and dated. Store them with your flashlight batteries.

Keep a fire extinguisher near where you cook and heat. Ready.gov lists one in its emergency kit.16 Learn to use it from your local fire service while classes still run.

Babies, children and anyone who needs help

Assign one adult to each baby and to anyone who cannot get out alone, plus a backup.2 Carry them in the night drill too.

🚰 Pipes: protect the water inside your walls

Your pipes and water heater hold water you can use. FEMA and the Red Cross explain how to get it out.7

In Texas in 2021, water system problems hit more than 14 million people.12 Learn your own pipes before the taps stop.

Find the main water valve and learn to close it. If the public water may be contaminated, closing it keeps dirty water out of your pipes.6

Emptying the pipes before a hard freeze

If you cannot keep the house above freezing, empty the pipes into clean containers. Water expands as it freezes. That is why stored water needs 5–8 cm (2–3 inches) of space at the top of the container.21 A full pipe has no space at all.

  1. Close the main valve

    This stops outside water from flowing in.6

  2. Open the highest tap

    Open the faucet on the top floor to let air into the pipes.7

  3. Drain the lowest tap

    Catch the water from the lowest faucet in clean containers.7

  4. Label and store it

    Write the date on each container. Use plastic, not glass, where it may freeze.21 If the public water may have been unsafe, treat it first: see Disinfecting Water.

The water heater holds more. For the safe order (power off first, then drain), see Storing Water.

Gas turned off? Call a professional

If the gas to the water heater was shut off, a professional needs to turn it back on.7 Refill the tank before the power or gas goes back on.7

Shelves must be strong. Water weighs over 8 lb per gallon (about 1 kg per liter), so stored containers need sturdy shelving.21

A full bathtub is your drinking supply.

The National Weather Service says to use bathtub water for cleaning and flushing only, not for drinking.22

Tubs and buckets drown toddlers

From 2006 to 2010, 434 children under 5 drowned in US homes. 82% were under age 2, and 81% of the deaths involved bathtubs or bath products.23

Drowning can happen in less than 2.5 cm (1 inch) of water. Never leave water containers unattended, use toilet-lid locks and keep bathroom doors closed.24

🌀 After a storm: check the house before you trust it

Check the house from outside first, in daylight, before anyone moves back into damaged rooms. Keep children and dogs back until you are done.

  1. Look from outside

    Walk all the way around. Look for a sagging roof, cracked or leaning walls, and trees resting on the building.

  2. Stay away from fallen wires

    Keep everyone, children and dogs included, well back.

  3. Smell gas? Get out

    Leave the house. Do not light flames or use switches. Stay out until it has been checked.

  4. Dress for floodwater

    Wear rubber boots, gloves and goggles.8

  5. Check your water

    Ask about boil water advisories. A private well must be tested and disinfected after floodwater goes down.25

  6. Clean what floodwater touched

    Disinfect children's toys. Wash clothes in hot water with detergent.8

Floodwater can contain sewage. Swallowing contaminated water or food can cause diarrheal disease.8 Floods also move chemical containers.8

Do not bathe in floodwater

The CDC says not to bathe in water that may be contaminated.8 Keep children from playing in it.

If you run a generator during cleanup, the same rule applies: outside, at least 6 m (20 ft) from doors, windows and vents.4

Hazard by hazard, see Floods and Hurricanes & Tornadoes. For collapse and debris dangers, see Salvage Hazards.

Walk the house now

Do the outside walk-around today, while the house is fine. Photograph the roof, walls and nearby trees so you can spot changes after a storm.

🗓️ Home prep, stage by stage

Do the slow jobs now. Leave only quick jobs for the storm warning and the outage itself.

  1. Now

    Choose the warm room. Fit CO alarms with battery backup.3 Find the main water valve.6 Practice the fire escape.2

  2. Storm warning

    Fill the bathtub and large containers for washing and flushing.22 Charge phones and keep gas tanks full.3

  3. Power out

    Flashlights on, no candles, no gas oven for heat.3 Generator outside, at least 6 m (20 ft) away.4

  4. Hard freeze

    If the house cannot stay above freezing, empty the pipes: main valve closed, top tap open, drain the lowest.67

  5. After the storm

    Boots, gloves and goggles for floodwater.8 Test and disinfect a private well.25

Get these before you need them

  • CO alarms with battery backup3
  • A flashlight for every person3
  • Plastic sheeting and duct tape16
  • Rubber boots, gloves and goggles8
  • Battery smoke alarms, spare batteries and a fire extinguisher
  • Food-grade water containers: see Storing Water

The 30-year view

CO alarms need replacing every 5 years.5 Over 30 years, that is 6 alarms for each spot in the house. Count them into your long-term plan.

Two drills a year2 adds up to 60 drills in 30 years. Ready.gov says to re-think your needs every year and replace expired items.16 Children grow, elders age and dogs change.
