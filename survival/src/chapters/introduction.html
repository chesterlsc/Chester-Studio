⚡ What electricity actually runs

The power grid is a hidden life-support system. Most of what keeps your household alive either arrives through wires or depends on something that does.

When the power stops, each system fails on its own clock. Some stop in seconds. Others hold out for hours or days on backup batteries and fuel.

Tap water

Your tap water is moved by electric pumps. Water and wastewater plants are often the biggest energy users in a town. They use about 30–40% of the energy a municipality uses, says the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA, 2024).1

In the February 2021 Texas freeze, more than 14 million people were hit by water outages and boil-water notices after days of power cuts and frozen pipes.8 Store water before you need it: see Storing Water for Months & Years.

Cold food and medicine

A closed fridge keeps food safe for about 4 hours. A full freezer holds about 48 hours and a half-full one about 24 hours, if the doors stay shut.9

Some medicines must stay cool. Find out now how long yours stay safe at higher temperatures.11 Part 3 (Food) and Part 4 (Medicine) cover both in detail.

Medical care

Medical care depends on power too. After Hurricane Maria, a household survey found that about one third of deaths after the storm were attributed to delayed or interrupted health care.5

In the US, more than 3 million Medicare patients have claims for electricity-dependent medical equipment. More than 4.6 million rely on that equipment or on services such as dialysis, oxygen or home health care (HHS emPOWER).10

Fuel, phones and money

Gas station pumps need electricity. The Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) lists fuel pumps, lighting and payment systems among the systems hit by power and telecom outages (2025).2

Phone service fails too. After Maria, surveyed households in Puerto Rico went an average of 41 days without cell phone service.5

Cash machines stop too. The April 2025 blackout in Spain and Portugal knocked out ATMs, traffic lights and subway networks.14

People on powered medical equipment

Oxygen concentrators, ventilators, powered wheelchairs and home dialysis all need electricity. In the US, over 3 million Medicare patients use electricity-dependent equipment.10 List everyone in your family and street who does. See Caring for Elderly & Disabled People.

Big blackouts are not rare and not only in the past. Here are the documented events this book learns from, in date order.

Spain and Portugal, April 2025

On April 28, 2025, at 12:33 Spanish time, mainland Spain and Portugal lost power.12 Subways, traffic lights and ATMs stopped working.14

Full restoration took about 16 hours.13 Early reports linked at least three deaths to the blackout. In Galicia, police investigated carbon monoxide from a generator after an older couple and their adult son died at home.15

The expert panel's final report, published in March 2026, found no single cause. It described a chain of failures with voltage control problems at its center.1213

Texas, February 2021 (Winter Storm Uri)

More than 4.5 million people in Texas lost power, some for as long as 4 days. Federal investigators found that frozen equipment at power plants was the largest single cause.17

Texas officially linked 246 deaths to the storm. About two-thirds were from cold exposure, mostly hypothermia.4 Water systems failed too, affecting more than 14 million people.8

Hurricane Helene, September 2024

Helene came ashore in Florida's Big Bend as a Category 4 hurricane on September 26, 2024.18 It left 5.9 million electricity customers without power across 10 states.19

The National Hurricane Center counted at least 250 US deaths. That made Helene the deadliest hurricane in the mainland US since Katrina in 2005.18

The Northeast blackout, August 2003

On August 14, 2003, about 50 million people in the US and Canada lost power. The outage cut 61,800 megawatts of electric load across eight US states and Ontario.16

Some parts of the US waited 4 days for power. Parts of Ontario had rolling blackouts for more than a week.16

Ukraine, 2022–2025: a grid under attack

Before 2022, Ukraine had about 38 gigawatts (GW) of dispatchable power capacity. It lost 19 GW to occupation, destruction and damage in the first year of the full-scale war. After more attacks in spring 2024, about 12 GW was left (IEA, 2025).20

That is about two-thirds of its capacity gone. Attacks on Ukraine's energy system continued through 2025.20

Grids only fail because of war or sabotage.

In Texas in 2021, the largest single cause was frozen power plant equipment.17 Quebec 1989 failed from a solar storm.23 The Iberian blackout had no single cause; voltage control problems were central.12

For full case studies of these and older events, see Real Long Blackouts: Lessons Learned.

🏝️ Puerto Rico after Maria: 11 months to restore power

Hurricane Maria hit Puerto Rico on September 20, 2017.5 About 11 months later, officials said power had been restored to the whole island.3

A Harvard-led team surveyed 3,299 randomly chosen households. Through the end of 2017, homes averaged 84 days without electricity, 68 days without water and 41 days without cell phone service (NEJM, 2018).5

Interrupted medical care was a major killer. About one third of the deaths after the storm in surveyed households were attributed to delayed or interrupted health care.5 The same study estimated 4,645 excess deaths to the end of 2017.5

Counting the dead took nearly a year. The first official toll was 64.5 A George Washington University study, commissioned by the governor, estimated 2,975 excess deaths from September 2017 to February 2018.21

Older adults faced the highest risk

The George Washington University study found that poor and elderly people were most at risk of dying after Maria.21 Plan medicines and care for older relatives first. See Chronic Conditions Without Pharmacies.

The 30-year view

Puerto Rico needed about 11 months to restore power to every area.3 This book plans for a much longer case: 30 years with no grid, with no repair crews or deliveries to count on. See What Fails When: The 30-Year Timeline.

☀️ Solar storms: the facts

Solar storms have already caused real blackouts. A big eruption on the Sun can throw a fast cloud of charged gas, called a coronal mass ejection (CME), toward Earth. The great storm of 1859 was caused by a very fast CME.22

1859: the Carrington Event

On September 1, 1859, astronomer Richard Carrington saw a bright flare on the Sun. The next day, the largest geomagnetic storm on record reached Earth.22

Auroras were seen as far south as the tropics. Currents surged through telegraph wires and set recording paper on fire at telegraph stations.22

1989: Quebec goes dark in 90 seconds

In March 1989, a geomagnetic storm tripped protective equipment on Hydro-Québec's grid. Within 90 seconds, 6 million people lost power, and it stayed off for 9 hours (NOAA).23

May 2024: the strongest storm in 20 years

On May 10, 2024, a G5 storm arrived. G5 is the top level on the US National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) 5-level scale, and this was the first G5 in over two decades (NASA, 2025).24

Grid operators reported isolated effects on large power transformers, voltage support equipment, line breakers and filters (NERC, 2024).25 GPS errors sent some self-steering farm tractors off course during spring planting.24

Solar storms only make pretty auroras.

In 1989 a solar storm cut power to 6 million people in Quebec.23 In 1859, storm currents set telegraph paper on fire.22

The full science, warning systems and what helps are in Solar Storms & Grid Collapse: The Facts.

☠️ The pattern: who died, and how

In Texas and Puerto Rico, most deaths did not happen when the lights went out. They came over the following days and months, from causes a household can plan for.

  • Cold: about two-thirds of the 246 Texas deaths in 2021 were from cold exposure, mostly hypothermia.4
  • Missed medical care: about one third of the deaths in the Puerto Rico survey were attributed to delayed or interrupted care.5
  • Generator fumes: in Spain in 2025, police investigated carbon monoxide after a family of three died with a generator running.15
  • Age and poverty: after Maria, poor and elderly people faced the highest risk.21

Every cause of death, ranked by speed, is in What Kills People First in Disasters.

Never run a generator indoors or in a garage

Not even with the doors and windows open. Run it outdoors, at least 20 feet (6 meters) from windows, doors and vents, and use a battery-powered or battery-backup CO alarm.7 See Carbon Monoxide: The Blackout Killer.

Babies and children

Children depend completely on adults for warmth, safe water and safe food. Learn the danger signs in Part 6 before you need them, and plan feeding now: see Feeding Babies When Formula Runs Out.

Store extra water for pregnancy

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) advises storing more water for pregnant women, for sick people and for hot climates.6 Pregnancy, birth and breastfeeding each have their own chapter in Part 5, starting with Pregnancy Care Without a Hospital.

Dogs and pets

Your dogs depend on the same water, food and shelter as you. Count every pet when you work out how much water and food to store. See Dogs in a Long Emergency.

📏 Why this book uses facts only

Every number in this book has a source you can check. The small number after a sentence links to the source list at the end of the chapter.

Official numbers change, so we give the year. Texas first counted 210 storm deaths, then 246 in its final report.4 Puerto Rico's first official count after Maria was 64.5 An independent study later estimated 2,975.21

Early numbers can be far too low

The Texas count rose by 36 deaths between reports.4 The first Maria count of 64 later sat beside study estimates of 2,975 and 4,645.215 When you read a number, check its date.

⏱️ Your next hour First 72 h

Do these six things now, while the power and shops still work. Start the timer and go.

  1. Fill water containers

    Store at least 1 gallon (3.8 liters) per person per day for 3 days. Work up to a 2-week supply.6

    Use clean, food-grade containers. Never use ones that held chemicals, including old bleach bottles.6

  2. Check your CO alarm

    Use a battery-powered or battery-backup alarm. Put one on every level of your home, where it will wake sleepers.711 Replace the alarm every 5 years.7

  3. List every medicine in the house

    Write down who takes it, how much is left, and whether it must stay cool. Ask a pharmacist how long it stays safe when warm.11

  4. Tape the fridge rule to the fridge

    Closed fridge: 4 hours. Full freezer: 48 hours. Half-full freezer: 24 hours. When in doubt, throw it out.9

  5. Write a paper contact list

    Phone numbers and addresses for family, neighbors, doctors and a contact outside your area. Phones can be out for weeks.5

  6. Check on one vulnerable person

    Find out who near you depends on powered medical equipment, and agree a plan together.10

  • Food-grade water containers6
  • Some cash in small notes: ATMs stopped in the 2025 Iberian blackout14
