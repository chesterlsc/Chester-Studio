🧰 Three tiers: who carries what

One kit cannot do every job. A box at home does not help on the road. A pocket kit cannot treat a whole family for a month.

So split your supplies into three tiers. Each tier copies an established model: the US Army's Improved First Aid Kit (IFAK) for personal kits, the American Red Cross family list for households, and the World Health Organization (WHO) essential medicines idea for the group store.614

The three tiers at a glance
TierWhere it livesBuilt fromMain job
1. PersonalOn your body or beltArmy IFAK ideaStop severe bleeding fast
2. HouseholdHome and carRed Cross family listCuts, burns, sprains, fever
3. GroupOne locked, shared storeWHO essential medicines idea + a clinicianRestock tiers 1 and 2; long-term medicines

Color-code the tiers

Use a red pouch for bleeding gear, a clear box for the household kit and labeled tubs for the group store. A tired person can then grab the right thing in the dark.

🩸 Tier 1: the personal bleeding kit

The personal kit solves one problem: bleeding that can kill before help arrives. The US Army says severe bleeding and airway problems are two of the leading causes of death on the battlefield, and its kit targets both.2

In 2013 the Army introduced a redesigned kit, the IFAK II.6 The redesign drew on soldier feedback and added a second tourniquet.2

The civilian version

You are not a soldier, but the logic carries over: small, light, always on you, and focused on bleeding. Carry only items you have been trained to use.

  1. Put the tourniquet where both hands reach it

    An outside pocket or belt pouch, not the bottom of a bag. Learn it first in a class. See Stopping Severe Bleeding.

  2. Add a pressure bandage and gauze

    These are for wounds a tourniquet cannot reach. Stop the Bleed teaches wound packing alongside tourniquets.5

  3. Add gloves

    At least 2 pairs. The Red Cross kit specifies nonlatex gloves.1

  4. Add a few days of your own medicines

    Keep them in labeled containers with a written list of names, doses and allergies.

  5. Add a card and a marker

    Write what you did and the time. The Army kit carries a casualty card and marker for the same job.6

  6. Make every kit the same

    Same pouch, same layout, same place on every adult. Anyone can then find anything in the dark.

Do

  • Carry it on your body
  • Keep the tourniquet reachable with either hand
  • Train before you rely on the gear
  • Replace anything you use the same day

Don't

  • Bury it in a backpack
  • Carry gear you have never practiced with
  • Add "nice to have" items until it is too heavy to carry daily
  • Share one personal kit across the family

A tourniquet is a skill, not a gadget

Stop the Bleed courses teach tourniquets together with direct pressure and wound packing.5 Take the course before you trust the gear, and practice only the way your instructor shows you.

🏠 Tier 2: the household kit

Start with the American Red Cross list. It is written for a family of four. The Red Cross says to keep one kit at home and one in the car.1

Copy the list exactly first. Add your family's extras after. The Red Cross also says to include personal items such as medicines and emergency phone numbers, or other items your health care provider suggests.1

Only four items are medicines or antiseptics: antibiotic ointment, antiseptic wipes, hydrocortisone ointment and low-dose aspirin.1 Everything else is dressings, tools and protection.

The aspirin in the kit is fine for anyone in pain.

Never give aspirin to children.7 Do not give it to teenagers either unless a doctor says so. If you are allergic to aspirin, have a bleeding problem or take a blood thinner, do not take the kit aspirin unless a clinician tells you to. Read the warnings on the label first.

Add these for your household

  • Each person's prescription medicines and a written medicine list
  • Children's versions of any OTC medicines you use, in their own pouch
  • Menstrual supplies, including reusable ones
  • Extra gloves, tape and gauze
  • Elastic bandages and splint material
  • A printed copy of this book's medical chapters

Children get their own pouch

Never give aspirin to children.7 WHO keeps a separate children's list, the EMLc, with 374 medicines in 2025.3

Keep children's medicines apart from adult ones, write each child's weight on the pouch, and dose only as the label says. See The Baby & Toddler Prep Kit.

Women and girls

Pack menstrual products for every woman and girl, plus pain relief used only as the label directs. If anyone is pregnant or breastfeeding, read the warnings on every label first and ask a clinician whenever one is available.

One medicine list per person

Write each person's medicines, doses, times, allergies and prescriber on one card. Tape it inside the kit lid. Anyone on a blood thinner should ask a clinician before taking the kit aspirin. For long-term illness, see Chronic Conditions Without Pharmacies.

Dogs need a separate kit

Pack the dog's supplies in their own box marked DOG, so human and dog medicines never mix. Give a dog medicine only when a veterinarian has told you to. See Dog First Aid.

🏘️ Tier 3: the group store

A group store is a small supply room, not a bigger first aid kit. It refills the personal and household kits, and it holds what one family cannot.

The model is how health systems plan. WHO says essential medicines are meant to be available within functioning health systems at all times, in adequate amounts.4 A group store tries to keep that promise on a small scale.

What goes in it

  • Bulk consumables: multiples of every Red Cross item, especially gloves, gauze and tape
  • Durable tools: tweezers, scissors, splints and spare thermometers (the Red Cross specifies non-mercury, nonglass)1
  • The medicine plan made with a doctor or pharmacist (next section)
  • Printed references and the supply log
  • Separate shelves for children's and dogs' supplies

Do the arithmetic. The Red Cross list covers 4 people.1 For a group of 12, three lists give 75 adhesive bandages, 6 compress dressings, 30 gauze pads and 6 pairs of gloves. That is only a starting point. The quantities section shows how to scale it from your own use.

Give the store one keeper

Name one person to run the log and a second person to check it. Every item that leaves gets written down: what, how many, for whom, and the date.

The 30-year view

Even today, with power and pharmacies, WHO reports that availability and affordability of essential medicines remain problems in many countries.4 Over decades, plan for supplies running out: keep reusable tools, learn the skills, and stay connected to any clinic that still works.

💊 Essential medicines (WHO 2025) and what "OTC" means

WHO's Model List of Essential Medicines (EML) names the medicines a health system needs most. WHO defines essential medicines as those that satisfy the priority health-care needs of a population.4

WHO published the current edition on 5 September 2025: the 24th adult list and the 10th children's list (EMLc).3 Together they name 523 medicines for adults and 374 for children.38

The 2025 update added key cancer treatments.3 It also added diabetes and obesity medicines in the GLP-1 class (semaglutide, dulaglutide, liraglutide) and the dual-action medicine tirzepatide.3

Core and complementary lists

The core list covers the minimum medicine needs of a basic health-care system. The complementary list covers priority diseases that need specialist diagnosis, monitoring or care.4

WHO chooses each medicine by public health relevance, evidence of efficacy and safety, and comparative cost-effectiveness.4 That is a good test for your own plan too: what problems are common in your group, and what is proven to help?

The WHO list is a home stockpile list. Buy everything on it.

It is written for health systems. Even the core list is the minimum for a basic health-care system, and complementary items need specialist diagnosis, monitoring or care.4 Use it as a map to discuss with a doctor or pharmacist.

What "OTC" means

Over-the-counter (OTC) medicines are sold without a prescription. Each country decides which medicines are OTC, so check your local rules with a pharmacist.

Give OTC doses only as written on the label. For what each common OTC medicine does, see Over-the-Counter Medicines.

📦 Quantities for long durations

Size your kit from your own use, not from a guess. We could not find an official kit list sized for decades without power. So use arithmetic on two numbers you control: how many people, and how much you actually use.

  1. Count your people

    List adults, children, babies, elders and dogs separately. Each group needs different items.

  2. Set the baseline

    One Red Cross list per 4 people, rounded up.1 A family of 6 needs 2 lists.

  3. Log your use for 3 months

    Write down every bandage, glove, wipe and dose you use. Real numbers beat guesses.

  4. Multiply to a year

    3 months × 4 = 1 year. Example: 10 bandages in 3 months is 40 a year, or 1,200 over 30 years.

  5. Do the medicine math

    1 tablet a day is 365 a year and 10,950 over 30 years. Show that number to your prescriber and plan together.

  6. Split durable from consumable

    Buy durable tools once, plus a spare. Buy consumables in bulk and rotate them.

🌡️ Storage conditions and rotation

A kit you cannot find, cannot open or that has expired is the same as no kit. The Red Cross says to check expiration dates and replace any used or out-of-date contents.1

Follow the storage line on each label. Read it on every medicine and sterile pack, and copy any temperature limit onto your inventory sheet.

If a medicine got too hot or too cold, do not guess. The US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) says to check its storage instructions or ask a pharmacist, health care provider or the manufacturer.10

Insulin without a fridge

The FDA says insulin vials and cartridges, opened or unopened, may be left unrefrigerated at 15–30°C (59–86°F) for up to 28 days.11 Pens and other products can differ, so check each leaflet, and replace insulin exposed to extreme conditions as soon as you can.11 See Chronic Conditions Without Pharmacies.

Review the whole kit at least once a year. Ready.gov says to replace expired items as needed and to re-think your needs every year as your family changes.12

  1. Use hard, sealed containers

    Use tubs with tight lids to keep out water, insects and rodents.

  2. Label the outside

    Write the tier, the contents and the earliest expiration date on the lid.

  3. Lock medicines away from children

    Store them high and locked. Keep the key where every adult knows to find it.

  4. Keep boxes off the floor and away from heat

    Not by the stove, not in the sun, not on a damp floor. Follow each label's storage line.

  5. First in, first out

    New stock goes to the back. Use the oldest first so nothing expires unseen.

  6. Keep the log with the box

    Every item in or out gets a line: what, how many and the date.

Do

  • Keep a kit at home and in the car1
  • Pick one fixed check date and stick to it
  • Keep sterile packs sealed and dry
  • Read the storage line on every label

Don't

  • Store medicines where children can reach them
  • Mix loose pills in unlabeled bags
  • Use a sterile pack that is wet or torn as if it were sterile
  • Leave the only kit in a car that might be gone

Child safety

Children explore boxes and bags. Keep every medicine, including the kit aspirin, in a locked box. Aspirin is never for children.7

🎓 Skills matter more than gear

Training turns a box of supplies into care. The Army rebuilt its kit from soldier feedback.2 Your family's kit should follow the same rule: carry what you know how to use.

The American College of Surgeons (ACS) Stop the Bleed program has trained more than 5 million people worldwide. It teaches three techniques: direct pressure, wound packing and tourniquets.5

Practice with your own kit

  • Empty the kit on a table. Name every item and say what it is for.
  • Repack it with your eyes closed. Then repack it in the dark.
  • Ask each adult to find the tourniquet in under 10 seconds.
  • Teach children where the kit lives and how to bring it to an adult.
