📏 How to read a shelf-life number

A shelf-life number is a limit under stated conditions. It tells you how long something stays safe or useful when it is stored the way the source describes.

Read the conditions, not just the number. EPA's bleach rule is for bleach stored at room temperature for less than one year.7 FDA's 28-day insulin rule is for 15–30°C (59–86°F).5 Outside those conditions, the number does not apply.

Every clock needs a start time

Label and date every water container when you fill it. The CDC asks for exactly that.1 Do the same for bleach, food boxes and medicine. A table cannot help you if you do not know when the clock started.

In an outage, write down the time the power went off. The fridge, freezer and medicine limits below all count from that moment. The first hours are covered step by step in The First 72 Hours.

Three kinds of numbers

  • Replace-by schedules. Water you bottled: every 6 months.1 CO alarm: every 5 years.8 Put these on a paper calendar.
  • Safety limits. Perishable food above 4°C (40°F): 2 hours.4 Past the limit, it goes.
  • Printed dates. Find out what a date means before you act on it. A CDC planning guide says the sell-by date on bottled water is mainly for stock rotation.10

For medicine, the label wins

Ready.gov's one-day rule for refrigerated medicine applies unless the label says otherwise.6 If a medicine was stored outside its label temperature, the FDA says to check its storage instructions or ask a pharmacist, doctor or the maker.11

Tables for other supplies

Dry staples, home-canned food, fuel, batteries and seeds each have their own chapter and storage rules: Long-Term Food Storage, Home Canning Safely, Generators & Fuel Storage, Small Solar & Batteries and Seed Saving & Seed Viability. Use the same three questions there.

💧 Water and water containers

Store-bought bottled water is your safest stored water. The CDC calls unopened, commercially bottled water the safest and most reliable water in an emergency.1

Water you bottle yourself gets replaced every 6 months. The CDC and Ready.gov give the same schedule.12

What makes water last, or not

  • Cool: 10–21°C (50–70°F).1
  • Dark: out of sunlight.1 Light makes plastic brittle and lets algae grow.13
  • Away from fuel and pesticides: vapors from gasoline, kerosene and pesticides can pass through plastic.116
  • Off bare concrete or dirt: plastic absorbs odors there.17 Air flow underneath limits moisture and mold.13
  • Room to freeze: leave 5–8 cm (2–3 in) of headspace, and do not use glass.16
  • The right plastic: food-grade HDPE, marked with a 2 in the recycling triangle, is the best choice.15

Container choice, rotation and how much to store are in Storing Water for Months & Years.

Babies, children and nursing mothers

University of Florida Extension says to store more water when there are children or nursing mothers at home.18 The CDC says to make powdered formula with bottled water until officials say the tap water is safe.19 Keep a few unopened bottles set aside for formula only.

Count the dog

The CDC says to store more water if you have pets.1 Ready.gov's pet kit includes a water bowl and several days of water.20 Put the dog's water on the same 6-month calendar as yours, and read the labels on any pet medicines for the word "refrigerate".

Bleach keeps forever if the cap stays on.

EPA's emergency water method calls for bleach stored at room temperature for less than one year.7 Older bleach is outside the official method. Date every bottle when you buy it.

The smell test tells you the dose worked

  1. Measure the dose

    8 drops of 6% bleach, or 6 drops of 8.25%, per 3.8 L (1 gallon) of clear water.7

  2. Double it for hard cases

    Let cloudy water settle and filter it through a clean cloth first. Cloudy, colored or very cold water gets twice the dose.7

  3. Stir and wait 30 minutes

    Stir, then let the water stand for 30 minutes.7

  4. Smell it

    A faint chlorine smell means it is ready.7

  5. No smell? Repeat

    Add the same dose again and wait another 15 minutes.7

Pregnancy and nursing

The CDC does not recommend iodine water treatment for pregnant women.22 The CDC also says to store more water during pregnancy,1 and Florida Extension says the same for nursing mothers.18 Other methods are in Disinfecting Water.

Learn the smell now

Dose 3.8 L (1 gallon) of tap water with fresh bleach, wait 30 minutes and smell it.7 Then you will know the faint chlorine smell when it matters, even in the dark.

Older adults and medical devices

Ready.gov says to talk to your medical provider now about a power-outage plan for refrigerated medicines and for medical devices that need electricity.6 Older adults and young children are especially vulnerable to extreme temperatures.6

Ready.gov's one-day rule applies to any medicine that should be refrigerated, and that includes children's medicines. If your child's life depends on one, ask a doctor or pharmacist and keep using it until a new supply arrives.6 Ask the pharmacist which of your child's medicines need cold, and write the answer on the bottle.

🗓️ Your replacement calendar Long haul

Turn every number into a date on paper. A wall calendar works without power. Here is the order in which the official limits arrive.

  1. Hour 4

    A closed fridge reaches its limit.3

  2. Day 1

    A half-full freezer reaches its limit. After more than a day, refrigerated medicines are discarded unless the label says otherwise or a life depends on them.36

  3. Day 2

    A full, closed freezer reaches its limit.3

  4. Day 28

    Insulin vials and cartridges kept at 15–30°C (59–86°F) reach their limit.5

  5. Month 6

    Replace the water you bottled yourself.1

  6. Year 1

    Replace bleach for the EPA method. Check every kit and re-think your needs.726

  7. Year 5

    Replace CO alarms.8 Inspect plastic drums that sat in light.13

Run a shelf-life audit

  1. Pick one category

    Water, chemicals, cold food, medicine or gear. One shelf at a time is enough.

  2. Write the start date

    Fill date for water, purchase date for bleach and alarms. The CDC asks you to label and date stored water.1

  3. Write the replace-by date

    Water plus 6 months, bleach plus 1 year, CO alarm plus 5 years.178

  4. Check the signs

    Look for brittle plastic or algae,13 and for out-of-date first-aid supplies.25

  5. Fix the storage

    Move items somewhere cool, dark, off concrete and away from fuel.117

  6. Copy the dates to paper

    Put them on a wall calendar. Re-think your needs every year.26

The 30-year arithmetic

At one swap every 6 months, 30 years is 60 rounds of water rotation.1 At 5 years per alarm, each alarm spot needs 6 alarms in 30 years.8 Bleach replaced every year is 30 replacements.7

Plan now for what you can renew yourself. Water treatment without bought bleach is in Disinfecting Water.

Get these before you need them

  • Permanent markers and labels for dates
  • Two appliance thermometers, for the fridge and the freezer3
  • A battery-powered CO alarm8
  • Food-grade HDPE #2 water containers15
  • Boards or pallets to keep supplies off concrete17

✅ Do this now

  • Write the fill date on every water container, and the replace-by date 6 months later
  • Write the purchase date on every bleach bottle and replace bottles over 1 year old
  • Move water off bare concrete and away from fuel, pesticides and sunlight
  • Put appliance thermometers in the fridge and the freezer
  • List every medicine labeled "refrigerate", with its limit without a fridge
  • Read your insulin package insert and write its room-temperature limit on the box
  • Check the age of your CO alarm and replace it at 5 years
  • Check first-aid kit expiration dates and replace out-of-date items
  • Print the FoodSafety.gov power-outage food chart
  • Copy every replace-by date onto a paper wall calendar

🧠 Remember this

How often do you replace water you bottled yourself?

Every 6 months. Store it at 10–21°C (50–70°F), in the dark.

How long does a closed fridge keep food safe with no power?

About 4 hours. A full freezer about 48 hours, a half-full one about 24.

Perishable food above 4°C (40°F): how long?

2 hours, or 1 hour above 32°C (90°F). Then throw it out.

Insulin vials without a fridge?

Up to 28 days at 15–30°C (59–86°F). Pens differ: read the insert.

Other refrigerated medicine after a day without power?

Discard it unless the label says otherwise. If a life depends on it, ask a doctor or pharmacist.

How old can bleach be for EPA's water method?

Less than 1 year, stored at room temperature.

When do you replace a CO alarm?

Every 5 years.

Should you taste food to check it?

Never. When in doubt, throw it out.
