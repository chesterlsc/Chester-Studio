🌊 Storm surge: the deadliest hurricane hazard

In a hurricane, water kills more people than wind.9 The National Hurricane Center (NHC) says storm surge and large waves are the greatest threat to life and property along the coast.1

Storm surge is an abnormal rise of seawater generated by the storm, over and above the normal tide.1 It is the sea itself coming onto the land.

An NHC study by Edward Rappaport blamed 49% of US hurricane and tropical storm deaths from 1963 to 2012 on storm surge.9

That is why an NHC official put it so simply: "We evacuate from the water, not the wind, and we have evacuation zones."2 Learn your zone before the season starts. For the full decision, see Stay or Go?

Hurricane wind kills most people, so a strong house keeps you safe.

Storm surge caused 49% of US hurricane and tropical storm deaths from 1963 to 2012.9 On the coast, surge and waves are the greatest threat to life.1

🚗 Leave early, leave safely

A late, crowded evacuation can kill too. When Hurricane Rita threatened Texas, nearly 3.7 million people evacuated in 4 days.11

More than 100 of at least 119 deaths were linked to the evacuation, not to the storm itself.11 At least 10% were heat illness (hyperthermia) in vehicles, and a bus carrying nursing-home residents caught fire.11

Do not let this keep you in a surge zone. The lesson is to leave early, with a plan and fuel in the tank.12

  1. Know your zone now

    Find out before the season whether your home is in a storm surge evacuation zone.2

  2. Keep fuel in the car

    Keep at least half a tank at all times. Fill up when an evacuation seems likely.12

  3. Plan where you will go

    Decide how you will leave and where you will stay.12 Check that the place accepts your dogs, since public shelters may not.13

  4. Leave early

    Go early enough that severe weather cannot trap you on the road.12

  5. Use official routes

    Follow the recommended evacuation routes. Do not take shortcuts.12

  6. Carry water for the drive

    Pack drinking water for every person and pet in the car. Heat in vehicles killed people during the Rita evacuation.11

Dogs and other pets

Temporary public shelters may not allow animals.13 Find a pet-friendly place to stay before the season, not on the day you leave.

Microchip your dog. Vet clinics and animal shelters scan for microchips.13 Store extra water for pets, too.14 See Dogs in a Long Emergency.

Practice this now

Do a timed dry run on a calm day. Load the car with go-bags, water, medicines, dogs and leashes, and note how long it takes.

Then drive your official evacuation route once, so you know it in the dark and in the rain.

Next, test-run the generator at its marked spot with the CO alarms switched on, so the first time is not during a storm.

⛰️ Inland flooding: Hurricane Helene, 2024

Hurricanes kill far from the beach. For people living inland, NHC says flooding is the major threat.1

Hurricane Helene came ashore as a Category 4 hurricane on September 26, 2024. It caused at least 250 deaths in the US, making it the deadliest hurricane on the US mainland since Katrina.3

Helene left 5.9 million customers without power across 10 states.15 In the mountain city of Asheville, North Carolina, a systemwide boil water notice followed, and the water crisis lasted 53 days.1610

  1. Sept 26, 2024

    Helene comes ashore as a Category 4 hurricane.3

  2. After landfall

    5.9 million customers lose power across 10 states.15 Asheville goes under a systemwide boil water notice.16

  3. Nov 18, 2024

    After 120 water samples are tested, Asheville lifts the notice for all customers. The water crisis lasted 53 days.1016

How people die in sudden disasters

In rapid-onset disasters such as storms and floods, most deaths come from blunt trauma, crush injuries and drowning.17

The 30-year view

Helene left millions without power and a whole mountain city without safe tap water for 53 days.1510 With no grid for decades, your household has to store, collect and treat its own water.

Build that system now. See Disinfecting Water.

🦠 Floodwater, wells and disease

Treat floodwater like sewage. It can carry sewage and chemicals. Swallowing water or food it has contaminated causes diarrheal disease, such as E. coli and Salmonella infections.18

Private wells must be tested and disinfected after floodwaters go down. Check with your water utility or health department about boil water advisories.19

Boiling, disinfecting and filtering cannot remove chemical contamination.20 If floodwater may hold fuel or chemicals, use a different water source. See Floods.

  1. Check the water notice

    Ask the water utility or health department whether a boil water advisory is in force.19

  2. Gear up before cleanup

    Wear rubber boots, rubber gloves and goggles.18

  3. Do not bathe in it

    Do not bathe in water that may be contaminated with floodwater.18

  4. Wash what it touched

    Wash clothes in hot water with detergent. Disinfect children's toys before they play with them.18

  5. Test the well

    Test and disinfect a private well before anyone drinks from it.19

Dead bodies left after a storm will start an epidemic.

There is no evidence that dead bodies cause epidemics after natural disasters.17 Outbreak risk comes from displacement, crowding, and unsafe water and sanitation.17

Disasters that do not displace people rarely lead to outbreaks. Diarrheal outbreaks can follow contamination of drinking water.17 Keeping your household housed and your water clean protects you most.

Babies and formula

Mix formula with bottled water until officials declare the tap water safe.20 Boiling cannot remove chemicals, so water touched by floodwater is not a safe base for formula.20

Disinfect toys that touched floodwater before a baby mouths them.18

⚡ Carbon monoxide: the after-storm killer

Generators poison people after hurricanes. After two major hurricanes in Alabama and Texas in 2005, portable generators poisoned at least 78 people with carbon monoxide (CO) and killed 10.21

Most of those generators were outside, but near the home.21 "Outside" is not enough. Distance matters.

CO has no color and no smell. People who are asleep can die from it before they have any symptoms.22

Generators are not the only source. CO comes from burning fuel in cars, small engines, stoves, lanterns, grills, fireplaces, gas ranges and furnaces, and it builds up in enclosed spaces.22 After a storm, cook and run engines outdoors only.

  1. Outside only

    Never run it in a home, garage or carport.4 Open doors and windows do not make it safe.23

  2. Measure the distance

    Place it at least 6.1 m (20 ft) from doors, windows and vents.4 Use a tape measure the first time.

  3. Aim the exhaust away

    Point the exhaust away from every building.23

  4. Alarm the sleeping areas

    Put battery or battery-backup CO alarms near sleeping areas. Replace each alarm every 5 years.22

  5. Act on symptoms fast

    Headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain or confusion: get everyone into fresh air, then get medical help.4

Do

  • Generator outside, 6.1 m (20 ft) or more from doors, windows and vents
  • Exhaust pointed away from the house
  • Battery CO alarms near sleeping areas
  • An extension cord longer than 6.1 m (20 ft)

Don't

  • Run it in a garage or carport, even with the door open
  • Set it just outside a door or window
  • Heat the home with a gas oven4
  • Run a car in a garage or carport4

A generator is safe in the garage if the big door is open.

Never use a generator inside a home or garage, even with doors and windows open.23 Keep it at least 6.1 m (20 ft) from doors, windows and vents.4

Who is at higher risk

Infants, older people, and people with heart disease, anemia or breathing problems are at higher risk from CO.22 CO poisons animals too, so dogs shut in with a running engine are in danger.22

Full details are in Carbon Monoxide: The Blackout Killer.

Get these before storm season

  • Battery or battery-backup CO alarms for each sleeping area22
  • An extension cord longer than 6.1 m (20 ft), so the generator can stay far away
  • A tape measure and paint to mark the generator spot

💧 Store water before landfall, then guard it

Fill your bathtub and large containers before the storm. Use the bathtub water for cleaning and flushing toilets only. Do not drink it.7

For drinking, store at least 3.8 liters (1 gallon) per person per day for at least 3 days, and for 2 weeks if you can.14 Helene showed why: Asheville's water crisis lasted 53 days.10

Use food-grade containers, never old bleach or pesticide bottles. Label and date them, and replace stored tap water every 6 months.14 Keep them out of sunlight and away from gasoline and pesticides.14 See Storing Water for Months & Years.

A filled tub can drown a toddler

From 2006 to 2010, 434 children under 5 drowned at home in the US. 82% were under age 2, and 81% of the deaths involved bathtubs or bath products.8

Keep the bathroom door closed while the tub is full. Never leave a young child alone near a filled tub or bucket.

Pregnancy and stored water

Store more water if anyone in the home is pregnant or sick, or if you live in a hot climate.14 Count the extra before the season, not after the taps stop.

Food after the outage

When the power has been off for hours, sort the fridge and freezer before anyone eats. Use the rules in Food Safety Without a Refrigerator.

🧓 The long tail: deaths after the storm passes

Most of a hurricane's deaths can come after the wind stops. After Hurricane Maria hit Puerto Rico in 2017, the official death count was 64.56

A study commissioned by Puerto Rico's governor found 2,975 excess deaths from September 2017 to February 2018.6 That is more than 46 times the official count.

A survey of 3,299 households found homes went an average of 84 days without power, 68 days without water and 41 days without cell service.5 About one third of deaths came from interrupted medical care.5

Once the storm passes, the danger is over.

Maria's official toll was 64.5 Researchers later linked 2,975 excess deaths to it, counted from September 2017 to February 2018.6 About a third of deaths came from interrupted medical care.5

Older people and medical devices

The poor and the elderly were at highest risk after Maria. Older men had a 35% higher risk of death.6

In the US, over 3 million Medicare beneficiaries use electricity-dependent medical equipment. Counting dialysis, oxygen and home health, the number is over 4.6 million.24 Plan a backup for every device, and write down how long each battery lasts. See Caring for Elderly & Disabled People.

Insulin without a fridge

Insulin vials and cartridges, opened or unopened, may be kept unrefrigerated at 15–30°C (59–86°F) for up to 28 days. Pens and other products differ, so check the label.25

Replace insulin that has been exposed to extreme heat or cold.25 See Chronic Conditions Without Pharmacies.
