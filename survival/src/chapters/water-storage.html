🚰 Why stored water comes first First 72 h

When the taps stop, stored water is what you drink while you find and treat more. The CDC calls unopened, commercially bottled water the safest and most reliable water in an emergency.1

Long outages happen. After Hurricane Helene, Asheville, North Carolina, did not lift its boil water notice for all customers until November 18, 2024.9 Local public radio called it a 53-day water crisis.8

The CDC minimum is 1 gallon (3.8 L) per person per day. Store at least 3 days, and a 2-week supply if you can.1 Store more in hot climates, and for pregnant women and people who are sick.1

Do the math once

At 1 gallon per person per day, a family of 4 needs 56 gallons (212 L) for 2 weeks.1 That is about one 208 L (55-gallon) drum. For heat, babies, nursing mothers and dogs, see How Much Water You Really Need.

🛢️ Choosing safe containers

Use containers made for food or water. The CDC says FDA-approved, food-grade water storage containers are best.1

Look for the number 2 inside the recycling triangle. Utah's emergency management agency calls food-grade high-density polyethylene (HDPE #2) containers your best choice.10 If you reuse bottles, Ready.gov says to choose 2-liter soft drink bottles (PETE #1), not milk or juice jugs.2

  • A waterproof marker and labels

🧼 Clean, fill and treat

Sanitize every container before its first fill. This is the CDC method. It needs only soap, water and plain unscented bleach.1

  1. Wash and rinse

    Wash the container with soap. Rinse it completely with water.1

  2. Mix the sanitizer

    Stir 1 teaspoon (5 mL) of unscented liquid chlorine bleach (5–9% sodium hypochlorite) into 1 quart (about 1 liter) of water.1

  3. Coat every surface

    Pour it into the container, cap it and shake well so the solution touches all inside surfaces.1

  4. Wait, then empty

    Wait at least 30 seconds, then pour the solution out. Let the container air-dry, or rinse it with clean, safe water.1

  5. Fill with safe water

    Fill it with clean, safe water and screw the cap on tightly.1

  6. Label it

    Write "drinking water" and the date on the container.1

Know where your fill water comes from. Ready.gov says tap water from a utility that treats it with chlorine needs nothing added. Water from a well or other untreated source needs bleach.2 Utah State University Extension says water from wells, rivers or rainwater must be treated before you store it.13

To treat clear water with bleach, the EPA dose is 8 drops of 6% bleach, or 6 drops of 8.25% bleach, per gallon (3.8 L).14 Double the dose if the water is cloudy, colored or very cold.14

Stir, then wait 30 minutes before anyone drinks it. The water should smell slightly of chlorine. If it does not, EPA says to repeat the dose and wait another 15 minutes.14

For a full 208 L (55-gallon) drum of clear, untreated water, scale up EPA's 8-gallon dose of 2/3 teaspoon. That is about 4½ teaspoons (23 mL) of 6% bleach, or about 3½ teaspoons (17 mL) of 8.25% bleach.14 Double it for cloudy water.14

Plain, unscented bleach only

The EPA says not to use scented or color-safe bleach, or bleach with added cleaners.14 Its method uses bleach stored at room temperature for less than a year.14 For more on fresh bleach, tablets and pool shock, see Disinfecting Water.

Bleach cannot fix chemical pollution

The CDC warns that water polluted with harmful chemicals or toxins cannot be made safe by boiling, disinfecting or filtering.15 Never store water from a source that may hold fuel or chemicals. See Chemical Contamination.

🔄 How often to rotate Year 1

Replace water you bottled yourself every 6 months. The CDC and Ready.gov both give this interval.111

For store-bought water, go by the date on the bottle. That is the CDC's household rule.1 Its planning guide for hospitals adds that commercial "sell by" dates mainly help stores rotate stock. They do not mean the water has gone bad.16

Water you disinfect and bottle yourself does not keep forever. The same CDC guide says to check it from time to time for leftover (residual) chlorine and to treat it again if needed.16

  1. Day 0

    Sanitize, fill and treat if needed. Label "drinking water" with the date.1

  2. Month 6

    Replace the water.1 Use the old water for flushing, washing or the garden. Check each empty container for cracks and leaks.

  3. Month 12

    Rotate again. Buy a fresh bottle of bleach at the same time: EPA's method uses bleach less than a year old.14 Use the oldest store-bought water first.

  4. Every rotation

    Write the next due date on a calendar on paper, not only on your phone.

A label that answers every question

Write four lines on each container: DRINKING WATER, the fill date, the source (city tap, or well treated with how many drops of bleach), and the replace-by date. The CDC asks for "drinking water" and the date at minimum.1

Start rationing your stored water on day one.

FEMA, the Red Cross and county emergency managers say never ration drinking water unless authorities order it.2017 Drink the amount you need today and try to find more for tomorrow.20

🏠 Hidden water already in your house First 72 h

Protect the water in your pipes first. If the public supply may be contaminated, find your home's incoming water valve and close it.17 That keeps dirty water out of your pipes and water heater.

The Red Cross lists the hot-water tank, the pipes and ice cubes as safe water inside the home.18 The CDC also lists the water heater tank, and melted ice cubes made with water that was not contaminated.19

The water heater: 30–80 gallons

A home water heater can hold 30–80 gallons (114–303 L).3 The CDC means the tank that supplies your taps, not a home heating system.19

  1. Turn off the power or gas

    Switch off the electricity or gas that heats the tank.420

  2. Let it cool

    Wait for the tank to cool before you open anything.4

  3. Close the cold-water inlet

    Turn off the water intake valve at the tank.20

  4. Let air in

    Turn on a hot-water faucet in the house so the tank can drain.20

  5. Drain into a clean container

    Put a clean container under the drain valve at the bottom of the tank, then open the valve.4

  6. Refill before you restart

    Refill the tank before you turn the power or gas back on.20 If the gas was shut off, a professional needs to turn it back on.20

Power off, then wait

FEMA's order is power off, let the tank cool, then open the drain.4 Keep children away from the drain valve while the water is still hot.

Find your valves today

Find your main water valve and the water heater drain valve now, in daylight. Tag the main valve so anyone can find it in the dark. If you live in an earthquake zone, see Earthquakes for strapping the water heater.

Water in the pipes

  1. Close the main valve

    Stop outside water from flowing in.17

  2. Open the highest tap

    Turn on the faucet on the highest floor to let air into the pipes. A small amount of water will trickle out.20

  3. Drain the lowest tap

    Collect the water from the lowest faucet in the house.20

The toilet: tank maybe, bowl never

Never use water from the toilet bowl. FEMA and the Red Cross both rule it out.418

The tank behind the toilet is different. FEMA's current guidance lists it only if the water is clear and has no chemical cleaners, such as colored tablets, and says to disinfect it before use.4 The older FEMA and Red Cross booklet says not to use flush tank water at all.1820

Use tank water for flushing and washing first.3 Drink it only as a last resort, and disinfect it before you do.4

Do

  • Drain the water heater after the power is off and it has cooled4
  • Melt ice cubes made with uncontaminated water19
  • Take pipe water from the lowest tap20
  • Use toilet tank water for flushing3

Don't

  • Drink from the toilet bowl4
  • Use water from radiators, waterbeds, swimming pools or spas18
  • Use a toilet tank that holds colored cleaning tablets4
  • Turn the heater back on before the tank refills20

🛁 Bathtub storage: the facts

Fill the bathtub before a storm, but do not drink it. The National Weather Service says to fill bathtubs and large containers in case tap water stops. Use bathtub water for cleaning and flushing only.5

Clean the tub and test that the plug holds before the storm. Scoop the water out with a bucket when you need it. For flushing without running water, see Toilets Without Water.

A full bathtub is a drinking water reserve.

The National Weather Service says bathtub water is for cleaning and flushing only: do not drink it.5 Keep drinking water in sanitized, food-grade containers.1

A filled tub or bucket can drown a toddler

From 2006 to 2010, CPSC counted 434 drowning deaths of children under 5 at home. 82% were under age 2, and 81% of the incidents involved bathtubs or bath products.6

Drowning can happen quickly and in very shallow water. Never leave a tub, bucket or any container of water unattended. Keep bathroom doors closed and use toilet-lid locks.21 See Keeping Kids Safe.

📍 Where to keep it: heat, light, fumes and weight

Pick a cool, dark spot away from fuel and chemicals. The CDC says 10–21°C (50–70°F), out of direct sunlight, and away from toxic substances such as gasoline or pesticides.1

Fumes can pass through plastic. Nebraska Extension warns that vapors from gasoline, kerosene and pesticides can penetrate plastic containers.12 Do not keep water next to the fuel cans in the garage.

Light ruins containers and feeds algae. Be Ready Utah says sunlight, and even artificial light, makes containers brittle or leaky. Blue 208 L (55-gallon) barrels in direct sun can start to fall apart within 5 years.22 Sunlight also lets algae grow in stored water.22

Raise containers off bare concrete or dirt. Utah State University Extension says plastic absorbs odors there, which changes the taste.13 Be Ready Utah adds that air flow underneath limits moisture and mold.22

Good spots

  • An inside closet or under a bed, away from outside walls
  • A cool, dark ground floor, on a pallet122
  • Several places in the house, so one leak or break does not take it all

Bad spots

  • Next to gasoline, kerosene or pesticides12
  • In direct sun, such as a porch or window122
  • On bare concrete or dirt13
  • On a shelf that is not built for the weight12

Weight: choose the spot before you fill

Water weighs 1 kg per liter, or about 8.3 lb per gallon.7 So a full 208 L (55-gallon) drum weighs about 208 kg (458 lb). Once it is full, you will not move it.

Nebraska Extension says shelves must be sturdy and stable, because water weighs over 8 pounds per gallon.12 Put drums at ground level on a pallet, where a pump or siphon can reach them.

Do not dip cups or hands into stored water. CDC guidance on safe storage says hands, cups and dippers carry germs into open containers.23 Pump or siphon water from a drum into a smaller container that has a lid and a tap.23

Carry less, store closer

A full 19 L (5-gallon) jug weighs about 19 kg (42 lb).7 If someone in your home cannot lift that, keep small jugs with a tap where they sleep, and refill them from the big containers. See Caring for Elderly & Disabled People.

❄️ Freezing, heat and the seasons

Leave 5–8 cm (2–3 inches) of air at the top of any container that might freeze. Water expands as it freezes and can burst a full container.12

Use freezer-safe plastic, not glass, for water that may freeze.12 If your storage spot freezes in winter, leave the headspace or move the water into the heated part of the house.

Pipes freeze too. See Preparing Your Home for a Long Outage and Winter Storms & Extreme Cold.

Hot climates: store more, store lower

In the tropics, 10–21°C (50–70°F) may be impossible to reach. The CDC says to store more water in hot climates.1 Use the coolest, darkest room you have, away from sunny walls and tin roofs.

⏳ The 30-year view Long haul

Every stored container is temporary. Sun-exposed barrels can start to fall apart within 5 years.22 Plan to replace containers, not only the water in them.

For daily drinking water over years, use a safe-storage container. CDC guidance describes one with a small opening and a lid, plus a tap or narrow spout, so no hands or cups go inside.23 Open buckets and pots let hands, cups and dippers carry fecal germs into treated water.23

Build the refill loop

In year 1, stored water carries you until rain catchment, a well or a treated stream supply is working. Then stored water becomes your buffer for dry spells and broken pumps. See Water Filters & SODIS, Build a Biosand Filter and Pottery & Making Containers.

Keep sealed water for the baby

For powdered formula, the CDC says use bottled water until officials say tap water is safe. With no bottled water, boil water for 1 minute (3 minutes above 2,000 m or 6,500 ft) and let it cool.15

For babies under 2 months, premature babies or babies with weak immune systems, do not let the boiled water cool below 70°C (158°F) before you mix in the powder. That heat kills Cronobacter germs.15 Then cool the bottle to body temperature, and test a few drops on your wrist before feeding.15 See Feeding Babies.

Count the dogs

Ready.gov's pet kit includes a water bowl and several days' supply of water.24 Store dog water in the same food-grade containers and add it to your totals. See Dogs in a Long Emergency.

Pregnancy and periods

The CDC says to store extra water for pregnant women.1 Keep a small covered container in the bathroom for washing during periods. See Periods Without Stores.
